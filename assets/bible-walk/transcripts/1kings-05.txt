High in the mountains of Lebanon, the axes ring out. A giant cedar tree, older than anyone can remember, creaks, and leans, and falls with a crash that echoes through the valleys. Teams of men drag the great logs down to the sea. There they tie them together into rafts, and float them down the coast. Every one of those trees is going to Jerusalem. They are going to become part of the house of God.
In chapter four, we saw Solomon’s kingdom at its best. Judah and Israel were as many as the sand of the sea, and every man sat under his vine and under his fig tree. God gave Solomon wisdom and understanding exceeding much. He spoke three thousand proverbs, and people came from all over the world to hear him.
Now, in chapter five, Solomon begins the great work of his life. Listen for why he can build, when his father David could not. And listen for how God keeps his promise.
To the north of Israel, on the coast, was the city of Tyre. Its king was Hiram. When he heard that Solomon had become king, he sent his servants to greet him. 'for Hiram was ever a lover of David.' Hiram and David had been friends. Hiram had even sent cedar wood and builders to make David’s palace.
So Solomon sends a message back to Hiram. You know, he says, that my father David could not build a house for the name of the LORD his God. There were wars all around him, until the LORD put his enemies under his feet.
But now, things are different.
Solomon asks Hiram for help. Command your servants to cut cedar trees for me from Lebanon. My servants will work with yours, and I will pay your men whatever wages you ask. 'for thou knowest that there is not among us any that can skill to hew timber like unto the Sidonians.' The people of Tyre and Sidon were the best woodcutters and builders in the region. Solomon was wise enough to know what his own people could not do, and to ask for help.
When Hiram hears Solomon’s message, he is overjoyed. 'Blessed be the LORD this day, which hath given unto David a wise son over this great people.' Hiram was not an Israelite. But he could see God’s hand in Solomon’s wisdom.
Hiram agrees to everything. My servants will bring the cedar and fir trees down from Lebanon to the sea, he says. I will tie them into rafts and float them down the coast to wherever you choose. There my men will break them apart, and you can take them away. And in return, you will provide food for my household.
So Hiram gives Solomon all the cedar and fir he needs. And every year, Solomon sends Hiram a great amount of wheat and pure olive oil.
Then the writer adds a sentence that reminds us who was really behind all of this.
Building the temple was an enormous job. Solomon raised a workforce from all over Israel. The men worked in turns. One month they worked in Lebanon, cutting timber, and then they had two months at home with their families. Others carried heavy loads, and others cut stone in the hills. And there were many supervisors to oversee the work.
But keep this in mind too. Later in the story, after Solomon dies, the people will say that his demands were too heavy. Even great building projects can come at a cost to ordinary people.
'And the king commanded, and they brought great stones, costly stones, and hewed stones, to lay the foundation of the house.' Huge blocks of stone, carefully cut and shaped. A foundation strong enough to last. Solomon’s builders and Hiram’s builders worked side by side, preparing the timber and the stone. Everything was getting ready, for the house of the LORD.
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 4. 'But now the LORD my God hath given me rest on every side, so that there is neither adversary nor evil occurrent.' An adversary is an enemy. Evil occurrent means anything bad happening. David had been a man of war, and God had given him victory after victory. That was his part. Now, because of what God had done through David, Solomon could live in peace and build. One generation fought, so the next could build. And all of it was God’s gift of rest.
Verse 5. 'And, behold, I purpose to build an house unto the name of the LORD my God, as the LORD spake unto David my father, saying, Thy son, whom I will set upon thy throne in thy room, he shall build an house unto my name.' I purpose means I intend, I have decided. In thy room means in your place. Solomon was not building the temple to make a name for himself. He was building it because God had promised it. A house for God’s name. A place where people would come to meet with God.
Verse 12. 'And the LORD gave Solomon wisdom, as he promised him: and there was peace between Hiram and Solomon; and they two made a league together.' A league means an agreement, a treaty. As he promised him. God had promised Solomon wisdom at Gibeon, and he kept his word. And that wisdom showed itself in peace, even with a foreign king. God’s wisdom builds bridges between people, not walls.
Solomon wrote in a psalm, 'Except the LORD build the house, they labour in vain that build it'. And Paul wrote, 'For other foundation can no man lay than that is laid, which is Jesus Christ.'
Is there something good God has given you to build? A family, a friendship, a piece of work, or a habit of prayer? Ask him to be the builder. And think of someone who has helped you, as Hiram helped Solomon, and thank God for them.
Here is today’s question. Solomon said, the LORD my God hath given me rest on every side, so I purpose to build. What good thing could you build for God with the peace and the gifts he has given you?
Let us pray. Lord God, thank you that you keep every promise you make. Thank you for the rest you give, and for the people who help me along the way. Be the builder of my life, my home and my work. Let everything I build be for your name, and on the foundation of Jesus Christ. Amen.
