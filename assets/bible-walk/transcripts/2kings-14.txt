A little thistle grows on a mountainside. It is prickly and proud. One day it sends a message to the great cedar tree that towers above it. Give your daughter to my son in marriage. As if they were equals! And then a wild animal wanders by, and steps on the thistle, and crushes it flat. That is a story one king tells another. And it is a warning.
In chapter thirteen, the old prophet Elisha put his hands over the king’s hands on a bow, and said, the arrow of the LORD’s deliverance. But the king struck the ground only three times. Elisha died, and a dead man who touched his bones came back to life. And the LORD was gracious to Israel, because of his covenant with Abraham, Isaac and Jacob.
Now, in chapter fourteen, we see a king of Judah whose success goes to his head. Listen for the story of the thistle. And listen for a familiar name.
In Judah, Amaziah, the son of Joash, becomes king. 'And he did that which was right in the sight of the LORD, yet not like David his father'. He was a good king, but not wholehearted. And the high places were still not removed.
When Amaziah has his kingdom firmly in hand, he punishes the servants who had taken his father’s life. But he does something wise and good.
Then Amaziah wins a great victory over the Edomites, in the Valley of Salt, and captures one of their cities. It is a real success. But it goes to his head.
So Amaziah sends a message to Jehoash, the king of Israel. 'Come, let us look one another in the face.' In other words, come out and fight me. He wanted to prove he was the stronger king.
And Jehoash of Israel sends back the story of the thistle. The thistle in Lebanon sent to the cedar in Lebanon, saying, give your daughter to my son as his wife. And a wild beast passed by, and trampled the thistle.
And then Jehoash explains the story.
'But Amaziah would not hear.' So the two kings meet in battle at Beth-shemesh, in Judah. And Judah is badly beaten. Its soldiers run home, every man to his tent.
Jehoash captures Amaziah himself, and marches on to Jerusalem. He breaks down a long stretch of the city wall. He takes all the gold and silver and everything valuable in the house of the LORD and in the palace, and takes hostages, and goes back to Samaria.
All of that, because Amaziah could not stay at home and be content with what God had given him. Pride cost him, and his people, dearly.
Amaziah lives on for many years. But at the end, there is a plot against him in Jerusalem. He flees to the city of Lachish, but they follow him there, and his life is ended. They bring him back on horses and bury him in Jerusalem. And the people of Judah make his young son Azariah king.
Then the story turns north again. In Israel, a new king named Jeroboam comes to the throne, the son of Joash. Not the first Jeroboam, who made the golden calves, but another king with the same name. He reigns for many years. He too does evil, and holds on to the sins of the first Jeroboam.
And yet, something good happens in his days.
Jonah! The very same Jonah who ran away from God, was swallowed by a great fish, and preached to Nineveh. We walked through his book earlier. Here he is in second Kings, bringing God’s word of hope to Israel. Before Nineveh, Jonah was a prophet at home, promising that God would help his people win back their land.
Why did God help Israel, when its king did evil? The writer tells us. 'For the LORD saw the affliction of Israel, that it was very bitter: for there was not any shut up, nor any left, nor any helper for Israel.' No one else would help them. But God did.
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 6. 'But the children of the murderers he slew not: according unto that which is written in the book of the law of Moses, wherein the LORD commanded, saying, The fathers shall not be put to death for the children, nor the children be put to death for the fathers; but every man shall be put to death for his own sin.' In those days, kings often punished whole families for what one person had done. But Amaziah obeyed God’s law, which said each person is responsible for their own wrongdoing. Children should not be punished for their parents’ sins. God’s justice is fair, and he cares about the innocent.
Verse 10. 'Thou hast indeed smitten Edom, and thine heart hath lifted thee up: glory of this, and tarry at home: for why shouldest thou meddle to thy hurt, that thou shouldest fall, even thou, and Judah with thee?' Thine heart hath lifted thee up means your success has made you proud. Tarry at home means stay at home. Enjoy what you have won, and do not go looking for trouble. One victory had made Amaziah think he could win every battle. Success can be more dangerous than failure, if it makes us proud.
Verse 27. 'And the LORD said not that he would blot out the name of Israel from under heaven: but he saved them by the hand of Jeroboam the son of Joash.' God was not finished with Israel yet. Even though their king did evil, God used him to save them. God can use anyone to do good, even people who do not deserve it. And he does it because he cares about his people, and sees their suffering.
Solomon wrote, 'Pride goeth before destruction, and an haughty spirit before a fall.' And Ezekiel heard God say, 'The son shall not bear the iniquity of the father'.
Has a success recently made you a little proud? Thank God for it, and give him the glory. And is there a battle you are looking for that you do not need to fight? Perhaps God is saying to you, tarry at home.
Here is today’s question. Amaziah’s victory over Edom made his heart proud, and it led to his defeat. How can you enjoy God’s blessings without letting them make you proud?
Let us pray. Lord God, thank you for every success you give me. Keep my heart from being lifted up with pride. Help me to be content, and to stay away from battles I do not need to fight. Thank you that you see the suffering of your people, and that you help when no one else will. Amen.
