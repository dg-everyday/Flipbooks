A little boy, five years old, is being carried by his nurse, running as fast as she can. Terrible news has come: the king and the prince are dead. The enemy may be coming. In her haste, she stumbles, and the little boy falls. And from that day on, he can never walk properly again. He is the grandson of a king, and the son of David’s dearest friend. And one day, he will be shown a kindness he never expected.
In chapter three, there was a long war between the house of Saul and the house of David, but David waxed stronger and stronger. Abner turned to David, and was sent away in peace. But Joab struck him down, to avenge his brother. David mourned for Abner, and said, know ye not that there is a prince and a great man fallen this day in Israel?
Now, in chapter four, the house of Saul is collapsing. Listen for what two men think will please David. And listen for the little boy who is mentioned in the middle of the chapter.
When Ish-bosheth, Saul’s son, hears that Abner has died in Hebron, he loses heart.
Ish-bosheth has two men who are captains of raiding bands, Baanah and Rechab, brothers from the tribe of Benjamin.
And then, suddenly, the writer stops and tells us about someone else.
Mephibosheth. Remember that name. Jonathan’s son. A grandson of Saul. Lame in both feet. He seems of no importance at all in this chapter. But David has promised Jonathan that he will show kindness to his family for ever. Mephibosheth’s story is not over.
Back to Rechab and Baanah. They go to the house of Ish-bosheth in the heat of the day, while he is lying on his bed, resting at midday. They come into the house as if to fetch wheat. And they strike him down where he lies. Then they escape, and travel all through the dark hours to Hebron.
They come to David, bringing proof of what they have done. They say, here is the proof that Ish-bosheth, the son of Saul your enemy, who tried to take your life, is dead. Today the LORD has avenged my lord the king on Saul and his family.
They expect a reward. They think David will be delighted. They even use God’s name, as if God had sent them.
But David answers them.
David reminds them of the Amalekite in Ziklag, who thought he was bringing good news when he told David that Saul was dead, and expected a reward. Instead, he was punished for claiming to have harmed the LORD’s anointed.
And David says, how much more, when wicked men have struck down an innocent man in his own house, on his own bed? Should I not hold you responsible for his blood?
David orders that Rechab and Baanah be punished for their crime, and they are put to death. But Ish-bosheth is given an honourable burial, in the tomb of Abner at Hebron.
Again and again, people have thought they could win David’s favour by harming his enemies. The Amalekite. Joab, in a way. And now Rechab and Baanah. And again and again, David has refused to accept power gained by violence.
David had learned something deep during his years of running from Saul. He did not need to grab the kingdom, or have others grab it for him. God had promised it to him. And God, who had rescued him from every trouble, would give it to him in his own way and his own time.
With Ish-bosheth gone, there is no one left from Saul’s house to lead Israel, except a little lame boy named Mephibosheth. The way is open for David to become king over all Israel. And he has not had to lift a hand against the house of Saul to get there.
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 1. 'And when Saul’s son heard that Abner was dead in Hebron, his hands were feeble, and all the Israelites were troubled.' His hands were feeble. Ish-bosheth had depended on Abner for everything. When Abner was gone, he had nothing left. Ish-bosheth never seems to have depended on God. When our strength comes from people, we are lost when they are gone. But when our strength comes from God, we can stand, whatever happens.
Verse 4. 'And Jonathan, Saul’s son, had a son that was lame of his feet. He was five years old when the tidings came of Saul and Jonathan out of Jezreel, and his nurse took him up, and fled: and it came to pass, as she made haste to flee, that he fell, and became lame. And his name was Mephibosheth.' A forgotten little boy, crippled by a fall, hidden away. In the world of kings and armies, he seemed to count for nothing. But God does not forget the broken and the overlooked. In a few chapters, we will see how David remembers him, for Jonathan’s sake.
Verse 9. 'And David answered Rechab and Baanah his brother, the sons of Rimmon the Beerothite, and said unto them, As the LORD liveth, who hath redeemed my soul out of all adversity,' Who hath redeemed my soul out of all adversity. David looked back over all his years of danger, and saw that it was God who had rescued him, every time. He did not need wicked men to help him. God had always been enough. And God would give him the kingdom without any help from violence.
A psalm of David says, 'The LORD redeemeth the soul of his servants: and none of them that trust in him shall be desolate.' And a proverb says, 'Better is a little with righteousness than great revenues without right.'
Is there something you want, which you are tempted to get by wrong means? Look back over your life, and see how God has rescued you before. Trust him to give you what is right, in his way and his time.
Here is today’s question. Mephibosheth seemed unimportant, but God had not forgotten him. Who in your community is easily forgotten, and how could you show them God’s care?
Let us pray. Lord God, you have redeemed my soul out of all adversity. Thank you for every time you have rescued me. Keep me from trying to get what I want by wrong means. Thank you that you do not forget the broken and the overlooked. Help me to remember them too. Amen.
