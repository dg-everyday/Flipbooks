An old king sits at the city gate, waiting. Out there, beyond the hills, a battle has been fought. A watchman on the wall shouts that a runner is coming. Then another. The king stands up. He has only one question. Not, have we won? Not, is my kingdom safe? Just one question. Is my son all right?
In chapter seventeen, Ahithophel advised Absalom to strike David at once, but Hushai persuaded him to wait and gather all Israel. For the LORD had appointed to defeat the good counsel of Ahithophel. David crossed the Jordan safely, and in the wilderness, friends brought food, saying, the people is hungry, and weary, and thirsty.
Now, in chapter eighteen, the battle between David and his son is fought. Listen for what David asks of his commanders. And listen for his cry at the end of the chapter.
David counts the people with him, and sets commanders over them. He sends out the army in three groups, under Joab, Abishai and Ittai. And the king says, I will go out with you myself.
But the people say, you must not go. If we run away, they will not care about us. Even if half of us die, they will not care. But you are worth ten thousand of us. It is better for you to help us from the city.
The king says, I will do whatever you think best. So the king stands beside the gate, while all the army marches out by hundreds and by thousands.
And as they go, the king gives one last command, loud enough for everyone to hear.
Absalom had stolen his kingdom, shamed him before all Israel, and was trying to take his life. And David still loved him.
The army goes out into the field against Israel, and the battle is fought in the forest of Ephraim. David’s men win a great victory. The battle spreads out over the whole countryside, and the forest takes more lives that day than the sword.
Then Absalom meets some of David’s men. He is riding a mule, and the mule goes under the thick branches of a great oak tree. And Absalom’s head gets caught in the branches. He is left hanging between heaven and earth, while the mule runs on from under him.
One of David’s men sees it, and tells Joab. I saw Absalom hanging in an oak.
Joab says, you saw him? Why did you not strike him down? I would have given you ten pieces of silver and a belt.
But the man says, even if you gave me a thousand pieces of silver, I would not lift my hand against the king’s son. We all heard the king command you and Abishai and Ittai, protect the young man Absalom for my sake. And if I had done it, nothing is hidden from the king, and you yourself would have stood back and let me take the blame.
Joab says, I will not waste time with you. And Joab goes to the tree, and with his own hands, and the help of his armour bearers, he ends Absalom’s life.
Joab disobeyed the king. Perhaps he thought it was the only way to end the rebellion. But he did not deal gently, as David had asked.
Then Joab blows the trumpet, and the army stops chasing Israel. They take Absalom, and throw him into a great pit in the forest, and pile a great heap of stones over him.
The writer adds a sad detail. In his lifetime, Absalom had set up a stone pillar in the king’s valley, as a monument to himself, because he said, I have no son to carry on my name. He named it after himself, and it is called Absalom’s monument. A proud monument in the valley, and a heap of stones in a forest pit.
Two runners set out with the news, Ahimaaz the son of Zadok, and a Cushite. Ahimaaz takes a shorter way, and gets there first.
David is sitting between the gates. The watchman on the roof sees a man running alone. If he is alone, the king says, he has news. Then the watchman sees another runner. The first one runs like Ahimaaz, he says. And the king says, he is a good man, and comes with good news.
Ahimaaz calls out, all is well! He bows before the king, and says, blessed be the LORD your God, who has delivered up the men who rebelled against my lord the king.
And the king asks, is the young man Absalom safe?
Ahimaaz cannot bring himself to say it. I saw a great commotion, he says, but I did not know what it was. The king says, stand aside.
Then the Cushite arrives. Good news, my lord the king! The LORD has delivered you today from all who rose up against you.
And the king asks the same question.
And the king is overcome.
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 5. 'And the king commanded Joab and Abishai and Ittai, saying, Deal gently for my sake with the young man, even with Absalom. And all the people heard when the king gave all the captains charge concerning Absalom.' Deal gently for my sake. Absalom was a rebel, a traitor, and a danger to everyone. But to David, he was still his son. A father’s love does not stop, even when a child goes terribly wrong. It is a faint picture of God’s love for those who rebel against him.
Verse 32. 'And the king said unto Cushi, Is the young man Absalom safe? And Cushi answered, The enemies of my lord the king, and all that rise against thee to do thee hurt, be as that young man is.' The Cushite delivered the news as gently as he could. But there was no gentle way to tell a father that his son was dead. The king had won his kingdom back. But it did not matter. He had lost his son.
Verse 33. 'And the king was much moved, and went up to the chamber over the gate, and wept: and as he went, thus he said, O my son Absalom, my son, my son Absalom! would God I had died for thee, O Absalom, my son, my son!' Would God I had died for thee. David wished he could have died in his son’s place. But he could not. Long after, another Son of David would do exactly that. Jesus died in the place of rebels, so that they could come home to the Father.
Paul wrote, 'But God commendeth his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us.' And Jesus told of a father whose son was lost, and said, 'this my son was dead, and is alive again; he was lost, and is found.'
Is there someone you love who has gone far from God, or from you? Pray for them by name today. Ask God to bring them home. And thank God that Jesus did what David could not do: he died in the place of rebels.
Here is today’s question. David said, would God I had died for thee. How does David’s grief help you understand God’s love for people who have turned away from him?
Let us pray. Lord God, thank you for a love that does not stop, even when we rebel. Thank you that Jesus did what David could not do, and died in my place. I pray today for those I love who are far from you. Deal gently with them, and bring them home. Amen.
