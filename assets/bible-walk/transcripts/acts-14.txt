The crowd in the city of Lystra has gone wild with excitement. They are shouting in their own language, the gods have come down to us in the likeness of men! The priest of Zeus is bringing oxen and garlands of flowers to the city gates, ready to offer sacrifices to two travellers. And those two travellers, Paul and Barnabas, are horrified.
In chapter thirteen, the Holy Ghost sent Barnabas and Saul out from Antioch. Saul, now called Paul, preached in the synagogue at Antioch in Pisidia that through Jesus is preached the forgiveness of sins. Many Gentiles believed, but Paul and Barnabas were driven out of the city, and went on to Iconium, filled with joy and with the Holy Ghost.
Chapter fourteen finishes their first great journey. It has high points and low points, a crowd that wants to worship them and a crowd that wants to kill them. Watch how Paul and Barnabas handle both.
At Iconium, Paul and Barnabas go into the synagogue, and speak so well that a great crowd of both Jews and Greeks believe. But those who refuse to believe stir up the Gentiles and poison their minds against the believers. Paul and Barnabas stay a long time anyway, speaking boldly in the Lord, who confirms the word of his grace with signs and wonders. The city is divided; some side with the Jewish leaders, and some with the apostles. When they learn of a plot to stone them, they flee to Lystra and Derbe, and preach the gospel there.
At Lystra sits a man who cannot use his feet. He has been lame from his mother’s womb, and has never walked. He listens to Paul speaking. Paul looks closely at him, and sees that he has faith to be healed. So Paul says with a loud voice, 'Stand upright on thy feet.' And he leaps up and walks.
When the people see what Paul has done, they shout that the gods have come down. They call Barnabas Jupiter, the chief of their gods, and Paul Mercurius, the messenger of the gods, because he is the main speaker. The priest of Jupiter brings oxen and garlands to the gates, wanting to offer sacrifice with the people.
When Paul and Barnabas hear of it, they tear their clothes and run in among the crowd. Sirs, why are you doing these things? 'We also are men of like passions with you'. We are human, just like you. We have come to tell you to turn from these empty things to the living God, who made heaven and earth and the sea, and everything in them. In the past he let all nations walk in their own ways. Even with these words, they can hardly stop the crowd from sacrificing to them.
Then the mood changes. Jews come from Antioch and Iconium, and win the crowd over. They stone Paul, and drag him out of the city, thinking he is dead. But as the disciples stand around him, he rises up, and goes back into the city. The next day he leaves with Barnabas for Derbe.
At Derbe they preach the gospel and teach many people. And then they do something remarkable. Instead of taking the short road home, they turn around and go back through the very cities where they were attacked: Lystra, Iconium, and Antioch. They strengthen the disciples there. In every church they appoint elders, and pray with fasting, and commend them to the Lord in whom they believed.
Finally they sail back to Antioch in Syria, where they had first been sent out by the grace of God for the work they have now completed. And they stay there a long time with the disciples.
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 17. 'Nevertheless he left not himself without witness, in that he did good, and gave us rain from heaven, and fruitful seasons, filling our hearts with food and gladness.' The people of Lystra did not know the Scriptures. So Paul starts with what they did know: rain, harvests, food and gladness. Every good gift in creation is a witness pointing to the God who gives it. When you enjoy a good meal or a gentle rain, remember who sent it, and thank him.
Verse 22. 'Confirming the souls of the disciples, and exhorting them to continue in the faith, and that we must through much tribulation enter into the kingdom of God.' Tribulation means trouble and hardship. Paul did not promise the new believers an easy life. He had just been stoned and left for dead. He told them the truth: the road to God’s kingdom goes through hard places. But he also strengthened them, and encouraged them to keep going. Hardship does not mean God has left us. It is part of the journey home.
Verse 27. 'And when they were come, and had gathered the church together, they rehearsed all that God had done with them, and how he had opened the door of faith unto the Gentiles.' To rehearse means to tell, to recount. Notice whom Paul and Barnabas give the credit to: all that God had done with them. They had preached, travelled, suffered and been stoned. But it was God who opened the door. And it was a door of faith, open to anyone from any nation who would believe.
A psalm says, 'The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork.' Paul told the people of Lystra the same thing: God has never left himself without witness. Creation speaks of him everywhere, and the good news of Jesus tells us his name.
Look around you today for the witnesses God has left: food, rain, sunshine, friends and gladness. Thank him for them. And if you are going through hardship, remember Paul’s words. It is not a sign that God has forgotten you. Keep going, and continue in the faith.
Here is today’s question. Paul said, we must through much tribulation enter into the kingdom of God. What hardship are you facing, and how can you keep trusting God through it today?
Let us pray. Lord God, you have never left yourself without witness. Thank you for rain and food and gladness, and for the door of faith you opened to all nations. When troubles come, strengthen my soul, and help me to continue in the faith. Amen.
