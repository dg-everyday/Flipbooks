An old prophet named Agabus walks into the room, takes Paul’s belt, and ties up his own hands and feet with it. Then he says, this is what the Holy Ghost says: the Jewish leaders at Jerusalem will bind the man who owns this belt, and hand him over to the Gentiles. Everyone in the room turns to Paul, and they begin to beg him with tears: please, do not go up to Jerusalem.
In chapter twenty, Paul raised Eutychus, who fell from a window, and said a tearful goodbye to the elders of Ephesus on the beach at Miletus. He told them he was going to Jerusalem, bound in the spirit, knowing that chains and afflictions waited for him. He wanted only to finish his course with joy.
Now, in chapter twenty-one, Paul arrives in Jerusalem. The warnings come true. This chapter begins the last part of the book of Acts: Paul’s arrest, his trials, and his long journey to Rome.
After tearing themselves away from the Ephesian elders, Paul and his companions set sail, past the islands, and land at Tyre. There they find disciples, and stay seven days. These believers tell Paul through the Spirit not to go up to Jerusalem. When the time comes to leave, all of them, with their wives and children, walk with the travellers out of the city. They kneel down on the shore and pray, and say goodbye.
At Caesarea, they stay in the house of Philip the evangelist, one of the seven chosen to care for widows, the one who told the Ethiopian official about Jesus. Philip has four unmarried daughters who prophesy. While they are there, the prophet Agabus comes down from Judaea, and acts out his warning with Paul’s belt.
When the believers, including Luke, hear it, they beg Paul not to go. And Paul answers them. When he will not be persuaded, they stop pleading.
So they go up to Jerusalem, and the believers welcome them gladly. The next day Paul goes to see James, the brother of Jesus, and all the elders are there. Paul greets them, and tells them one by one the things God has done among the Gentiles through his ministry.
When they hear it, they glorify the Lord. Then they tell Paul, you see, brother, how many thousands of Jews there are who believe, and they are all zealous for the law. They have been told that you teach the Jews who live among the Gentiles to abandon Moses, and not to circumcise their children or follow the customs. That is not true, but it is what they have heard. So the elders suggest a way to show that Paul still honours the law. Four men among them have taken a vow. Join them, they say, purify yourself with them, and pay their expenses. Then everyone will know that the rumours are false. As for the Gentile believers, they remind him, we have already written our decision.
Paul agrees, and goes into the temple with the men. But when the seven days are almost over, some Jews from the province of Asia see him in the temple. They stir up the whole crowd, and seize him, shouting, men of Israel, help! This is the man who teaches everyone everywhere against our people, our law, and this place. And he has brought Greeks into the temple, and polluted this holy place. They had earlier seen Trophimus, a Gentile from Ephesus, with Paul in the city, and assumed Paul had brought him into the temple courts, where Gentiles were forbidden.
The whole city is in uproar. The people run together, drag Paul out of the temple, and the doors are shut. As they are trying to kill him, news reaches the commander of the Roman soldiers that all Jerusalem is in confusion. He immediately takes soldiers and centurions, and runs down to the crowd. When the people see the soldiers, they stop beating Paul.
The commander arrests Paul, orders him bound with two chains, and asks who he is and what he has done. Some in the crowd shout one thing, some another. Since he cannot learn the truth because of the uproar, he orders Paul to be carried into the fortress. The violence of the crowd is so great that the soldiers have to carry Paul up the steps, while the people follow, crying, 'Away with him.'
As Paul is about to be led in, he speaks to the commander in Greek. The commander is surprised. Are you not the Egyptian who led a rebellion and four thousand murderers out into the wilderness? Paul says, 'I am a man which am a Jew of Tarsus, a city in Cilicia, a citizen of no mean city: and, I beseech thee, suffer me to speak unto the people.' The commander gives him permission. Paul stands on the stairs, and motions to the people with his hand. A great silence falls. And Paul begins to speak to them in the Hebrew tongue.
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 13. 'Then Paul answered, What mean ye to weep and to break mine heart? for I am ready not to be bound only, but also to die at Jerusalem for the name of the Lord Jesus.' Paul’s friends loved him, and their tears were breaking his heart. But he was not going to Jerusalem by accident or out of stubbornness. He believed God was leading him there. He was ready for chains, and even for death, for the name of Jesus. Sometimes following Jesus means going where others beg us not to go.
Verse 14. 'And when he would not be persuaded, we ceased, saying, The will of the Lord be done.' Notice Luke’s honesty: we, the believers, including himself, were begging Paul not to go. But when they saw he was sure of God’s leading, they stopped arguing and prayed: the will of the Lord be done. These are almost the same words Jesus prayed in the garden, not my will, but thine, be done. When we cannot see the way ahead, we can still trust the One who does.
Verse 19. 'And when he had saluted them, he declared particularly what things God had wrought among the Gentiles by his ministry.' Saluted means greeted. Declared particularly means told in detail, one thing after another. Wrought means worked, did. Notice again where Paul puts the credit: what God had wrought. Paul had travelled thousands of miles, and suffered beatings, stonings and shipwreck. But the story he told was about what God had done.
Jesus had told Peter, near the end of John’s Gospel, that another would bind him, and carry him where he did not want to go. And Jesus himself 'stedfastly set his face to go to Jerusalem', knowing what waited for him there. Paul followed in his Master’s footsteps.
Is there something you are facing where you cannot see how it will turn out? Pray the prayer of Paul’s friends: the will of the Lord be done. And when you tell your story, tell it the way Paul did, as the story of what God has done.
Here is today’s question. Paul’s friends prayed, the will of the Lord be done. What situation in your life do you need to place in God’s hands with that prayer today?
Let us pray. Lord, your will be done. When I do not understand the road ahead, help me to trust you. Give me Paul’s courage to follow wherever you lead, and help me always to tell what you have done, and give you the glory. Amen.
