An old man walks into his house, climbs to his upstairs room, and opens the windows that face toward Jerusalem, far away. He kneels down, as he has done three times a day for most of his life, and prays. He knows that a new law has just been signed that makes this a crime. He knows that men are watching his window. And he knows the punishment: a den of hungry lions.
In Jeremiah chapter thirty-one, God promised his people an everlasting love and a new covenant. Meanwhile they were living in exile in Babylon. Daniel had been carried there as a young man, before Jerusalem fell. From the beginning he refused to give up his faith. He and his friends would not eat the king’s food offered to idols. God gave Daniel wisdom to explain the king’s dreams, and he rose to high office. His three friends were thrown into a fiery furnace for refusing to bow to a golden image, and God brought them out without even the smell of smoke.
Years passed. Babylon itself fell, conquered suddenly by the Medes and the Persians, and a new king, Darius, came to the throne. Daniel is now an old man. In The Big Picture, he shows how God’s people can stay faithful in a land that is not their own.
King Darius sets a hundred and twenty princes over his kingdom, and three presidents over them. Daniel is one of the three, and he stands out above them all, 'because an excellent spirit was in him'. The king plans to set him over the whole realm.
The other officials are jealous. They look for some fault in Daniel’s work, but they cannot find any, because he is faithful. So they say, 'We shall not find any occasion against this Daniel, except we find it against him concerning the law of his God.'
They go to the king with a plan. Let the king make a law that for thirty days, anyone who asks anything of any god or man, except the king, will be thrown into the den of lions. And let it be signed as a law of the Medes and Persians, which cannot be changed. Darius, flattered, signs it.
Daniel knows it has been signed. And he goes home and prays, just as he always has. The men gather and find him praying and making requests to his God. They hurry to the king. Did you not sign a law? Daniel does not respect you or your law; he prays three times a day.
Now the king understands what he has done. He is deeply upset, and he works hard all day to find a way to save Daniel. But the officials remind him that a law of the Medes and Persians cannot be changed.
So Daniel is brought and thrown into the den of lions. And the king says to him, 'Thy God whom thou servest continually, he will deliver thee.' A stone is rolled over the mouth of the den, and the king seals it with his own ring, so that nothing can be changed.
The king goes back to his palace. He will not eat, he sends away the musicians, and he cannot sleep. At first light he hurries to the den and calls out in a sorrowful voice, 'O Daniel, servant of the living God, is thy God, whom thou servest continually, able to deliver thee from the lions?'
And from inside the den comes Daniel’s voice: 'O king, live for ever.'
The king is overjoyed. Daniel is lifted out of the den, and no wound of any kind is found on him, 'because he believed in his God.' The men who accused him are thrown to the lions themselves. And King Darius writes to every nation and language in his kingdom.
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 10. 'Now when Daniel knew that the writing was signed, he went into his house; and his windows being open in his chamber toward Jerusalem, he kneeled upon his knees three times a day, and prayed, and gave thanks before his God, as he did aforetime.' Notice the last words: as he did aforetime. Daniel did not suddenly become brave. He simply kept doing what he had always done. His habit of prayer was built long before the crisis came. And notice that, facing the lions, he gave thanks. Faithfulness in big moments grows from faithfulness in ordinary days.
Verse 22. 'My God hath sent his angel, and hath shut the lions mouths, that they have not hurt me: forasmuch as before him innocency was found in me; and also before thee, O king, have I done no hurt.' God did not keep Daniel out of the den. He kept him safe inside it. Sometimes God rescues us from trouble, and sometimes he rescues us in it. Either way, he is with us. Daniel could also say with a clear conscience that he had done no wrong. He had served the king faithfully, and God faithfully.
Verse 26. 'I make a decree, That in every dominion of my kingdom men tremble and fear before the God of Daniel: for he is the living God, and stedfast for ever, and his kingdom that which shall not be destroyed, and his dominion shall be even unto the end.' A foreign king, who did not know God, ends up announcing to the whole empire that Daniel’s God is the living God, and that his kingdom will never end. One old man’s quiet faithfulness made God known to the nations, just as God had promised Abraham long before.
The New Testament remembers people of faith who 'stopped the mouths of lions'. And there is a picture here worth noticing. A stone was rolled over the den and sealed by the ruler, and everyone expected death. But at first light the servant of God came out alive. One day a stone would be rolled over another grave, and sealed, and on the third day it would be empty.
What are your daily habits with God? Build them now, in ordinary days, so that they hold you when trouble comes. And if you are in a den right now, remember: God can shut the lions’ mouths, and he is with you there.
Here is today’s question. Daniel prayed three times a day, as he did aforetime. What daily habit of prayer will you keep, or begin, today?
Let us pray. Living God, you are steadfast for ever, and your kingdom will never be destroyed. Make me faithful like Daniel, in ordinary days and in hard ones. When I face lions, shut their mouths, and let my life make you known. Amen.
