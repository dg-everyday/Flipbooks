Three crosses stand on a hill outside Jerusalem. On the middle one hangs a man whom crowds once followed, who healed the sick and raised the dead. Above his head a sign says THE KING OF THE JEWS. People passing by shake their heads and laugh. And in the middle of the day, darkness covers the whole land.
In John chapter eleven, Jesus raised Lazarus from the dead, and the leaders decided that he must die. A few days later, Jesus rode into Jerusalem on a young donkey, just as the prophet Zechariah had said the King would come, and the crowds shouted Hosanna. He drove the buyers and sellers out of the temple.
Then, at the Passover, he ate a last meal with his twelve disciples. He took bread and said, this is my body. He took the cup and said, this is my blood of the new testament, which is shed for many: the new covenant Jeremiah had promised. In the garden of Gethsemane he prayed, not what I will, but what thou wilt. Judas betrayed him with a kiss, the disciples ran away, and the high priest’s council condemned him to death.
Now, in Mark chapter fifteen, the story reaches the place every thread in the Bible has been leading to. In The Big Picture, this is the cross.
At first light, the chief priests bind Jesus and hand him over to Pilate, the Roman governor. Pilate asks him, 'Art thou the King of the Jews?' Jesus answers, 'Thou sayest it.' The priests accuse him of many things, but Jesus says nothing more, and Pilate marvels. Like the lamb in Isaiah, he opened not his mouth.
At the feast, Pilate would release one prisoner to the people. There is a man named Barabbas in prison, a rebel who had committed murder. Pilate knows the priests have handed Jesus over out of envy, so he offers to release the King of the Jews. But the priests stir up the crowd to ask for Barabbas. Pilate asks, then what shall I do with Jesus? And they cry out, 'Crucify him.' 'Why, what evil hath he done?' But they shout all the more.
The soldiers take Jesus inside, dress him in purple, twist a crown of thorns onto his head, and mock him: 'Hail, King of the Jews!' They strike him and spit on him. Then they lead him out to crucify him. A man from Cyrene named Simon, coming in from the country, is forced to carry his cross.
They bring him to Golgotha, the place of a skull, and crucify him. They divide his clothes by casting lots. It is the third hour. Two thieves are crucified with him, one on his right and one on his left. Mark says, 'And the scripture was fulfilled, which saith, And he was numbered with the transgressors.' Those are Isaiah’s words.
The crowds mock him. The chief priests say, 'He saved others; himself he cannot save.' Come down from the cross, they say, and we will believe.
At the sixth hour darkness covers the whole land until the ninth hour. Then Jesus cries out with a loud voice. Some think he is calling Elijah. Then Jesus cries with a loud voice again, and gives up the ghost.
At that moment the veil of the temple is torn in two. And the Roman centurion standing in front of the cross, who saw how he died, says, 'Truly this man was the Son of God.'
Women who had followed Jesus from Galilee watch from far off. Then Joseph of Arimathaea, a member of the council who was waiting for the kingdom of God, goes boldly to Pilate and asks for the body. He wraps Jesus in fine linen, lays him in a tomb cut out of the rock, and rolls a stone against the door.
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 15. 'And so Pilate, willing to content the people, released Barabbas unto them, and delivered Jesus, when he had scourged him, to be crucified.' The guilty man walks free, and the innocent man goes to die in his place. Barabbas did nothing to earn his release. He simply received it, because Jesus took the cross that should have been his. That is the gospel in one scene. Every one of us is Barabbas.
Verse 34. 'And at the ninth hour Jesus cried with a loud voice, saying, Eloi, Eloi, lama sabachthani? which is, being interpreted, My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?' These are the first words of Psalm twenty-two, a psalm of David. On the cross Jesus was carrying the sin of the world, and he experienced being cut off from his Father, the separation that sin brings. He was forsaken so that we never have to be. And the psalm he quoted does not end in despair. It ends with all the ends of the earth turning to the LORD.
Verse 38. 'And the veil of the temple was rent in twain from the top to the bottom.' Ever since Eden, the way into God’s presence had been closed. In the tabernacle and the temple, a thick veil hid the most holy place, and only the high priest could go in, once a year. When Jesus died, God tore that veil from top to bottom. The way back to God, closed in the garden, is open.
Every thread of The Big Picture meets at the cross. In Genesis chapter three, God promised a son of the woman who would crush the serpent’s head, though wounded himself. In Genesis chapter twenty-two, Abraham said, 'God will provide himself a lamb'. In Exodus, the blood of the Passover lamb saved God’s people from death. And Isaiah saw a servant wounded for our transgressions. On this day, the Lamb of God was provided.
Stand at the cross today, like the centurion, and look at what Jesus did. He took your place. Then come near to God, through the torn veil, with confidence. The way is open.
Here is today’s question. Barabbas went free because Jesus took his place. What will you say to God today, knowing that Jesus took yours?
Let us pray. Lord Jesus, you were forsaken so that I could be brought near. You took my place, as you took Barabbas’s. Thank you that the veil is torn and the way to God is open. Truly, you are the Son of God. Amen.
