A servant is carrying wine to the most powerful man in the world, the king of Persia. In the king’s presence, servants must always look happy. A sad face could cost you your job, or your life. But today this servant cannot hide his grief. The king looks up and asks him why. And in the split second before he answers, the servant does something nobody else in the room can see. He prays.
In Daniel chapter six, God shut the lions’ mouths, and Daniel stayed faithful in exile. Just as Jeremiah had promised, after seventy years God brought his people back. Persia had conquered Babylon, and the Persian king Cyrus allowed the Jews to return home. A first group went back and, after many struggles, rebuilt the temple.
But Jerusalem itself was still a ruin. Its walls were broken down and its gates burned. A city without walls in those days was defenceless and disgraced.
Far away in the Persian palace at Shushan lived a Jew named Nehemiah. He was the cupbearer to King Artaxerxes, a trusted servant who tasted the king’s wine. In chapter one, Nehemiah heard how bad things were in Jerusalem, and he sat down and wept, and fasted, and prayed for days. In The Big Picture, this is God’s people coming home.
Some months later, Nehemiah brings the king his wine. He has never looked sad in the king’s presence before. The king notices. 'Why is thy countenance sad, seeing thou art not sick? this is nothing else but sorrow of heart.' Nehemiah is terrified.
But he answers honestly. 'Let the king live for ever: why should not my countenance be sad, when the city, the place of my fathers’ sepulchres, lieth waste, and the gates thereof are consumed with fire?' The city where my ancestors are buried lies in ruins.
The king asks him, 'For what dost thou make request?' And Nehemiah prays, then answers.
Send me to Judah, he says, to the city of my fathers, so that I may rebuild it. The king, with the queen sitting beside him, asks how long he will be gone, and when he will return. And it pleases the king to send him. Nehemiah asks for letters to the governors beyond the river, so they will let him pass, and a letter to the keeper of the king’s forest, to give him timber for the gates and the wall. And the king grants it all, 'according to the good hand of my God upon me.'
Nehemiah travels to Jerusalem with officers and horsemen from the king. But not everyone is pleased. Two local officials, Sanballat and Tobiah, are deeply troubled that someone has come to help the people of Israel.
For three days Nehemiah tells no one his plan. Then he goes out in the dark with a few men, riding his animal, to inspect the walls himself. He rides around the broken walls and the burned gates. In places the rubble is so thick that his animal cannot even get through.
Then he gathers the leaders and the people. 'Ye see the distress that we are in, how Jerusalem lieth waste, and the gates thereof are burned with fire: come, and let us build up the wall of Jerusalem, that we be no more a reproach.' He tells them how God’s good hand has been on him, and what the king has said. And the people answer with one voice.
But Sanballat, Tobiah and another man named Geshem laugh at them and scorn them. What are you doing? Are you rebelling against the king? And Nehemiah answers them.
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 4. 'Then the king said unto me, For what dost thou make request? So I prayed to the God of heaven.' There was no time to kneel, close his eyes, or find a quiet room. The king was waiting for an answer. So Nehemiah prayed a silent prayer, right there, in a moment. He had prayed for days in chapter one, so this quick prayer came from a heart already full of prayer. You can talk to God anywhere, any time, in the middle of a conversation, and he hears.
Verse 18. 'Then I told them of the hand of my God which was good upon me; as also the king’s words that he had spoken unto me. And they said, Let us rise up and build. So they strengthened their hands for this good work.' Nehemiah did not just give orders. He told the people what God had done. When they heard of God’s good hand, their courage rose. Telling one another what God has done strengthens everyone’s hands for the work.
Verse 20. 'Then answered I them, and said unto them, The God of heaven, he will prosper us; therefore we his servants will arise and build: but ye have no portion, nor right, nor memorial, in Jerusalem.' Mockery is one of the oldest ways to stop God’s work. Nehemiah did not argue or panic. He pointed to God. The God of heaven will give us success. That is why we will build. When people laugh at what God has called you to do, keep your eyes on him.
The wall was finished in just fifty-two days. Then the people gathered to hear God’s law read aloud, and they wept. And they were told, 'for the joy of the LORD is your strength.' The people were home, the temple stood, and the walls were rebuilt. But there was still no king of David’s line on the throne. With Nehemiah and the prophets after him, the Old Testament ends. God’s people waited, for a long time, for the promised King.
Is there something broken in your life, your family, or your community that God is calling you to help rebuild? Begin with prayer, like Nehemiah, then take a first step. And when you need help in a hard moment, pray a quick prayer to the God of heaven. He hears.
Here is today’s question. Nehemiah prayed a quick prayer before he answered the king. In what moment today will you stop and pray before you speak or act?
Let us pray. God of heaven, thank you that your good hand is on your people, and that you hear even my quickest prayers. Show me what you want me to help rebuild, and give me courage when others mock. Let us rise up and build. Amen.
