Long after Moses, a man is nailed to a wooden cross outside Jerusalem. As the day ends, his friends hurry to take his body down before nightfall, and lay it in a tomb. Why the rush? Partly because of a law Moses gave, here in Deuteronomy chapter twenty one. And that law tells us something astonishing about what happened on that cross.
In chapter twenty, Moses told the people, when you go out to battle and see horses and chariots, be not afraid, for the LORD your God is he that goeth with you, to fight for you, to save you. Before any battle, they were to proclaim peace. And even in a siege, they were to spare the fruit trees.
Now, in chapter twenty one, Moses gives a group of laws about life and death, families, and justice. Some are hard, and we will tell them briefly. Listen for how God cares about innocent life. And listen for the law at the end that points far ahead.
Suppose, Moses says, someone is found dead in a field, and no one knows who did it.
God does not let it be ignored. The elders and judges are to measure the distance to the nearest towns. Then the elders of the nearest town take a young cow, one that has never worked, down to a valley with flowing water, and there it is offered. The priests come, for they settle every dispute. And all the elders of that town wash their hands over the heifer, and say, our hands did not shed this blood, and our eyes did not see it.
And they pray.
Even when no one could be found guilty, a death mattered. Innocent life was precious to God. The whole community was to treat it seriously, and seek God’s mercy.
Then Moses gives several laws about family life.
One is about a woman taken captive in war. It gave her a time to mourn, and protected her from being treated as property. If she was later sent away, she was to go free, and could not be sold. Another is about a son who is utterly stubborn and rebellious, and given to excess, and refuses all correction from his parents. These are hard laws from Israel’s life in that time, and Christians have understood them in different ways. What we can notice is that they put limits on cruelty, and brought family matters before the elders, rather than leaving them to angry individuals.
Then Moses gives a law that protected the weaker members of a family.
In those days, a man might have two wives, and love one more than the other. Moses says that if the firstborn son belongs to the wife who is less loved, the father must still give him the firstborn’s rights, a double share of the inheritance. He must not favour the son of the wife he loves more.
God would not let a father’s feelings override justice. The firstborn’s rights belonged to him, whoever his mother was.
The Bible describes families like this honestly. But it does not hold them up as God’s best. Again and again, from Jacob onwards, homes with more than one wife were full of jealousy and pain.
Then comes the last law of the chapter.
If a man is put to death for a crime, Moses says, and his body is hung on a tree, his body must not stay there through the dark hours. Bury him the same day.
And then Moses gives the reason.
To be hanged on a tree was a sign of being under God’s curse, the curse that falls on sin.
And this is where the cross comes in. When Jesus died, he was hung on a wooden cross, a tree. And the apostle Paul, thinking of this very verse, wrote one of the most astonishing sentences in the New Testament. Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us.
Jesus had done nothing wrong. He did not deserve any curse. But on the cross, he took the curse that our sin deserved, so that we could receive God’s blessing instead.
And his friends took his body down before the day was over, just as this law said, and laid him in a tomb. But the tomb could not hold him.
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 8. 'Be merciful, O LORD, unto thy people Israel, whom thou hast redeemed, and lay not innocent blood unto thy people of Israel’s charge. And the blood shall be forgiven them.' Be merciful, O LORD. Even when the guilty could not be found, the people prayed for mercy. Every death mattered, and every community was responsible. When wrong is done among us, the right response is to take it seriously, and to cry out to God for mercy.
Verse 17. 'But he shall acknowledge the son of the hated for the firstborn, by giving him a double portion of all that he hath: for he is the beginning of his strength; the right of the firstborn is his.' Hated here means less loved. God protected the son whose mother was unloved. God sees those who are overlooked in families, and he insists on fairness for them. Favouritism has no place in God’s family.
Verse 23. The body must be buried the same day, Moses says, 'for he that is hanged is accursed of God;' To hang on a tree was to be under God’s curse. Paul quoted this when he wrote about the cross. Jesus became a curse for us. He took what we deserved, so that we could have what he deserved. That is the heart of the gospel.
Paul wrote, 'Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us'. And Peter wrote that Jesus 'his own self bare our sins in his own body on the tree'.
Spend a quiet moment today thinking about the cross. Jesus took the curse that your sin deserved. Thank him for it. And think of anyone in your family or circle who is overlooked or less loved. Show them fairness and kindness.
Here is today’s question. Paul said that Christ became a curse for us, when he hung on the tree. What does it mean to you that Jesus took the curse you deserved?
Let us pray. Lord Jesus, you were hung on a tree, and became a curse for me. Thank you that you took what I deserved, so that I could receive God’s blessing. Be merciful to us, O LORD. Help me to treat everyone fairly, and to care for those who are overlooked. Amen.
