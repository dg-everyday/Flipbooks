A servant has run away from a cruel master in another land. He has crossed the border into Israel, frightened and exhausted, with nothing. His master’s men are looking for him. If he is found, he will be dragged back. And God says to his people: do not hand him over. Let him live among you, wherever he chooses. Do not oppress him.
In chapter twenty two, Moses told the people, when you see your brother’s lost ox or sheep, thou shalt not hide thyself. Help lift a fallen animal. Let the mother bird go. Build a wall around your roof to keep people safe. And the law protected a woman who cried out with no one to save her.
Now, in chapter twenty three, Moses gives more laws for life in God’s camp and in the land. Listen for why the camp must be clean. And listen for how God treats the runaway, the borrower, and the hungry traveller.
The chapter begins with laws about who could join the assembly of Israel, the gathering of God’s people for worship. Some were kept out for a time.
The Ammonites and the Moabites were not to join the assembly. Moses explains why. They did not meet Israel with bread and water on the journey from Egypt. And they hired Balaam to curse Israel.
And then Moses adds something beautiful.
And yet, remember the book of Ruth? Ruth was a Moabite. And she was welcomed into Israel, and became the great grandmother of King David, and is named in the family line of Jesus. Even when the law kept people out, God made a way in, for those who truly turned to him.
Moses goes on. Do not hate an Edomite, for he is your brother. Do not hate an Egyptian, because you lived as foreigners in his land. Their grandchildren may join the assembly of the LORD.
Even the Egyptians, who had enslaved them. God did not want his people to carry hatred for ever.
Then Moses speaks about keeping the army’s camp clean in time of war. There are simple rules about washing, and about keeping waste outside the camp, buried in the ground.
And Moses gives the reason.
God was right there, in the middle of the camp. So his people were to keep it clean, and holy.
Then comes the law about the runaway servant.
Do not hand back to his master a servant who has escaped to you. Let him live among you, in whatever town he chooses.
In other nations, a runaway slave was hunted down and returned. But in Israel, God said, give him refuge. Let him choose where to live. Do not oppress him. God’s people were to be a place of safety for those escaping cruelty.
Then Moses gives laws about money. Do not charge interest to your brother when you lend him money, or food, or anything else. Lending was meant to help a brother in need, not to profit from his hardship.
Then Moses speaks about vows. When you make a vow to the LORD your God, do not be slow to keep it. The LORD will surely require it of you. But if you choose not to make a vow, that is no sin.
And then the chapter ends with a law about hungry travellers.
When you walk through your neighbour’s vineyard, you may eat as many grapes as you want. But you must not put any in a basket. And when you walk through your neighbour’s field of grain, you may pick the ears with your hand. But do not take a sickle to his crop.
A hungry person passing by could eat. That was generous. But they could not take away more than they needed. That was fair to the owner.
Long after, Jesus and his disciples were walking through fields of grain, and the disciples were hungry, and picked some ears and ate them. That was exactly what this law allowed.
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 5. 'Nevertheless the LORD thy God would not hearken unto Balaam; but the LORD thy God turned the curse into a blessing unto thee, because the LORD thy God loved thee.' Turned the curse into a blessing. That is what God does for those he loves. Balaam was hired to curse, and God made him bless. And on the cross, Jesus took our curse, and turned it into blessing. Because the LORD thy God loved thee.
Verse 14. 'For the LORD thy God walketh in the midst of thy camp, to deliver thee, and to give up thine enemies before thee; therefore shall thy camp be holy: that he see no unclean thing in thee, and turn away from thee.' The LORD walketh in the midst of thy camp. God was not far away. He walked among them, to protect and deliver them. Paul later wrote that God’s people are his temple, and that his Spirit lives in us. So our lives, too, are to be holy.
Verse 16. 'He shall dwell with thee, even among you, in that place which he shall choose in one of thy gates, where it liketh him best: thou shalt not oppress him.' Where it liketh him best. The runaway could choose his new home. He was not to be sent back, or mistreated. God’s people are to be a refuge for those fleeing cruelty, and to welcome them with kindness.
Paul wrote, 'Know ye not that ye are the temple of God, and that the Spirit of God dwelleth in you?' And the Gospel tells how Jesus’ disciples 'began to pluck the ears of corn, and to eat.'
Is there someone in your community who has fled hardship, a refugee, a newcomer, someone escaping a hard situation? Think about how you could make them welcome. And thank God that he walks with you, today.
Here is today’s question. God told his people to give refuge to a runaway servant, and let him live where he chose. What does this teach you about how God wants us to treat people who are fleeing hardship?
Let us pray. Lord God, thank you that you walk in the midst of your people. Thank you that you turn curses into blessings, because you love us. Make me someone who gives refuge to the oppressed, and welcomes the stranger. And keep my life holy, as a place where you dwell. Amen.
