A farmer walks to the house of God, carrying a basket. In it are the very first grapes, the first figs, the first sheaves of grain from his own land. He hands the basket to the priest. And then, standing before God, he begins to tell a story. Not his own story, but his family’s story, going all the way back to Jacob. A story of wandering, slavery, and rescue.
In chapter twenty five, Moses said, thou shalt not muzzle the ox when he treadeth out the corn. He told the people to use a perfect and just weight. And he reminded them of the law of the brother’s duty to a widow, the law Boaz kept for Ruth. And he said, remember Amalek, who attacked the feeble at the back.
Now, in chapter twenty six, Moses comes to the end of the laws for the land. Listen for the story the farmer tells. And listen for the promise God and his people make to each other at the end.
When you have come into the land the LORD your God is giving you, Moses says, and you have settled there, then take some of the first of all the fruit of the ground that you harvest, and put it in a basket, and go to the place the LORD your God chooses.
Go to the priest, and say to him, I declare today to the LORD your God that I have come into the land which the LORD swore to our fathers to give us.
The priest takes the basket, and sets it down before the altar of the LORD.
And then the farmer speaks.
A Syrian ready to perish. That was Jacob, a wanderer, who lived for a time in Syria with his uncle Laban, and later went down to Egypt with a small family, seventy people. And there, they became a great nation.
And the Egyptians treated us harshly, the farmer goes on. They afflicted us, and made us work as slaves.
Then we cried out.
And the LORD brought us out of Egypt with a mighty hand and an outstretched arm, with great terror, and with signs and wonders. And he brought us into this place, and gave us this land, a land flowing with milk and honey.
And now, look, I have brought the first of the fruit of the land which you, O LORD, have given me.
Then he sets the basket down before the LORD, and worships.
Think about what this meant. Every farmer, every year, retold the whole story of God’s rescue. The basket of fruit was not just a gift. It was a testimony. Everything I have, I have because God rescued us.
And then Moses says, and you shall rejoice in every good thing the LORD your God has given you and your household, you, and the Levite, and the stranger among you.
The thanksgiving was a celebration, shared with those who had no land of their own.
Then Moses speaks about the third year tithe, the tithe given to the Levite, the stranger, the fatherless and the widow. When you have given it, Moses says, you shall say before the LORD, I have given the holy portion to the Levite, the stranger, the fatherless and the widow, just as you commanded me. I have not forgotten your commands.
And then the farmer prays a lovely prayer. Look down from your holy dwelling place, from heaven, and bless your people Israel, and the land you have given us, as you swore to our fathers, a land flowing with milk and honey.
Then Moses brings the whole section of laws to a close, with a solemn covenant.
Today the LORD your God commands you to keep these laws, Moses says. So keep them, and do them, with all your heart and soul.
Today you have declared that the LORD is your God, and that you will walk in his ways, keep his laws, and listen to his voice.
And today, Moses says, the LORD has declared something too.
And God promised to set them high above all the nations he had made, in praise, and fame, and honour, a holy people to the LORD their God.
Two declarations. The people say, the LORD is our God. And God says, you are my people. That is what a covenant is. A promise of belonging, both ways.
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 5. 'And thou shalt speak and say before the LORD thy God, A Syrian ready to perish was my father, and he went down into Egypt, and sojourned there with a few, and became there a nation, great, mighty, and populous:' Ready to perish. Israel’s story began with a wandering, nearly lost man. Every Israelite was to remember their humble beginnings. It is good for us, too, to remember where we came from, and how God has brought us so far.
Verse 7. 'And when we cried unto the LORD God of our fathers, the LORD heard our voice, and looked on our affliction, and our labour, and our oppression:' The LORD heard, and looked. God saw their suffering, and heard their cry. He is the same God today. When we cry out to him, he hears, and he sees.
Verse 18. 'And the LORD hath avouched thee this day to be his peculiar people, as he hath promised thee, and that thou shouldest keep all his commandments;' Avouched means declared, publicly claimed. God publicly claimed Israel as his own treasured people. The New Testament says the same of all who belong to Christ: a peculiar people, to shew forth the praises of him who hath called you out of darkness into his marvellous light.
Peter wrote, 'But ye are a chosen generation, a royal priesthood, an holy nation, a peculiar people; that ye should shew forth the praises of him who hath called you out of darkness into his marvellous light'. And a psalm says, 'Bless the LORD, O my soul, and forget not all his benefits'.
Bring God the first of something today, the first part of your day in prayer, the first of your income, or the first of your energy. And as you give it, tell him the story of how he has rescued and blessed you.
Here is today’s question. Every year, the farmer retold the story of God’s rescue as he brought his firstfruits. What is the story of how God has rescued and blessed you, that you could tell?
