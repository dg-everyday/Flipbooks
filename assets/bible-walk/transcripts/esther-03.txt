Every day, when a certain man walks through the king’s gate, everyone bows low to the ground. Everyone except one. Day after day, one man stays standing. The proud man notices. And his anger grows so great that it is no longer enough to punish one man. He decides to destroy a whole people.
In chapter two, the king searched for a new queen, and the young orphan Esther was taken to the palace. She won the favour of all who saw her, and the king set the royal crown on her head. She kept her people secret, as Mordecai told her. And Mordecai uncovered a plot against the king, and it was written in the book of the chronicles, but he was never rewarded.
Now, in chapter three, the villain of the story steps onto the stage. His name is Haman. Listen for how pride and anger grow into something terrible. And listen for how carelessly a king can sign away the lives of thousands.
After these things, King Ahasuerus promotes a man named Haman, the son of Hammedatha the Agagite. He sets Haman’s seat above all the princes who are with him. Haman is now the most powerful man in the empire, after the king.
Haman is called the Agagite. Agag was a king of the Amalekites, long before, in the days of King Saul. The Amalekites had been bitter enemies of Israel ever since the days of Moses. And Mordecai, remember, is from the tribe of Benjamin, the same tribe as King Saul. An old conflict is waking up again.
The king has commanded that everyone must bow to Haman.
The king’s servants at the gate say to Mordecai, why do you disobey the king’s command? They speak to him day after day, but he will not listen. So they tell Haman, to see whether Mordecai will get away with it. For Mordecai has told them that he is a Jew.
The Bible does not tell us exactly why Mordecai would not bow. Perhaps, as a Jew, he would not give a man the kind of honour he believed belonged to God alone. Perhaps it was because Haman was an Agagite, an enemy of his people. Whatever his reason, Mordecai would not bow.
When Haman sees that Mordecai does not bow, or show him reverence, he is filled with fury.
So Haman and his people cast Pur, that is, the lot, before Haman, day after day, month after month, to choose a lucky day for his plan. Casting lots was like throwing dice. Haman lets chance pick the day. And the lot falls on a day almost a year away. Haman thinks he is trusting luck. But that long delay will give God’s people time.
Then Haman goes to the king. Listen to how cleverly he speaks. There is a certain people, scattered abroad among all the provinces of your kingdom. Their laws are different from everyone else’s, and they do not keep the king’s laws. It is not in the king’s interest to put up with them. If it please the king, let it be written that they may be destroyed. And I will pay ten thousand talents of silver into the king’s treasury.
Notice that Haman never names the people. He never mentions Mordecai. He mixes a little truth with a lot of lies. And he offers a huge bribe, a fortune in silver.
The king takes his signet ring from his hand, and gives it to Haman, the Jews’ enemy. With that ring, Haman can seal laws in the king’s name. The king says, the silver is given to thee, the people also, to do with them as it seemeth good to thee.
The king does not even ask who these people are. He signs away their lives without a second thought.
So the king’s scribes are called, and everything Haman commands is written, to every governor and ruler of every province, to every people in its own language. It is written in the name of King Ahasuerus, and sealed with the king’s ring.
The letters are sent out by swift messengers into all the provinces. The order is to destroy all the Jews, young and old, little children and women, in one day, and to take their goods for plunder. It is a law of the Medes and Persians. It cannot be changed.
The messengers hurry out, at the king’s command, and the decree is given in Shushan the palace.
From a human point of view, it seems hopeless. A law that cannot be changed. A king who does not care. A powerful enemy. And God’s people, scattered and helpless. But remember who is already in the palace, wearing the royal crown.
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 2. 'And all the king’s servants, that were in the king’s gate, bowed, and reverenced Haman: for the king had so commanded concerning him. But Mordecai bowed not, nor did him reverence.' Everyone else bowed. It would have been easy for Mordecai to go along with the crowd. But he stood alone. Sometimes faithfulness to God means being the only one who will not bow. That takes courage, and it may cost something. Mordecai’s stand would put him, and his people, in great danger. But he would not give to a man the honour that belonged to God.
Verse 6. 'And he thought scorn to lay hands on Mordecai alone; for they had shewed him the people of Mordecai: wherefore Haman sought to destroy all the Jews that were throughout the whole kingdom of Ahasuerus, even the people of Mordecai.' Haman’s pride was wounded by one man. And his hatred grew until he wanted to destroy a whole people. This is what pride and hatred do when they are left to grow. They are never satisfied. Behind Haman’s plan was an old hatred of God’s people, one that has appeared again and again in history. But God has promised to keep his people.
Verse 15. 'The posts went out, being hastened by the king’s commandment, and the decree was given in Shushan the palace. And the king and Haman sat down to drink; but the city Shushan was perplexed.' What a picture. A law has just been signed that will destroy thousands of families. And the king and Haman sit down to drink together, as if nothing has happened. But the city is perplexed, confused and troubled. Even ordinary people in Shushan can see that something terrible and wrong has been done. Those who hold power without caring for people can do great harm.
God promised Abraham, 'And I will bless them that bless thee, and curse him that curseth thee'. And a psalm says, 'The LORD bringeth the counsel of the heathen to nought: he maketh the devices of the people of none effect.'
Mordecai would not bow to Haman, even when everyone else did. Is there a place in your life where you are under pressure to go along with the crowd in something you know is wrong? Ask God today for the courage to stand.
Here is today’s question. Haman’s pride grew into hatred that wanted to destroy a whole people. How can we guard our hearts, so that small hurts and pride do not grow into bitterness?
Let us pray. Lord God, give me courage like Mordecai’s, to honour you above everyone else, even when I stand alone. Guard my heart from pride and hatred. And thank you that no plan against your people can stand when you are against it. You are my refuge. Amen.
