For almost a year, a date has hung over the Jewish people like a dark cloud. The day Haman chose by casting lots. The day his law named for their destruction. Families have counted down to it in fear. Now the day has come. But it does not turn out the way Haman planned. Not at all.
In chapter eight, the king gave Haman’s house to Esther, and his ring to Mordecai. Esther pleaded with tears for her people, and because Haman’s law could not be changed, a new law was written, allowing the Jews to stand for their lives. Mordecai went out in royal robes, and the Jews had light, and gladness, and joy, and honour.
Now, in chapter nine, the appointed day arrives. This chapter is hard to hear, because it tells of a day of battle. But listen for how the story turns completely around, and how the day meant for sorrow becomes a day of joy.
The day comes when the king’s first decree is to be carried out, the day when the enemies of the Jews hoped to overpower them. 'though it was turned to the contrary, that the Jews had rule over them that hated them'. It is turned upside down.
The Jews gather in their cities throughout all the provinces, to defend themselves against those who seek their harm. And no one can stand against them, for the fear of them falls on all the people.
All the rulers of the provinces, the governors and officers of the king, help the Jews, because the fear of Mordecai has fallen on them.
The writer tells us why.
So the Jews defend themselves against their enemies, against those who hate them. In Shushan the palace, many of their enemies fall, among them the ten sons of Haman, the enemy of the Jews.
And then the writer adds something important, and he says it three times in this chapter. 'but on the spoil laid they not their hand.' Haman’s law had said that the Jews’ goods could be taken as plunder. The new law allowed the Jews to take plunder too. But they did not touch it. They were not fighting to get rich. They were fighting for their lives.
The king hears the news, and he asks Esther what more she wants. She asks that the Jews in Shushan be allowed to defend themselves for one more day, and the king grants it.
Across the provinces, the Jews gather and stand for their lives, and they have rest from their enemies. Many thousands of those who hated them fall.
This is a hard chapter for us to hear. It is right to feel the sorrow of it. But remember how it came about. The Jews did not start this battle. It was a day of attack planned against them, by a law that could not be undone. Anyone who wanted to harm them had been given permission. The Jews were defending their families and their lives. And they took no plunder.
When the fighting is over, the Jews rest, and make it a day of feasting and gladness. In the villages and towns, they celebrate with feasting, and with sending portions of food to one another.
Mordecai writes these things down, and sends letters to all the Jews in all the provinces of King Ahasuerus, near and far. He tells them to remember these days every year.
Mordecai’s letter includes gifts to the poor. The joy of being rescued is not kept to themselves. It overflows in generosity to those in need.
So the Jews agree to do as they have begun, as Mordecai has written to them.
They call these days Purim, after the word Pur, which means the lot. Haman cast the lot to choose the day of their destruction. But that very day became the day of their deliverance. And so the feast takes its name from the very thing Haman used to plan their end.
Then Queen Esther and Mordecai the Jew write a second letter, with all authority, to confirm the days of Purim. It is sent to all the Jews in the hundred and twenty seven provinces of the kingdom of Ahasuerus, with words of peace and truth. And the decree of Esther confirms these matters of Purim, and it is written in the book.
Look how far the story has come. At the start of the book, the Jews were a forgotten minority. Then they were condemned to death. Now they are honoured, protected and full of joy. And Esther, the orphan girl, is writing letters with royal authority.
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 4. 'For Mordecai was great in the king’s house, and his fame went out throughout all the provinces: for this man Mordecai waxed greater and greater.' Waxed greater and greater means he grew more and more powerful. Remember Haman, who was raised higher and higher, and then fell. Now Mordecai, the man who refused to bow, the man who sat in sackcloth, is raised higher and higher. The proud are brought down, and the humble are lifted up. That is the pattern of the whole book, and of the whole Bible.
Verse 22. 'As the days wherein the Jews rested from their enemies, and the month which was turned unto them from sorrow to joy, and from mourning into a good day: that they should make them days of feasting and joy, and of sending portions one to another, and gifts to the poor.' Turned from sorrow to joy, from mourning into a good day. This sums up the book of Esther. And notice how they celebrate: with feasting, with sharing food with one another, and with gifts to the poor. When God turns our sorrow into joy, the natural response is to share it, especially with those in need.
Verse 24. 'Because Haman the son of Hammedatha, the Agagite, the enemy of all the Jews, had devised against the Jews to destroy them, and had cast Pur, that is, the lot, to consume them, and to destroy them;' Haman trusted in the lot, in chance, to choose the day of destruction. But behind the lot was the hand of God. The day Haman thought would be his triumph became his people’s deliverance. Haman’s plans were never really in the hands of chance. They were in the hands of God.
The book of Proverbs says, 'The lot is cast into the lap; but the whole disposing thereof is of the LORD.' And a psalm says, 'Thou hast turned for me my mourning into dancing: thou hast put off my sackcloth, and girded me with gladness'.
The Jews celebrated their rescue by sharing food with one another and giving gifts to the poor. How could you share your joy in God today? Think of one person in need, and give them something, a meal, a gift, or your time.
Here is today’s question. Haman trusted in the lot, but the whole disposing thereof is of the LORD. What does it mean to you that your life is not in the hands of chance, but in the hands of God?
Let us pray. Lord God, thank you that my life is not ruled by chance, but held in your hands. Thank you that you can turn sorrow into joy, and mourning into a good day. When you rescue me, help me to remember it, to give thanks, and to share my joy generously with others, especially the poor. Amen.
