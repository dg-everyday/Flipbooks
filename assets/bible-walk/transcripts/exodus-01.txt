In the burning sun of Egypt, thousands of men are making bricks from mud and straw, and hauling them to build great cities for the king. Taskmasters stand over them, shouting orders. The king is afraid of these people. He has made them slaves. But the harder he works them, the more of them there are.
In the introduction, we heard that Exodus means going out, and that it carries on straight from Genesis. At the end of Genesis, Jacob’s family came down to Egypt, seventy people in all. Joseph saved them from the famine, and before he died, he said, God will surely visit you, and bring you out of this land.
Now, in chapter one, many years have passed. Joseph and all his brothers have died. And the family that came down to Egypt has become a great people. But the land that once saved them has turned against them. Listen for two brave women, and for the quiet hand of God.
The chapter begins by naming the sons of Israel who came into Egypt with Jacob, each with his household: Reuben, Simeon, Levi and Judah, Issachar, Zebulun and Benjamin, Dan and Naphtali, Gad and Asher. All the souls that came from Jacob were seventy. Joseph was in Egypt already. Then Joseph dies, and all his brothers, and all that generation.
But God’s promise to Abraham does not die with them.
Then a new king arises over Egypt, who knew not Joseph. He does not remember, or does not care, how Joseph saved Egypt. He looks at the Hebrews and sees only a threat. He says to his people, behold, the people of the children of Israel are more and mightier than we. Come, let us deal wisely with them, lest they multiply, and if there is a war, they join our enemies, and fight against us, and get them up out of the land.
So the Egyptians set taskmasters over them, to afflict them with heavy burdens. They build for Pharaoh treasure cities, Pithom and Raamses.
The Egyptians make the children of Israel serve with rigour, with harshness. 'And they made their lives bitter with hard bondage, in morter, and in brick, and in all manner of service in the field'. The descendants of Joseph, who once ruled Egypt, are now its slaves.
When hard labour does not work, the king tries something far more cruel. He speaks to the Hebrew midwives, the women who help mothers give birth. Two of them are named: Shiphrah and Puah. The king commands them that when they help the Hebrew women give birth, if the baby is a son, they are to put him to death. If it is a daughter, she may live.
It is a terrible command, from the most powerful man in the land. But the midwives fear God more than they fear the king.
The king calls for the midwives, and says, why have you done this thing, and saved the boys alive? The midwives answer, the Hebrew women are not like the Egyptian women. They are lively, strong, and they give birth before the midwives can even get to them.
'Therefore God dealt well with the midwives: and the people multiplied, and waxed very mighty.' And because the midwives feared God, he made them houses, he gave them families of their own.
Notice something about this chapter. The king of Egypt is never named. He is simply Pharaoh, the king. But the two humble midwives are named, Shiphrah and Puah. The world remembers kings. God remembers those who fear him.
Then Pharaoh gives a command to all his people. Every son that is born to the Hebrews, you shall cast into the river, and every daughter you shall save alive. The river Nile, the source of Egypt’s life, is to become a place of death for Hebrew boys. It is into this dark world that the next chapter will bring a baby boy.
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 7. 'And the children of Israel were fruitful, and increased abundantly, and multiplied, and waxed exceeding mighty; and the land was filled with them.' These words echo the very first chapter of the Bible, where God told Adam and Eve, be fruitful, and multiply, and fill the earth. And God had promised Abraham that his seed would be as the stars of heaven. Here, in a foreign land, God was keeping that promise. Even far from the promised land, God’s blessing was at work.
Verse 12. 'But the more they afflicted them, the more they multiplied and grew. And they were grieved because of the children of Israel.' Pharaoh tried to crush God’s people. But the harder he pushed, the more they grew. No king can stop what God has promised. Throughout history, God’s people have often grown most in the hardest times. Pressure that was meant to destroy them only made them stronger.
Verse 17. 'But the midwives feared God, and did not as the king of Egypt commanded them, but saved the men children alive.' To fear God means to honour him above everyone else, to care more about what he thinks than what anyone else thinks. The midwives had no power, no army, no position. But they refused to do evil, even when the king commanded it. Their courage saved many lives. When human authority commands us to do what God forbids, we must obey God.
In the book of Acts, the apostles stood before the council and said, 'We ought to obey God rather than men.' And Proverbs says, 'The fear of man bringeth a snare: but whoso putteth his trust in the LORD shall be safe.'
Is there pressure on you to do something you know is wrong, because someone powerful wants you to? Remember Shiphrah and Puah. Fear God more than people. And if you are going through a hard time, remember that God’s people often grow strongest under pressure.
Here is today’s question. The midwives feared God more than they feared the king. Where do you need courage to do what is right, even when others want you to do wrong?
Let us pray. Lord God, thank you that no king or power can stop your promises. Give me the courage of Shiphrah and Puah, to fear you more than any person, and to protect those who are weak. When hard times come, help me to keep growing in you. Amen.
