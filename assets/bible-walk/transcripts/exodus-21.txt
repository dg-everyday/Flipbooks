Imagine a brand new country, just starting out. For a long, long time they were slaves, and their masters made all the rules. Now they are free. But what happens when someone steals? When someone is hurt in a fight? When an animal causes an accident? How should people treat one another? In Exodus chapter twenty one, God begins to answer those questions.
Welcome back to Exodus on The Living Voice. In chapters one to twenty, we walked through the great rescue. God’s people, the children of Israel, were slaves in Egypt. God called Moses at the burning bush, and said, I AM THAT I AM. He sent ten plagues on Egypt. At the first Passover, the blood of a lamb on the doorposts kept every home safe. God parted the Red Sea, and his people walked through on dry ground. And at Mount Sinai, in fire and smoke, God spoke the Ten Commandments.
Now we walk through the second half of Exodus, chapters twenty one to forty. First come laws for daily life. Then God gives instructions for a holy tent, the tabernacle, where he will live among his people. Then comes a terrible failure, the golden calf. And at the end, God’s glory fills the tabernacle.
In chapter twenty one, listen for how God protects the weak. And listen for a famous law that is often misunderstood.
After the Ten Commandments, God gives Moses more detailed laws. Chapters twenty one, twenty two and twenty three are sometimes called the book of the covenant. They show how the Ten Commandments work in everyday life.
Some of these laws may sound strange or hard to us. Christians have understood in different ways how these laws apply today. But as we listen, we can see what God cares about. And that never changes.
The chapter begins with servants. In those days, if a person fell into terrible debt, and could not pay, they might sell themselves to work for someone else, to pay it off. This was very different from the cruel slavery Israel had suffered in Egypt. God set limits, to protect the servant.
And then a surprising law. What if the servant did not want to go? What if he loved his master, and his family, and wanted to stay? Then he could choose to serve his master for life. His master would bring him to the door, and mark his ear, as a sign that he had chosen this freely, out of love.
Then God gives laws about violence. 'He that smiteth a man, so that he die, shall be surely put to death.' Human life is precious, because people are made in God’s image. Taking a life was the most serious crime of all.
But God makes a difference between murder and an accident. If someone killed another person by accident, without planning it, God would appoint a place where he could flee, and be safe. Later, these places were called cities of refuge. God cares about justice, and about mercy.
Children must honour their father and mother. Anyone who kidnapped a person to sell them was to be punished with death. 'And he that stealeth a man, and selleth him, or if he be found in his hand, he shall surely be put to death.'
If two men fought, and one was hurt but did not die, the one who hurt him had to pay for the time he lost from work, and make sure he was 'thoroughly healed.' Harm had to be put right.
Then comes the famous law.
Then laws to protect servants from cruel masters.
Then laws about animals and accidents. If an ox gored a person, the ox was killed. But if the owner knew the ox was dangerous, and did nothing, the owner was responsible too. If someone dug a pit and left it uncovered, and an animal fell in, 'The owner of the pit shall make it good'. We are responsible for what we own, and what we do.
Can you see what God cares about in all these laws? He protects the weak: servants, the injured, those who have accidents. He limits revenge. He makes people responsible for their actions. And he values every human life.
Jesus later summed up the whole law in two commands. Love God with all your heart, and love your neighbour as yourself. Chapter twenty one shows what loving your neighbour looked like in a farming village long ago. Not hurting them. Not stealing them. Paying them back if you damage what is theirs. And protecting those who have no power.
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 2. 'If thou buy an Hebrew servant, six years he shall serve: and in the seventh he shall go out free for nothing.' No one was to be a servant for ever against their will. After six years, they went free, and did not have to pay anything. The pattern of six and then seven echoes the week of creation, six days of work and then rest. God, who had set his people free from Egypt, built freedom into their laws. And Deuteronomy adds that the master was to send him away with gifts, not empty handed.
Verse 24. 'Eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, foot for foot'. Many people think this law is cruel. But it was actually a law of limits. In those days, if someone hurt you, you might take terrible revenge, far worse than the harm done. This law said, the punishment must fit the crime, and no more. And it was for judges to decide, not for people to take revenge themselves. Jesus went even further. He taught his followers not to take revenge at all, but to turn the other cheek.
Verse 26. 'And if a man smite the eye of his servant, or the eye of his maid, that it perish; he shall let him go free for his eye’s sake.' In most nations of that time, a servant had almost no rights. A master could do whatever he liked. But not in Israel. If a master injured his servant, even by knocking out a tooth, the servant went free. God was saying, servants are people, made in my image, and I am watching how you treat them.
Jesus said, 'Ye have heard that it hath been said, An eye for an eye, and a tooth for a tooth: But I say unto you, That ye resist not evil'. And Paul wrote, 'There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither bond nor free, there is neither male nor female: for ye are all one in Christ Jesus.'
Think about the people around you who have less power than you: younger children, people who are new, people who are left out. How can you protect them and treat them kindly today? And if you have damaged or broken something that belongs to someone else, put it right.
Here is today’s question. The law of an eye for an eye was given to limit revenge. Why do you think people often want to pay back more than the harm done to them, and what did Jesus teach instead?
Let us pray. Lord God, thank you that you care about justice, and that you protect the weak. Help me to treat everyone as someone made in your image. Keep me from taking revenge, and help me to forgive. When I have caused harm, help me to put it right. And help me to love my neighbour as myself. Amen.
