Some people are building a house. Neighbours come by and say, can we help? But the builders know these neighbours do not really want the house to be built. They say no, thank you. The neighbours smile, and walk away. And then the trouble starts. Whispers. Complaints. Letters to the authorities. Rumours. Until one day, an official arrives, and orders the builders to stop.
In chapter three, the people came together as one man and built an altar, though fear was upon them. They kept the feast, and laid the foundation of the temple. The priests blew trumpets, the Levites sang, he is good, for his mercy endureth for ever. And the people shouted for joy, while the old men who remembered the first temple wept.
Now, in chapter four, the builders face opposition. Listen for how it begins, with a friendly offer. And listen for what happens to the work.
The enemies of Judah and Benjamin hear that the returned exiles are building the temple of the LORD. So they come to Zerubbabel and the leaders, and make what sounds like a kind offer. 'Let us build with you: for we seek your God, as ye do; and we do sacrifice unto him since the days of Esar- haddon king of Assur, which brought us up hither.'
Who were these people? Do you remember the end of the northern kingdom, in second Kings? The king of Assyria brought people from other lands to live in Samaria. They feared the LORD, the writer said, but they also served their own gods. These were their descendants. They worshipped the LORD as one god among many.
So Zerubbabel says no.
That might sound unkind. But the leaders knew that if they let these people help, their mixed worship would come into the house of God. It had happened before, and it had led to exile. They would not let it happen again.
And immediately the friendly mask comes off.
They hire advisers, people to speak against the Jews to the Persian officials, to frustrate their plans. And they keep it up, for years and years, through the reign of Cyrus, and into the reign of Darius.
Then the writer does something interesting. He jumps ahead in time, to show how the opposition kept going for a very long time, even later, in the reigns of the kings Ahasuerus and Artaxerxes, when the people were rebuilding the walls of Jerusalem. It is as if he says, here is the kind of thing they kept doing, again and again.
He gives an example. A Persian official named Rehum, and a scribe named Shimshai, write a letter to king Artaxerxes. Let the king know, they write, that the Jews have come to Jerusalem, and are rebuilding that rebellious and wicked city. They are finishing the walls, and repairing the foundations. If this city is rebuilt, they will stop paying taxes, and the king will lose money. Look in the records. You will find that this city has always rebelled against kings. That is why it was destroyed. If it is rebuilt, you will lose control of the whole region.
It was a clever letter, mixing a little truth with a lot of fear, and appealing to the king’s money and his power.
And it worked. The king writes back. I have had the records searched, and it is true. Jerusalem has rebelled against kings in the past. So order these men to stop, and do not let the city be rebuilt, until I give further orders.
When Rehum and Shimshai read the king’s letter, they hurry to Jerusalem, and force the Jews to stop, by power and by force.
And then the writer comes back to his main story, about the temple in the days of Darius. And the result of all that opposition is summed up in one sad sentence.
For years, the foundation of the temple sat there, with weeds growing over it. The people went home to their own houses, and their own work. The excitement of the great shout had faded. Opposition had done its work.
But this is not the end of the story. In the next chapter, God will send two prophets to get things moving again.
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 3. 'But Zerubbabel, and Jeshua, and the rest of the chief of the fathers of Israel, said unto them, Ye have nothing to do with us to build an house unto our God; but we ourselves together will build unto the LORD God of Israel, as king Cyrus the king of Persia hath commanded us.' Not all help is good help. Zerubbabel saw that this offer would bring false worship into God’s house. Sometimes being faithful means saying no, even to people who seem friendly. It takes wisdom to know when to accept help, and when to refuse it.
Verse 4. 'Then the people of the land weakened the hands of the people of Judah, and troubled them in building'. Weakened the hands means they discouraged them, made them lose heart. They did not attack with swords. They used discouragement, worry, rumours and complaints. Discouragement is one of the enemy’s favourite weapons. It does not need to destroy our work. It only needs to make us give up.
Verse 24. 'Then ceased the work of the house of God which is at Jerusalem. So it ceased unto the second year of the reign of Darius king of Persia.' The work ceased. Not because God had failed, but because the people had given up. Sometimes God’s work stops, not because of a great defeat, but because people get tired, discouraged, and distracted. But God does not give up on his work, even when we do.
Paul wrote, 'be ye stedfast, unmovable, always abounding in the work of the Lord, forasmuch as ye know that your labour is not in vain in the Lord.' And he wrote, 'If God be for us, who can be against us?'
Is there something good you started for God, and then stopped because you got discouraged? Bring it to him today. Ask him to strengthen your hands again. And think about whether your words strengthen or weaken the hands of others.
Here is today’s question. The enemies weakened the hands of the builders until the work stopped. What tends to discourage you from serving God, and how can you resist it?
Let us pray. Lord God, you know how easily I get discouraged. When others weaken my hands, strengthen them again. Give me wisdom to know which help to accept, and which to refuse. Help me to be steadfast in your work, knowing it is never in vain. And help my words to strengthen others, not weaken them. Amen.
