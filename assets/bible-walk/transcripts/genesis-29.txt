A young man works for seven long years, tending sheep in the heat and the cold, for one reason only. He loves a woman named Rachel, and her father has promised her to him. At last, the wedding day comes. There is a great feast. The bride is veiled. But when the veil comes off, the young man finds he has been tricked.
In chapter twenty-eight, Jacob fled from his brother Esau. He slept with a stone for a pillow, and dreamed of a ladder reaching to heaven. God promised, I am with thee, and will keep thee in all places whither thou goest. And Jacob woke and said, surely the LORD is in this place; and I knew it not.
Now, in chapter twenty-nine, Jacob arrives at the end of his journey, in the land of his mother’s family. He finds love, and he finds hard work. And the man who deceived his father is about to find out how it feels to be deceived.
Jacob goes on his journey, and comes into the land of the people of the east. He sees a well in a field, with three flocks of sheep lying by it. A great stone covers the mouth of the well. Jacob asks the shepherds where they are from. From Haran, they say. Do you know Laban, the son of Nahor? We know him. Is he well? He is well, and behold, Rachel his daughter is coming with the sheep.
When Jacob sees Rachel, his mother’s brother’s daughter, with Laban’s sheep, he goes near, rolls the stone from the well’s mouth by himself, and waters Laban’s flock. Then Jacob kisses Rachel, and lifts up his voice, and weeps. He tells her that he is her father’s relative, Rebekah’s son. And Rachel runs and tells her father.
Laban runs to meet Jacob, embraces him, kisses him, and brings him to his house. Surely thou art my bone and my flesh, he says. Jacob stays with him for a month, working.
Then Laban says, should you serve me for nothing because you are my relative? Tell me, what shall your wages be? Laban has two daughters. The elder is Leah, and the younger is Rachel. Leah is tender eyed, but Rachel is beautiful and well favoured. And Jacob loves Rachel. He says, I will serve you seven years for Rachel your younger daughter. Laban says, it is better that I give her to thee, than that I should give her to another man.
When the seven years are fulfilled, Jacob says, give me my wife. So Laban gathers all the men of the place and makes a feast. But Laban takes Leah, his elder daughter, and brings her to Jacob instead. With the bride veiled, and perhaps in the dark of the wedding tent, Jacob does not discover the trick until it is too late.
Then Jacob confronts Laban.
Laban answers coolly. It must not be so done in our country, to give the younger before the firstborn. Did Jacob notice the sting in those words? He had taken the place of the firstborn himself. Laban says, fulfil her week, finish the wedding week with Leah, and we will give you Rachel too, for another seven years of service.
Jacob agrees. After the week, Laban gives him Rachel to be his wife as well. And Jacob loves Rachel more than Leah, and serves Laban another seven years.
Now the chapter turns to Leah, the wife who is not loved. When the LORD sees that Leah is unloved, he gives her children. But Rachel has no children.
Leah has a son, and calls him Reuben, which sounds like see, a son. She says, surely the LORD hath looked upon my affliction; now therefore my husband will love me. She has another son, and calls him Simeon, which sounds like heard. Because the LORD hath heard that I was hated, he hath therefore given me this son also. She has a third son, and calls him Levi, which means joined. Now this time will my husband be joined unto me.
Each name is a cry for her husband’s love. But with her fourth son, something changes.
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 20. 'And Jacob served seven years for Rachel; and they seemed unto him but a few days, for the love he had to her.' Love makes hard things light. Seven years of hard work flew by, because Jacob’s heart was set on Rachel. That is true in every part of life. When we serve out of love, the work does not feel like a burden. And this is how God wants us to serve him: not as slaves counting the days, but out of love for him.
Verse 25. 'and he said to Laban, What is this thou hast done unto me? did not I serve with thee for Rachel? wherefore then hast thou beguiled me?' Beguiled means deceived. Jacob, the deceiver, had been deceived. He had disguised himself as his brother to fool his blind father. Now a sister was disguised as her sister to fool him. God did not cause Laban’s sin. But Jacob was reaping what he had sown. Sometimes God lets us feel the pain we have caused others, so that we learn, and change.
Verse 35. 'And she conceived again, and bare a son: and she said, Now will I praise the LORD: therefore she called his name Judah; and left bearing.' With her first three sons, Leah hoped that her husband would love her. With Judah, she simply praised the LORD. Her circumstances had not changed. But her heart had. She stopped looking for her worth in her husband’s eyes, and found it in God. And from Judah, the son of praise, would come King David, and in time, Jesus himself.
Matthew begins the family line of Jesus with these words: 'Abraham begat Isaac; and Isaac begat Jacob; and Jacob begat Judas and his brethren'. Judas there is the Greek form of Judah. The unloved wife became the mother of the royal line. And a psalm says, 'When my father and my mother forsake me, then the LORD will take me up.'
Are you looking for your worth in someone else’s approval? Like Leah, turn your eyes to the LORD, who sees you and loves you. And whatever work you have today, ask God to help you do it out of love.
Here is today’s question. Leah stopped seeking her husband’s love and said, now will I praise the LORD. Where do you need to find your worth in God instead of in other people?
Let us pray. Lord God, you see those who are unloved and overlooked, as you saw Leah. Thank you that my worth is found in you. Help me to serve you out of love, and not out of duty. And teach me, when I have wronged others, to learn and to change. Now will I praise the LORD. Amen.
