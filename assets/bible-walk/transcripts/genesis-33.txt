Across the plain, a cloud of dust. Four hundred men are marching toward Jacob’s family. At their head is Esau, the brother Jacob cheated twenty years before. Jacob limps forward alone, bowing to the ground seven times as he goes. And then Esau starts to run.
In chapter thirty-two, Jacob heard that Esau was coming to meet him with four hundred men, and he was greatly afraid. He prayed, I am not worthy of the least of all the mercies. Then, alone by the river Jabbok, he wrestled with a man until the breaking of the day. He would not let go until he was blessed. God gave him a new name, Israel, and Jacob limped away from Peniel as the sun rose.
Now, in chapter thirty-three, comes the moment Jacob has dreaded for twenty years. He must face his brother. Listen for what happens, and for how God has been at work, not only in Jacob, but in Esau too.
Jacob lifts up his eyes, and sees Esau coming, and with him four hundred men. He divides the children among Leah, Rachel and the two maidservants. He puts the maidservants and their children first, then Leah and her children, and Rachel and Joseph last of all. Even now, his favouritism shows.
But this time Jacob does not hide at the back. He goes on ahead of them all, and bows himself to the ground seven times, until he comes near to his brother. In the ancient world, bowing seven times was how a servant approached a great king. Jacob, who stole the blessing that said, be lord over thy brethren, now bows before his brother as his servant.
And then something happens that Jacob never expected.
Esau lifts up his eyes and sees the women and the children. Who are these with thee? Jacob says, the children which God hath graciously given thy servant. The maidservants come near with their children and bow. Then Leah and her children. And last, Joseph and Rachel, and they bow.
Esau asks, what do you mean by all these droves of animals I met? Jacob says, these are to find grace in the sight of my lord. Esau says, I have enough, my brother; keep that thou hast unto thyself.
But Jacob insists.
Jacob says, take, I pray thee, my blessing that is brought to thee; because God hath dealt graciously with me, and because I have enough. The word Jacob uses is blessing. Long ago he took Esau’s blessing. Now, in a way, he gives a blessing back. And Jacob urges him, and Esau takes it.
Esau says, let us take our journey, and I will go before thee. But Jacob says, my lord knows that the children are tender, and the flocks and herds with young are in my care. If they are driven hard for even one day, all the flock will die. Let my lord pass on ahead, and I will lead on slowly, at the pace of the cattle and the children, until I come to my lord in Seir.
Esau offers to leave some of his men with Jacob. But Jacob says, what needeth it? Let me find grace in the sight of my lord. So Esau returns that day on his way to Seir. And Jacob does not follow him to Seir. Perhaps he was still cautious. Instead he journeys to Succoth, and builds himself a house, and makes booths, shelters, for his cattle. The name Succoth means booths.
Then Jacob comes safely to the city of Shechem, in the land of Canaan, on his way from Padan-aram. He buys a piece of a field, where he has pitched his tent, from the children of Hamor, Shechem’s father, for a hundred pieces of money.
Like his grandfather Abraham, Jacob owns a small piece of the promised land. And there, he does what Abraham did when he first arrived in Canaan.
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 4. 'And Esau ran to meet him, and embraced him, and fell on his neck, and kissed him: and they wept.' Jacob had prepared for war. He had sent gifts, divided his camp, and prayed in fear. But Esau ran to him, and embraced him. God had answered Jacob’s prayer, not by giving him a victory, but by changing his brother’s heart. Twenty years of fear melted away in a moment. God can soften hearts that seem hard as stone.
Verse 10. 'And Jacob said, Nay, I pray thee, if now I have found grace in thy sight, then receive my present at my hand: for therefore I have seen thy face, as though I had seen the face of God, and thou wast pleased with me.' Jacob had just seen the face of God at Peniel, and lived. Now he sees the face of his brother, and it is full of welcome instead of anger. Forgiveness on a human face can look like the face of God. When we forgive someone who wronged us, we show them a glimpse of what God is like.
Verse 20. 'And he erected there an altar, and called it El-elohe-Israel.' El-elohe-Israel means God, the God of Israel. Jacob uses his new name. God is no longer just the God of my father Abraham, or the God of Isaac. He is Jacob’s own God. The runaway who once bargained with God at Bethel, if you will do this, then you shall be my God, now builds an altar and declares, he is my God.
Jesus told of a father who saw his returning son a great way off, 'and had compassion, and ran, and fell on his neck, and kissed him.' It is almost the same picture as Esau and Jacob. And Paul wrote, 'be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you.'
Is there someone you have been afraid to face, because of something that happened between you? Pray, as Jacob did, and ask God to prepare both your hearts. Take a humble step toward them. And if someone comes to you seeking peace, run to meet them.
Here is today’s question. Esau ran to meet his brother and embraced him. Is there someone you need to forgive, or ask forgiveness from?
Let us pray. Lord God, thank you that you can change hard hearts, mine as well as others’. Thank you for forgiving me. Help me to forgive others as freely as Esau forgave Jacob, and to take humble steps toward peace. You are my God, the God of Israel. Amen.
