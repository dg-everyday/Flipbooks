Two brothers stand side by side for the last time, at their father’s grave. Then they go their separate ways. One stays in the land of promise. The other takes his wives, his children and his great herds, and moves away to the red mountains in the south. His family will become a nation, with chiefs and kings, long before his brother’s family does.
In chapter thirty-five, God called Jacob back to Bethel. His family put away their strange gods, and God renewed his promise and the name Israel. On the road, Rachel died giving birth to Benjamin. And at last, Isaac died, old and full of days, and Esau and Jacob buried him together.
Now, in chapter thirty-six, before the story turns to Joseph, Genesis pauses to tell us what became of Esau. It is mostly a list of names. But even here, there are lessons about God’s faithfulness, and about choices that shape a family for generations.
The chapter begins with a title, just as Genesis has done before for Adam, Noah, Shem, Terah, Ishmael and Isaac.
Esau took his wives from the daughters of Canaan: Adah, the daughter of Elon the Hittite, and Aholibamah, a granddaughter of Zibeon the Hivite. He also married Bashemath, Ishmael’s daughter. Adah bore Eliphaz. Bashemath bore Reuel. Aholibamah bore Jeush, Jaalam and Korah. These are the sons of Esau, born to him in the land of Canaan.
Then Esau takes his wives, his sons and daughters, all the people of his house, his cattle, his beasts and all his goods, and goes into another country, away from his brother Jacob.
Notice how peaceful this is. Twenty years before, Esau wanted to kill Jacob. Now they part like Abraham and Lot did, simply because the land is too small for both of their flocks. The quarrel has been healed.
Then Genesis lists Esau’s grandchildren, the sons of Eliphaz and Reuel. One of Eliphaz’s sons is Amalek. His descendants, the Amalekites, will later become bitter enemies of Israel in the wilderness. Another of Eliphaz’s sons is Teman. Long after, in the book of Job, one of Job’s friends is called Eliphaz the Temanite.
Esau’s grandsons become dukes, which here means the chiefs of clans. Duke Teman, duke Omar, duke Kenaz, and many more. Edom grows into a nation, organised into tribes.
Genesis also lists the sons of Seir the Horite, the people who already lived in that land before Esau came. Among them is a man named Anah, who found mules in the wilderness, as he fed the donkeys of his father. These little details show that this is a record of real families, remembered and handed down.
Then comes a striking line: 'And these are the kings that reigned in the land of Edom, before there reigned any king over the children of Israel.' Edom has kings long before Israel does. Bela the son of Beor reigns in a city called Dinhabah. After him comes Jobab, then Husham of the land of Temani, then Hadad, who defeats Midian in the field of Moab, and others after them. Each king reigns, and dies, and another takes his place.
The chapter ends with a final list of the dukes of Esau, according to their families, their places and their names. 'he is Esau the father of the Edomites.'
Then, in the very next verse, at the start of chapter thirty-seven, Genesis turns back to Jacob, who lives in the land where his father was a stranger. And the story of Joseph begins.
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 1. 'Now these are the generations of Esau, who is Edom.' Esau was not the son of the promise. And yet God did not forget him. God had promised Rebekah that two nations would come from her, and here is the second nation, just as God said. God had blessed Abraham’s other son Ishmael, and now he blesses Abraham’s grandson Esau. God is faithful to his word, even to those outside the chosen line.
Verse 7. 'For their riches were more than that they might dwell together; and the land wherein they were strangers could not bear them because of their cattle.' Jacob and Esau were both blessed with great wealth. The brothers who once fought over a blessing now had more than the land could hold. And they parted without a quarrel. God had answered Jacob’s prayer at the river Jabbok. The hatred was gone. Reconciliation does not always mean living side by side. Sometimes it means going our separate ways, in peace.
Verse 8. 'Thus dwelt Esau in mount Seir: Esau is Edom.' Edom means red. It reminds us of the red stew for which Esau sold his birthright. Esau settled outside the land God had promised to Abraham. He chose a different place, and his descendants would follow a different path. In time, the Edomites would often stand against Israel. One choice, made in a moment of hunger, shaped the history of a nation.
The little book of Obadiah is a message about Edom, and it warns, 'For thy violence against thy brother Jacob shame shall cover thee'. And yet the prophet Amos looks forward to a day when even 'the remnant of Edom, and of all the heathen' will be called by God’s name. God’s mercy reaches further than we expect.
The choices we make today can shape the lives of people who come after us. Ask God to help you choose well, not for a moment’s gain, but for what lasts. And thank him that his faithfulness reaches even to those we might think are outside his care.
Here is today’s question. Esau’s choices shaped the history of his whole family. What choices are you making now that might affect the people who come after you?
Let us pray. Lord God, you are faithful to every word you speak, and your care reaches further than I can see. Thank you for healing the quarrel between Jacob and Esau. Help me to make choices today that honour you, and that bless those who come after me. Amen.
