A teenage boy walks across the fields toward his brothers, wearing a brightly coloured coat that their father gave him. They see him coming from far away. They know that coat. They hate that coat. And as he comes closer, they begin to whisper. Here comes the dreamer. Let us kill him, and see what becomes of his dreams.
In chapter thirty-six, Genesis told us what became of Esau. He moved to mount Seir, and his family became the nation of Edom, with chiefs and kings. Before that, in chapter thirty-five, Rachel died giving birth to Benjamin, and Isaac died, and Jacob’s twelve sons were listed.
Now, in chapter thirty-seven, begins the last great story of Genesis: the story of Joseph. It will take up most of the rest of the book. It is a story of jealousy, betrayal and suffering. But listen for how God is quietly at work, even when he seems absent.
Jacob lives in the land of Canaan, where his father was a stranger. Joseph, at seventeen years old, is feeding the flock with his brothers, the sons of Bilhah and Zilpah. And Joseph brings their father an evil report of them.
Jacob has learned nothing from the favouritism in his own childhood. He loves Joseph more than all his children.
When his brothers see that their father loves Joseph more than all of them, they hate him, and cannot speak peaceably to him.
Then Joseph has a dream, and he tells it to his brothers, and they hate him even more. Hear this dream, he says. We were binding sheaves in the field, and my sheaf arose and stood upright, and your sheaves stood round about and bowed down to my sheaf. His brothers say, shalt thou indeed reign over us? And they hate him yet the more for his dreams and for his words.
Then he dreams another dream, and tells it. Behold, the sun and the moon and the eleven stars made obeisance to me, bowed down to me. When he tells this to his father, Jacob rebukes him. Shall I and thy mother and thy brethren indeed come to bow down ourselves to thee to the earth? His brothers envy him. 'but his father observed the saying.' Jacob keeps it in mind, and wonders.
Joseph’s brothers go to feed their father’s flock near Shechem. Jacob sends Joseph to see whether it is well with his brothers and with the flocks. Joseph goes, but cannot find them. A man finds him wandering in the field, and tells him they have gone on to Dothan. So Joseph goes after them, and finds them in Dothan.
They see him far off, and even before he comes near, they plot to kill him.
Come now, they say, let us kill him, and throw him into some pit, and we will say, some evil beast has devoured him. And we shall see what will become of his dreams.
But Reuben, the eldest, hears it, and tries to save him. Let us not kill him, he says. Shed no blood. Throw him into this pit in the wilderness, but lay no hand on him. Reuben secretly plans to rescue Joseph and bring him back to his father.
When Joseph comes to his brothers, they strip him of his coat, the coat of many colours. They take him and throw him into a pit. The pit is empty, with no water in it. Then they sit down to eat bread. Years later, they will remember that Joseph pleaded with them from that pit, and they would not listen.
They look up and see a company of Ishmeelites coming from Gilead, with camels carrying spices, balm and myrrh, going down to Egypt. Judah says, what profit is it if we kill our brother and hide his blood? Let us sell him to the Ishmeelites, and not lay our hand on him, for he is our brother and our flesh. And his brothers agree.
Reuben returns to the pit, and Joseph is not there. He tears his clothes, and says to his brothers, the child is not; and I, whither shall I go?
The brothers take Joseph’s coat, kill a young goat, and dip the coat in its blood. They bring it to their father and say, this have we found: know now whether it be thy son’s coat or no. Jacob knows it. It is my son’s coat, he says. An evil beast hath devoured him; Joseph is without doubt rent in pieces. Once, Jacob deceived his father with a young goat and his brother’s clothes. Now his sons deceive him with a young goat and their brother’s coat.
Jacob tears his clothes, puts on sackcloth, and mourns for his son many days. All his sons and daughters rise up to comfort him, but he refuses to be comforted. He says, I will go down into the grave unto my son mourning. Thus his father weeps for him.
Meanwhile, the Midianites sell Joseph in Egypt to Potiphar, an officer of Pharaoh, and captain of the guard.
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 3. 'Now Israel loved Joseph more than all his children, because he was the son of his old age: and he made him a coat of many colours.' The coat was a sign of favour, perhaps even a sign that Joseph would be the heir. Jacob had suffered from his own parents’ favouritism, and yet he did the same thing to his children. Favouritism wounds families. Parents and leaders who love one more than the others plant seeds of jealousy and pain. God calls us to love fairly.
Verse 19. 'And they said one to another, Behold, this dreamer cometh.' The brothers mocked Joseph’s dreams. But the dreams came from God. And the very things the brothers did to stop the dreams were the things God used to fulfil them. By selling Joseph to Egypt, they sent him to the place where they would one day bow before him. No one can stop what God has planned.
Verse 28. 'Then there passed by Midianites merchantmen; and they drew and lifted up Joseph out of the pit, and sold Joseph to the Ishmeelites for twenty pieces of silver: and they brought Joseph into Egypt.' Twenty pieces of silver for a brother. Joseph was betrayed by the people closest to him, sold for silver, and carried away. It looked like the end of his story. But it was only the beginning. Long after, another son, beloved of his father, would be betrayed for pieces of silver. And God would use that betrayal to save the world.
In the book of Acts, Stephen told this story, and said, 'the patriarchs, moved with envy, sold Joseph into Egypt: but God was with him'. And a psalm says of Joseph, 'He sent a man before them, even Joseph, who was sold for a servant'.
Is there jealousy in your heart toward someone who seems more favoured than you? Bring it to God before it grows. And if you have been betrayed or treated unfairly, remember that your story is not over. God is still at work.
Here is today’s question. Joseph’s brothers tried to stop his dreams, but God used even their sin to fulfil his plan. Where do you need to trust that God is still at work in a painful situation?
Let us pray. Lord God, you are at work even when you seem far away. Keep me from jealousy and favouritism. Comfort those who have been betrayed by people close to them. And help me to trust that no one can stop what you have planned. Amen.
