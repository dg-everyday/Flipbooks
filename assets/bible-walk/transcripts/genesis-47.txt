In the grand palace of Egypt, the most powerful king in the world sits on his throne. An old shepherd from Canaan is brought in to meet him, leaning on his staff. Shepherds were looked down on in Egypt. But before the king can speak, the old man lifts his hands, and blesses Pharaoh.
In chapter forty-six, Jacob set out for Egypt with all his family, seventy souls. At Beersheba, God told him, fear not to go down into Egypt; I will go down with thee. Joseph rode out to meet his father in Goshen, and fell on his neck and wept. And Jacob said, now let me die, since I have seen thy face.
Now, in chapter forty-seven, Jacob’s family settles in Egypt. We see a meeting between a shepherd and a king. And we see Joseph’s wisdom as the famine grows worse. The chapter ends with Jacob looking ahead to his death, and to the promised land.
Joseph comes and tells Pharaoh, my father and my brethren, and their flocks and herds, and all that they have, are come out of the land of Canaan, and they are in the land of Goshen. He takes five of his brothers and presents them to Pharaoh.
Pharaoh asks them, what is your occupation? And they answer just as Joseph told them. Thy servants are shepherds, both we, and also our fathers. They ask to live in the land of Goshen, because there is no pasture for their flocks in Canaan, the famine is so severe.
Pharaoh says to Joseph, the land of Egypt is before thee. In the best of the land make thy father and brethren to dwell. In the land of Goshen let them dwell. And if you know any capable men among them, make them rulers over my cattle.
Then Joseph brings in Jacob his father.
Pharaoh asks Jacob, how old art thou? And Jacob gives a surprising answer.
Then Jacob blesses Pharaoh again, and goes out from his presence.
Joseph places his father and his brothers in the best of the land, in the land of Rameses, as Pharaoh commanded.
Meanwhile, the famine grows very severe. There is no bread in all the land, and the people of Egypt and Canaan faint because of the famine. Joseph gathers up all the money in Egypt and in Canaan for the corn they buy, and brings the money into Pharaoh’s house.
When the money runs out, the Egyptians come to Joseph and say, give us bread, for why should we die in thy presence? So Joseph says, give your cattle, and I will give you bread for your cattle. And they bring him their horses, their flocks, their herds and their donkeys, and he feeds them with bread for that year.
When that year ends, they come again. We will not hide from my lord that our money is spent, they say. We have nothing left but our bodies and our lands. Buy us and our land for bread, and we will be servants unto Pharaoh. Give us seed, that we may live, and not die, that the land be not desolate.
So Joseph buys all the land of Egypt for Pharaoh. Then he gives the people seed to sow the land, on one condition. At the harvest, they will give a fifth part to Pharaoh, and keep four parts for themselves, for seed, and for food for their households and their little ones. It was a fair arrangement in a time of disaster.
And the people are grateful.
Israel lives in the land of Egypt, in Goshen. They gain possessions there, and grow, and multiply exceedingly. Jacob lives in Egypt seventeen years.
When the time draws near that Israel must die, he calls his son Joseph. He asks Joseph to swear a solemn oath, in the way it was done in those days, as Abraham’s servant once did. Deal kindly and truly with me, he says. Bury me not, I pray thee, in Egypt. But I will lie with my fathers. Carry me out of Egypt, and bury me in their burying place. Joseph says, I will do as thou hast said. Swear unto me, says Jacob. And Joseph swears. And Israel bows himself upon the bed’s head, and worships God.
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 7. 'And Joseph brought in Jacob his father, and set him before Pharaoh: and Jacob blessed Pharaoh.' Who was the greater man in that room? Pharaoh had wealth, power and armies. Jacob was an old shepherd who needed Pharaoh’s help to survive. But the letter to the Hebrews says that the lesser is blessed by the better. Jacob carried the blessing of God. God had promised Abraham, in thee shall all families of the earth be blessed. Here, Abraham’s grandson blesses the king of Egypt.
Verse 9. 'And Jacob said unto Pharaoh, The days of the years of my pilgrimage are an hundred and thirty years: few and evil have the days of the years of my life been,' Jacob called his life a pilgrimage, a journey toward somewhere else. He was honest about the hard times. Few and evil, he said, meaning short and full of trouble. He had known deceit, exile, grief and fear. But he also knew that this life was not the end. Like Abraham, he was a pilgrim, looking for a better country.
Verse 12. 'And Joseph nourished his father, and his brethren, and all his father’s household, with bread, according to their families.' The brothers who once threw Joseph into a pit and sat down to eat bread now ate bread from his hand. Joseph did not hold back food from those who had wronged him. He cared for every one of them, even the little ones. Joseph’s forgiveness was not just words. It was shown in daily care.
The letter to the Hebrews says that Abraham, Isaac and Jacob confessed 'that they were strangers and pilgrims on the earth.' 'But now they desire a better country, that is, an heavenly'. And Peter wrote to believers, 'I beseech you as strangers and pilgrims'.
Think of your own life as a pilgrimage, a journey toward home with God. Be honest with him about the hard parts of the road. And look for a way to show care to someone in practical ways today, as Joseph nourished his family.
Here is today’s question. Jacob called his life a pilgrimage. How does thinking of your life as a journey toward God change the way you face hard times?
Let us pray. Lord God, thank you that my life is a journey toward you, and that this world is not my final home. Be with me on the hard parts of the road. Help me, like Jacob, to bless others, and like Joseph, to care for those around me, even those who have hurt me. Amen.
