The new temple walls are going up, and the young builders are excited. But a few very old men stand at the back, shaking their heads. They remember the first temple, King Solomon’s temple, shining with gold. Compared to that, this one looks like nothing. And their sadness spreads through the crowd like a cold wind. Then God sends Haggai with a message for the discouraged.
In chapter one, God asked his people, is it time for you to live in your panelled houses, while my house lies in ruins? He told them, consider your ways. The people obeyed. God said, I am with you. And he stirred up their spirit, and they began to build.
Now, in chapter two, Haggai brings three more messages: for builders who feel small, about being clean before God, and for Zerubbabel the governor. Listen for what God promises about this plain little temple.
About a month after the building began, the word of the LORD comes to Haggai again. Speak to Zerubbabel, to Joshua the high priest, and to the rest of the people.
And God asks them a question. 'Who is left among you that saw this house in her first glory? and how do ye see it now? is it not in your eyes in comparison of it as nothing?'
God knew exactly how they felt. In the book of Ezra, we heard that when the foundation was laid, the old men who had seen the first house wept with a loud voice. Now the walls were rising, and there was still no gold, no great king, just a small, poor group of people doing their best.
God does not pretend it is grand. But he will not let them give up.
Then God reminds them, 'so my spirit remaineth among you: fear ye not.' The God who led them out of Egypt was still among them, right there on that building site.
And then God makes a promise that reaches far into the future. 'For thus saith the LORD of hosts; Yet once, it is a little while, and I will shake the heavens, and the earth, and the sea, and the dry land'. And I will shake all nations, and 'the desire of all nations shall come: and I will fill this house with glory'. Christians have understood the desire of all nations in different ways. Many have seen in it a promise of Jesus, the one the whole world needed. Others read it as the treasures of the nations. Either way, God would fill this plain house with glory.
They were worried about gold. But God says, 'The silver is mine, and the gold is mine, saith the LORD of hosts.' He did not need their riches. He wanted their hearts.
And then comes the greatest promise of all.
Two months later, Haggai asks the priests a question about the law. If someone carries holy meat from an offering in his clothes, and his clothes touch some bread, does the bread become holy? The priests answer, no. And if someone who is unclean touches the bread, does it become unclean? Yes, they say, it does.
Holiness does not rub off by touching, but uncleanness does. Haggai explains. 'So is this people, and so is this nation before me, saith the LORD; and so is every work of their hands'. Being near the holy place had not made them holy. While their hearts had been far from God, everything they did was spoiled.
God tells them to look back. Before a single stone was laid, a heap of grain that should have held twenty measures held only ten. God sent blight and mildew and hail. 'yet ye turned not to me, saith the LORD.'
But now they had turned. So God says, consider from this day, the day the foundation of the temple was laid. And here is the promise.
That same day, Haggai brings a last message, for Zerubbabel. God says, I will shake the heavens and the earth, and overturn the thrones of kingdoms. Then God says, 'will I take thee, O Zerubbabel, my servant, the son of Shealtiel, saith the LORD, and will make thee as a signet: for I have chosen thee'.
A signet was a king’s seal ring, pressed into wax to mark a letter with his authority. Long before, through Jeremiah, God had said that even if king Jehoiachin, here called Coniah, were 'the signet upon my right hand, yet would I pluck thee thence'. Now God puts the signet back on, in Jehoiachin’s grandson. David’s family had not been forgotten. Christians have understood this promise in different ways, but many see it pointing beyond Zerubbabel. In Matthew chapter one, in the family line of Jesus, we read, 'and Salathiel begat Zorobabel'. That is Zerubbabel.
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 4. 'Yet now be strong, O Zerubbabel, saith the LORD; and be strong, O Joshua, son of Josedech, the high priest; and be strong, all ye people of the land, saith the LORD, and work: for I am with you, saith the LORD of hosts:' Three times God says, be strong. To the governor, to the high priest, and to all the people. No one was left out. Then he says, and work. Feeling small is not a reason to stop. And the reason they can be strong is not their own strength. It is this. I am with you.
Verse 9. 'The glory of this latter house shall be greater than of the former, saith the LORD of hosts: and in this place will I give peace, saith the LORD of hosts.' The latter house means this new one. How could this plain temple ever be more glorious than Solomon’s? Many years later, Mary and Joseph carried the baby Jesus into this same temple, and old Simeon took him in his arms and blessed God. The Prince of Peace himself came into that house. No gold could match that glory.
Verse 19. 'Is the seed yet in the barn? yea, as yet the vine, and the fig tree, and the pomegranate, and the olive tree, hath not brought forth: from this day will I bless you.' God promised to bless them before there was anything to see. The harvest had not come. The trees were bare. But God said, from this day. The day they turned back to him and began to build was the day the blessing began. They would have to trust his word, and wait for the fruit.
The prophet Zechariah, who preached alongside Haggai, asked, 'For who hath despised the day of small things?' And the letter to the Hebrews quotes Haggai’s promise, 'Yet once more I shake not the earth only, but also heaven.'
Do you ever feel that what you do for God is small? God sees it. He says to you, be strong, and work, for I am with you. Do the next small thing faithfully, and trust God to fill it with his glory.
Here is today’s question. The old men thought the new temple was nothing compared to the old one. When have you felt that what you could do for God was too small to matter?
Let us pray. Lord God, thank you that you do not despise the day of small things. When I feel weak or discouraged, help me to be strong, and to keep working, because you are with me. Thank you for sending Jesus, the Prince of Peace. Fill my small life with your glory. Amen.
That is the end of our walk through the book of Haggai. Well walked. You have finished another whole book of the Bible. Next on the walk, a big step: the Gospel of Matthew. It begins with the family line of Jesus, and in it you will find a name you now know well. Zorobabel, the governor Haggai called God’s signet.
