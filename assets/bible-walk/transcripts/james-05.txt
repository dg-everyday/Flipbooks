A farmer walks out to his field, and looks at the bare brown earth. He has ploughed it, and sown his seed. Now there is nothing more he can do but wait. He looks up at the sky. He cannot make the rain come. But he knows the rains will come, as they always do. So he waits, patiently, and he does not give up. James says that is how we are to wait for the Lord.
In chapter four, James asked where our quarrels come from, and found the answer in our selfish desires. He told us that God resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble. And he gave us a beautiful promise: draw nigh to God, and he will draw nigh to you. Our lives are like a vapour, here for a little time, so we say, if the Lord will.
Now we come to the last chapter of the letter. Listen for patience. And listen for prayer, from the beginning of this chapter to the end.
James begins with strong words, like one of the prophets of old. He speaks to rich people who have grown rich by treating others unfairly. Weep, he says, because trouble is coming. Your riches have rotted, and your clothes are eaten by moths. Your gold and silver have rusted, and the rust will be a witness against you.
And then he says why. 'Behold, the hire of the labourers who have reaped down your fields, which is of you kept back by fraud, crieth'. The hire means the wages. These rich men had not paid the workers who brought in their harvest. And James says, 'the cries of them which have reaped are entered into the ears of the Lord'. The rich may not have listened. But God heard every cry.
Then James turns to the believers who are suffering, and speaks gently.
He says it again. 'Be ye also patient; stablish your hearts: for the coming of the Lord draweth nigh.' Stablish means to make strong and steady. Hold firm. The Lord is coming.
And while you wait, do not turn on each other. 'Grudge not one against another, brethren, lest ye be condemned: behold, the judge standeth before the door.' When times are hard, it is easy to grumble about one another. But the Judge is close, and he sees.
Then James points back to the prophets, who spoke for God and suffered for it, as an example of patience. And to one man in particular.
Then James repeats something Jesus taught. 'But above all things, my brethren, swear not, neither by heaven, neither by the earth, neither by any other oath: but let your yea be yea; and your nay, nay'. Yea means yes, and nay means no. Be so honest that people do not need you to make a promise or an oath. When you say yes, mean yes. When you say no, mean no.
And now James turns to prayer, and he does not stop until the end of his letter. 'Is any among you afflicted? let him pray. Is any merry? let him sing psalms.' In sorrow, pray. In happiness, sing praise. Whatever is happening, bring it to God.
Is anyone sick? James says, call for the elders of the church, and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord. 'And the prayer of faith shall save the sick, and the Lord shall raise him up'. Christians have understood and practised these verses in different ways. But all agree on this: when someone is ill, we do not have to face it alone. We pray for one another, and we trust the Lord who heals.
Then James gives us a great example of prayer. 'Elias was a man subject to like passions as we are'. Elias is another form of the name Elijah. Like passions means the same feelings as us. Elijah was an ordinary person, with fears and weaknesses like ours. Yet 'he prayed earnestly that it might not rain: and it rained not on the earth by the space of three years and six months. And he prayed again, and the heaven gave rain'. God heard the prayers of an ordinary man. And he hears ours.
James ends with one last call to care for one another. If any of you wanders from the truth, and someone brings him back, know this. Whoever turns a sinner back from the wrong way will save a soul from death, and cover a multitude of sins.
From the first chapter to the last, James has called us to a living faith. A faith that rejoices in trials, asks for wisdom, does God’s Word, cares for the poor, guards the tongue, waits patiently, and prays.
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 7. 'Be patient therefore, brethren, unto the coming of the Lord. Behold, the husbandman waiteth for the precious fruit of the earth, and hath long patience for it, until he receive the early and latter rain.' A husbandman is a farmer. In that land, the early rain came at sowing time, and the latter rain came before the harvest. The farmer could not hurry the rain. But he trusted that it would come. When we are suffering, and waiting, and it seems God is slow, James says, be patient like the farmer. The harvest is coming. The Lord is coming.
Verse 11. 'Behold, we count them happy which endure. Ye have heard of the patience of Job, and have seen the end of the Lord; that the Lord is very pitiful, and of tender mercy.' Job lost almost everything, and did not understand why. But he held on to God. And the end of the Lord means how the Lord brought it all to an end, in blessing. Very pitiful does not mean pitiful as we use it today. It means full of pity, full of compassion. However long the trial, the Lord is kind, and his mercy is tender.
Verse 16. 'Confess your faults one to another, and pray one for another, that ye may be healed. The effectual fervent prayer of a righteous man availeth much.' Effectual means working, getting things done. Fervent means heartfelt and earnest. Availeth much means it achieves a great deal. Prayer is not a small thing. When we are honest with one another, and pray for one another with all our hearts, God works. Our prayers are powerful, not because of us, but because of the God who hears them.
Jesus told a story to show 'that men ought always to pray, and not to faint.' And Paul wrote, 'Be careful for nothing; but in every thing by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known unto God.'
Who could you pray for today? Someone who is sick, someone who is struggling, or someone who has wandered away from God. Pray for them by name, earnestly, from your heart. And if you are waiting on God for something, ask him for the patience of the farmer.
Here is today’s question. James says that the earnest prayer of a righteous person achieves a great deal. Who will you pray for, faithfully, this week, and what will you ask God to do?
Let us pray. Lord God, thank you for walking with me through the letter of James. Thank you that you are full of compassion and tender mercy. Give me patience while I wait, honesty in my words, and a heart that prays about everything. Help me to care for others, and to live a faith that is alive. Amen.
