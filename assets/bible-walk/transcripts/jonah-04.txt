A man sits alone on a hillside outside a great city, in the blazing sun. He is waiting, hoping, to watch the city be destroyed. God has just saved over a hundred thousand people. And this man is so angry about it that he says he would rather not live. He is not an enemy of God. He is God’s own prophet.
In chapter three, the word of the LORD came to Jonah the second time, and this time he went to Nineveh. He cried, yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown. The people believed God, and from the king to the least of them, they put on sackcloth and turned from their evil ways. And God saw their works, and spared the city.
Now we come to the last chapter of Jonah. It is the most surprising chapter of all. We find out at last why Jonah ran away. And the book ends, not with an answer, but with a question from God.
When God spares Nineveh, it displeases Jonah exceedingly, and he is very angry.
And Jonah prays. But it is a very different prayer from the one he prayed inside the fish.
Now we know why Jonah ran away. It was not fear. Jonah was not afraid that Nineveh would hurt him. He was afraid that God would forgive them. Nineveh was the enemy of his people. Jonah wanted them punished. And he knew God well enough to know that if they turned, God would show them mercy.
Jonah is so upset that he says, therefore now, O LORD, take, I beseech thee, my life from me; for it is better for me to die than to live. Jonah is in a dark place. His anger has turned to despair. And God does not shout at him, or leave him. God answers him gently, with a question.
Jonah does not answer. Instead, he goes out of the city, and sits on the east side of it. He makes himself a little shelter, and sits in its shade, to see what will happen to the city. Perhaps he still hopes God will change his mind and destroy it.
Then God begins to teach Jonah, in a way he could understand.
The LORD God prepares a gourd, a fast growing plant with big leaves, and makes it grow up over Jonah, to shade his head, and to ease his misery. And Jonah is exceeding glad of the gourd. It is the first time in the whole book that Jonah is happy.
But the next day, as the sun rises, God prepares a worm. It chews the plant, and the plant withers away.
And when the sun is up, God prepares a vehement east wind, a scorching wind from the desert. The sun beats down on Jonah’s head, until he feels faint. And again he wishes he could die. He says, it is better for me to die than to live.
Notice the word prepared, again and again. God prepared a fish. God prepared a gourd. God prepared a worm. God prepared a wind. God is in charge of every detail, and he is using all of them to teach one stubborn prophet.
God says to Jonah, doest thou well to be angry for the gourd? Is it right for you to be angry about the plant?
Jonah says, 'I do well to be angry, even unto death.'
Then the LORD gives him the lesson. 'Thou hast had pity on the gourd, for the which thou hast not laboured, neither madest it grow; which came up in a night, and perished in a night:' You did not plant it, or water it, or make it grow. It was here one day and gone the next. And yet you cared about it.
And then God asks his last question.
And that is how the book ends. With a question. We never hear Jonah’s answer. The book leaves the question hanging in the air, for Jonah, and for every reader since. Will you share God’s heart for people? Even people you do not like? Even your enemies?
Jonah cared more about a plant that gave him shade than about a city full of people. It is easy to look down on Jonah. But it is worth asking ourselves honestly: what do I care about most? My own comfort? Or the people God loves?
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 2. 'And he prayed unto the LORD, and said, I pray thee, O LORD, was not this my saying, when I was yet in my country? Therefore I fled before unto Tarshish: for I knew that thou art a gracious God, and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness, and repentest thee of the evil.' Here is one of the most beautiful descriptions of God in the whole Bible. Gracious. Merciful. Slow to anger. Of great kindness. These words go all the way back to when God showed his glory to Moses on the mountain. And Jonah throws them back at God as a complaint. The very thing that should have filled him with joy made him angry. But this is good news for us. God really is like that, to Nineveh, to Jonah, and to you.
Verse 4. 'Then said the LORD, Doest thou well to be angry?' God could have struck Jonah down for his rebellion. Instead, he asks him a question. God is patient with Jonah, just as he was patient with Nineveh. He does not force Jonah to change. He invites him to think. When we are angry with God, he does not turn away from us. He asks us, gently, to look at our own hearts.
Verse 11. 'And should not I spare Nineveh, that great city, wherein are more than sixscore thousand persons that cannot discern between their right hand and their left hand; and also much cattle?' Sixscore thousand is a hundred and twenty thousand people. Some think this means young children, too young to know right from left. Others think it means all the people, who did not know God’s ways. Either way, God sees them. He cares about every one of them, and even about the animals. God’s heart is far bigger than Jonah’s. And far bigger than ours.
Moses heard God proclaim his name: 'The LORD, The LORD God, merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abundant in goodness and truth'. And Jesus said, 'Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you'.
Is there a person, or a group of people, you would secretly rather God did not bless? Be honest with God about it, as Jonah was. Then ask him to give you his heart for them. Pray for them by name today.
Here is today’s question. The book of Jonah ends with God’s question: should not I spare Nineveh? How would you answer God, if he asked you that question about the people you find hardest to love?
Let us pray. Lord God, thank you that you are gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness. Thank you for your patience with me, even when my heart is small. Forgive me for caring more about my own comfort than about the people you love. Give me your heart, even for my enemies. Amen.
