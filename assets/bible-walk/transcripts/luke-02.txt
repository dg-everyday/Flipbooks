It is the busiest time anyone in Bethlehem can remember. Travellers have crowded into the little town from everywhere, and every room is taken. A young couple arrives, tired from the long road, and she is about to give birth. There is no room for them. So the Son of the Highest, the King promised to David, is born in a place where animals are kept.
In chapter one, the angel Gabriel promised a son to old Zacharias and Elisabeth, and they named him John. Then Gabriel came to Mary in Nazareth and told her she would have a son, Jesus, who would reign on David’s throne for ever. Mary answered, be it unto me according to thy word.
Now, in chapter two, the promise is born. Luke, the careful historian, begins with the name of the Roman emperor. And then he takes us from the palace of Caesar to a manger in Bethlehem, to shepherds in a field, to two old people in the temple, and to a boy of twelve asking questions.
The Roman emperor, Caesar Augustus, orders a census of the whole empire. Everyone must go to his own city to be registered. So Joseph travels from Nazareth, in Galilee, to Bethlehem, the city of David, because he belongs to David’s family. Mary goes with him, and she is expecting her child.
While they are in Bethlehem, the time comes for the baby to be born. 'And she brought forth her firstborn son, and wrapped him in swaddling clothes, and laid him in a manger; because there was no room for them in the inn.' Swaddling clothes were strips of cloth wrapped snugly around a newborn. A manger is a feeding trough for animals.
Out in the fields nearby, shepherds are watching over their flocks. Suddenly an angel of the Lord stands before them, the glory of the Lord shines around them, and they are terrified. The angel says, 'Fear not: for, behold, I bring you good tidings of great joy, which shall be to all people.' And he gives them a sign: you will find the baby wrapped in swaddling clothes, lying in a manger. Then a great crowd of angels appears, praising God: 'Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace, good will toward men.'
The shepherds hurry to Bethlehem and find Mary and Joseph, and the baby lying in the manger, just as they were told. They tell everyone what the angel said, and all who hear it wonder. 'But Mary kept all these things, and pondered them in her heart.' The shepherds go back, glorifying and praising God.
When he is eight days old, the baby is named Jesus, the name the angel gave. Later, Mary and Joseph bring him to the temple in Jerusalem, and offer the gift the law asked of poor families: a pair of turtledoves or two young pigeons. There an old man named Simeon, who has been waiting for the comfort of Israel, takes the baby in his arms and blesses God. He has been promised that he would not die before he saw the Lord’s Christ. 'Lord, now lettest thou thy servant depart in peace, according to thy word'. Then he tells Mary that this child will be a sign that many will speak against, 'Yea, a sword shall pierce through thy own soul also'. An old widow named Anna, who served God in the temple with fasting and prayers, gives thanks too, and speaks of him to everyone waiting for redemption.
The chapter ends when Jesus is twelve. His family goes up to Jerusalem for the Passover, and on the way home they realise he is missing. After three days of searching they find him in the temple, sitting among the teachers, listening and asking questions. Everyone is amazed at his understanding. His mother says, your father and I have been looking for you, anxious and sorrowing. And Jesus answers her. Then he goes home with them to Nazareth, and is obedient to them, and he grows in wisdom and stature, and in favour with God and man.
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 11. 'For unto you is born this day in the city of David a Saviour, which is Christ the Lord.' Three titles for one baby. Saviour: the one who rescues. Christ: the anointed King God promised to David. Lord: God himself. And notice the words, unto you. The angel did not bring news about someone far away. The Saviour was born for those shepherds, and for you.
Verse 30. 'For mine eyes have seen thy salvation,' Simeon had waited his whole life. And when he finally held the baby, he did not say, I have seen a child. He said, I have seen thy salvation. God’s rescue was not a plan or an idea. It was a person, small enough to hold in an old man’s arms. And Simeon says this salvation is a light for the Gentiles, the nations, as well as the glory of Israel. Luke wants his readers from every nation to know: this is for you too.
Verse 49. 'And he said unto them, How is it that ye sought me? wist ye not that I must be about my Father’s business?' Wist means knew. These are the first words of Jesus in Luke’s Gospel. Mary had said, your father and I. Jesus gently points to another Father. Even at twelve, he knew who he was and why he had come. And yet he went home and obeyed his earthly parents. The Son of God was also a faithful son in an ordinary home.
Long before, the prophet Micah had written, 'But thou, Bethlehem Ephratah, though thou be little among the thousands of Judah, yet out of thee shall he come forth unto me that is to be ruler in Israel'. Caesar thought he was simply counting his people. But his decree brought Mary and Joseph to the very town where God had promised the King would be born.
God did not send the news of his Son to the powerful first, but to shepherds. Whoever you are, it is for you. Like Mary, take some time today to ponder what God has done. And like the shepherds, tell someone.
Here is today’s question. Simeon held Jesus and said, mine eyes have seen thy salvation. How have you seen God’s salvation in your own life?
Let us pray. Glory to God in the highest. Thank you, Father, for sending your Son, born in a manger, to be my Saviour, Christ the Lord. Like Mary, help me to treasure what you have done, and like the shepherds, to tell others the good tidings. Amen.
