A rich man has prepared a great banquet. The food is ready, the tables are set, and he sends his servant to tell the invited guests, come, for all things are now ready. But one by one they make excuses. I have bought a field. I have bought five pairs of oxen. I have just got married. Not one of them comes. So the master of the house does something astonishing.
In chapter thirteen, Jesus told the parable of the patient gardener, set free a woman bent over for eighteen years, and wept over Jerusalem. He is still on the road to the city.
Now, in chapter fourteen, he is a guest at a dinner in the house of a leading Pharisee, on the sabbath. Meals in those days showed who mattered and who did not, by who was invited and where they sat. Jesus uses this dinner to teach about humility, about who is welcome at God’s table, and about what it costs to follow him.
The Pharisees are watching Jesus closely. In front of him is a man suffering from dropsy, a sickness that made the body swell with water. Jesus asks the lawyers and Pharisees, is it lawful to heal on the sabbath? They say nothing. So Jesus takes the man, heals him, and lets him go. Then he asks, which of you, if your donkey or your ox fell into a pit, would not pull him out at once on the sabbath day? They cannot answer.
Then Jesus notices how the guests are choosing the best seats, near the host. So he gives them some advice. When you are invited to a wedding, do not sit in the place of honour, in case someone more important arrives and the host says, give this man your seat, and you have to move down in shame. Instead, sit in the lowest place. Then the host may come and say, 'Friend, go up higher'.
Then Jesus speaks to the host himself. When you give a dinner, do not invite your friends, your relatives or your rich neighbours, who will invite you back. Invite the poor, the maimed, the lame and the blind. They cannot repay you, and you will be blessed, 'for thou shalt be recompensed at the resurrection of the just.'
One of the guests says, 'Blessed is he that shall eat bread in the kingdom of God.' And Jesus answers with the story of the great supper. When the invited guests make their excuses, the master is angry. He tells his servant, 'Go out quickly into the streets and lanes of the city, and bring in hither the poor, and the maimed, and the halt, and the blind.' The servant does it, and comes back: Lord, there is still room. Then the master says, go out further still. For none of those who were invited and refused will taste my supper.
Great crowds are travelling with Jesus. He turns and speaks to them plainly about the cost of following him. If anyone comes to me, he says, and does not love me more than his own family, more even than his own life, he cannot be my disciple. Jesus uses strong words to show how great the difference must be. And whoever does not carry his cross and come after me cannot be my disciple.
He gives two pictures. A builder who does not count the cost first may lay a foundation and be unable to finish, and everyone will mock him: this man began to build, and was not able to finish. And a king going to war against another king will first sit down and consider whether his ten thousand soldiers can face twenty thousand. If not, he will seek peace. In the same way, Jesus says, whoever does not forsake all that he has cannot be my disciple.
And he ends with salt. Salt is good. But if it loses its saltiness, it is useless, fit only to be thrown out. 'He that hath ears to hear, let him hear.'
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 11. 'For whosoever exalteth himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted.' To exalt is to lift up, and to be abased is to be brought low. In God’s kingdom, the way up is down. Those who push themselves forward will be put in their place, and those who take the lowest place will be lifted up by God. Jesus himself is the greatest example. He humbled himself all the way to the cross, and God has lifted him above every name.
Verse 23. 'And the lord said unto the servant, Go out into the highways and hedges, and compel them to come in, that my house may be filled.' Compel here means urge strongly, plead with them, the way you might insist that a shy guest come in out of the cold. It does not mean force. The invited guests had refused, so the master’s invitation went out further and further, to the poor in the city and then to strangers on the roads. God wants his house full. And that invitation has reached you.
Verse 28. 'For which of you, intending to build a tower, sitteth not down first, and counteth the cost, whether he have sufficient to finish it?' Jesus never hid the cost of following him. He did not want followers who would start with excitement and give up when it got hard. Count the cost, he says. Following Jesus may cost us comfort, pride and plans. But the cost of not following him is far greater.
Long before, the book of Proverbs gave the same advice about the place of honour: 'For better it is that it be said unto thee, Come up hither; than that thou shouldest be put lower in the presence of the prince'. Jesus took that wisdom and showed that it is not just good manners. It is the way God’s kingdom works.
Look for the lowest place today: a way to serve without being noticed, or to put someone else first. And think of someone who is usually left out. How could you welcome them, the way God has welcomed you?
Here is today’s question. Jesus said, he that humbleth himself shall be exalted. Where will you take the lower place today, for someone else’s sake?
Let us pray. Lord Jesus, you humbled yourself for me. Teach me to take the lower place, to welcome those who cannot repay me, and to count the cost of following you gladly. Thank you that your invitation reached even me. Amen.
