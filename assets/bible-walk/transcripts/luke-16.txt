Outside the gate of a rich man’s house lies a beggar named Lazarus, covered in sores. Every day the rich man walks past him in his fine purple clothes, on his way to another feast. Lazarus would be glad of the crumbs that fall from the rich man’s table. The only ones who come near him are the street dogs, licking his sores. Then both men die, and everything is turned upside down.
In chapter fifteen, Jesus told three stories of things lost and found: a sheep, a coin and a son, and showed the Father’s joy over every sinner who comes home. Now, in chapter sixteen, he turns to money. Luke tells us that the Pharisees who were listening 'were covetous', that is, they loved money. Jesus tells two stories that both begin with the words, 'There was a certain rich man'.
The first story is a puzzling one. A rich man has a steward, a manager who looks after his business. He hears that the steward has been wasting his goods, so he calls him in: give an account of your work, for you can no longer be my steward.
The steward thinks, what shall I do? I am not strong enough to dig, and I am ashamed to beg. Then he has an idea. He calls in his master’s debtors one by one. How much do you owe? A hundred measures of oil. Take your bill, sit down quickly, and write fifty. And you? A hundred measures of wheat. Write eighty. He makes friends for himself, so that when he loses his job, they will welcome him into their homes.
And the master praises the dishonest steward, not for his dishonesty, but because he acted shrewdly. Jesus says, 'the children of this world are in their generation wiser than the children of light.' People who live for this world often plan more carefully for their future than God’s people plan for eternity. Use your money, Jesus says, to make friends who will welcome you into everlasting homes. Use what you have now for what lasts for ever.
Then he speaks plainly about faithfulness and about masters. The Pharisees, who loved money, hear all this and mock him. Jesus says, you justify yourselves before men, but God knows your hearts. 'for that which is highly esteemed among men is abomination in the sight of God.'
Then comes the second story. There was a certain rich man, dressed in purple and fine linen, who feasted in luxury every day. At his gate lay the beggar Lazarus.
The beggar dies, and is carried by the angels to Abraham’s side. The rich man also dies, and is buried. And in torment, he looks up and sees Abraham far away, and Lazarus beside him. He cries out, 'Father Abraham, have mercy on me, and send Lazarus, that he may dip the tip of his finger in water, and cool my tongue'.
But Abraham answers, 'Son, remember that thou in thy lifetime receivedst thy good things, and likewise Lazarus evil things: but now he is comforted, and thou art tormented.' And between us and you there is a great gulf fixed, so that no one can cross over.
Then the rich man begs, send Lazarus to my father’s house, for I have five brothers. Let him warn them, so they do not come to this place. Abraham says, 'They have Moses and the prophets; let them hear them.' The rich man says, no, father Abraham, but if someone went to them from the dead, they would repent. And Abraham answers him with the last words of the story.
Jesus tells this as a story, and Christians have understood its details in different ways. But its warning is plain. The rich man was not condemned for being rich. He was condemned because he lived only for himself, and walked past the needy man at his own gate every day without seeing him.
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 10. 'He that is faithful in that which is least is faithful also in much: and he that is unjust in the least is unjust also in much.' How we handle small things shows who we really are. Small amounts of money, small promises, small tasks no one notices: these are where faithfulness is built. If we cannot be trusted with little, we will not be trusted with much. And money is one of the smallest things God gives us to look after.
Verse 13. 'No servant can serve two masters: for either he will hate the one, and love the other; or else he will hold to the one, and despise the other. Ye cannot serve God and mammon.' Mammon means money and possessions, treated almost like a god. Jesus does not say we should not have money. He says money makes a terrible master. Something will be first in our hearts. It cannot be both God and money. Whichever we serve will shape every choice we make.
Verse 31. 'And he said unto him, If they hear not Moses and the prophets, neither will they be persuaded, though one rose from the dead.' This is one of the most solemn sentences in the Gospels. The rich man thought a miracle would change his brothers. But Abraham said they already had God’s word. Soon after, someone did rise from the dead: Jesus himself. And still many would not believe. The problem was never a lack of evidence. It was a heart that would not listen.
Long before, the book of Proverbs warned, 'Whoso stoppeth his ears at the cry of the poor, he also shall cry himself, but shall not be heard.' The rich man stopped his ears to Lazarus at his gate. And then he cried out himself. Moses and the prophets had been saying the same thing all along: love God, and care for the poor.
Who is the Lazarus at your gate, someone in need that you pass every day without really seeing? Do one thing for them. And look honestly at your money. Is it your servant, or your master?
Here is today’s question. Jesus said, ye cannot serve God and mammon. What is one way you will show today that God, not money, is your master?
Let us pray. Lord, you know my heart. Forgive me for serving money and comfort, and for walking past people in need. Make me faithful in little things, generous with what you have given me, and quick to hear your word while there is still time. Amen.
