At the end of a long day, a father stands at his children’s bedroom door. Before they fall asleep, he speaks a blessing over them. The same words, every time. Words they will remember all their lives. In Numbers chapter six, God gives Aaron words like that, to speak over the whole people of Israel. And people have been speaking them over one another ever since.
In chapter five, God told his people to keep the camp clean, because, he said, in the midst whereof I dwell. When anyone wronged another, they were to confess their sin, repay what they had taken, and add a fifth. And a difficult law brought a husband’s suspicion before the LORD, so that God would judge, not the husband.
Now, in chapter six, we hear about people who choose to give themselves to God in a special way. And then we hear the blessing God wanted spoken over all his people. Listen for the three parts of the blessing.
The LORD speaks to Moses about the Nazarite.
Nazarite comes from a Hebrew word meaning separated, or set apart. Notice, either man or woman. Anyone in Israel could make this vow, for a time, to give themselves to God in a special way.
The Nazarite made three promises. First, no wine or strong drink, and nothing at all from the grapevine, not even grapes or raisins. Second, no razor would touch their head. They would let their hair grow long, as a sign to everyone that they were set apart for God. And third, they would not go near a dead body, not even for the funeral of their own father or mother.
God says, all the days of his separation he is holy unto the LORD.
If someone died suddenly beside the Nazarite, the vow was broken. Then the Nazarite had to shave their head, bring offerings, and start the vow all over again from the beginning.
When the time of the vow was finished, the Nazarite came to the door of the tabernacle with offerings. They shaved their head, and put the hair on the fire under the peace offering. And after that, they could drink wine again.
We have met Nazarites already on our walk. Samson was a Nazarite from his birth, though he treated his vow carelessly. Samuel’s mother Hannah promised that no razor would come on his head. And in the New Testament, the angel said that John the Baptist would drink neither wine nor strong drink. These were people set apart for God.
Then the chapter turns to something beautiful. The LORD speaks to Moses, and says, tell Aaron and his sons, this is how you shall bless the children of Israel.
And God gives them the words.
And then God adds a promise. 'And they shall put my name upon the children of Israel; and I will bless them.' The priests spoke the words, but it was God who blessed. The blessing did not depend on how good the priest was, or how good the people were. It came from God’s own generous heart, and it carried his name.
Think about what this blessing meant. The priests did not make up their own words. God himself gave them the blessing. He wanted to bless his people. He was not reluctant or grudging. He told the priests exactly what to say, so that his people would hear, again and again, that God was for them.
The blessing has three lines, and each one names the LORD. Each line has two parts. And each line grows a little longer, as if the blessing is getting bigger and fuller as it goes.
Ever since, God’s people have spoken these words over one another. At the end of worship. Over children. Over the sick. Over those setting out on a journey. Christians all over the world still use them today.
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 24. 'The LORD bless thee, and keep thee:' To bless means to give good things, and to show favour. To keep means to guard and protect, like a shepherd keeping his sheep. Notice that the word thee is singular. The blessing was spoken over the whole people, but each person could hear it as spoken to them. The LORD bless you, personally, and keep you safe.
Verse 25. 'The LORD make his face shine upon thee, and be gracious unto thee:' When someone’s face shines on you, they look at you with delight, the way a parent’s face lights up when they see their child. God’s face shining on us means his loving attention. And to be gracious means to give us kindness we have not earned. God looks at his people with joy, and treats them with grace.
Verse 26. 'The LORD lift up his countenance upon thee, and give thee peace.' Countenance means face. To lift up his face toward us means God turns toward us, and pays attention. And then, the last gift, peace. The Hebrew word is shalom. It means more than no fighting. It means wholeness, wellbeing, everything as it should be. God’s blessing ends with peace.
Paul wrote, 'Now the God of peace be with you all.' And he blessed the church with these words: 'The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, and the love of God, and the communion of the Holy Ghost, be with you all.'
Speak this blessing over someone today. A child, a friend, someone who is struggling. Say the words slowly: the LORD bless thee, and keep thee. And receive them for yourself too. God’s face shines on you.
Here is today’s question. God himself gave the priests the words to bless his people. What does it tell you about God’s heart, that he wanted his people to hear this blessing again and again?
Let us pray. The LORD bless me, and keep me. The LORD make his face shine upon me, and be gracious unto me. The LORD lift up his countenance upon me, and give me peace. Thank you, Lord, that you delight to bless your people. Help me to bless others too. Amen.
