Imagine a great ceremony that lasts twelve days. Each day, a different leader steps forward with exactly the same gift as the day before. A silver dish. A silver bowl. A golden spoon full of incense. Animals for offerings. Twelve times over, the same gift, described in full every time. It is the longest chapter in the books of Moses. Why would God want it written down this way?
In chapter six, we heard about the Nazarite, man or woman, set apart for God, with no wine, no razor, and no dead body. And God gave Aaron the blessing for the people: the LORD bless thee, and keep thee, the LORD make his face shine upon thee, the LORD lift up his countenance upon thee, and give thee peace.
Now, in chapter seven, the leaders of Israel bring gifts for the tabernacle. Listen for how the gifts are shared out. And listen for what happens at the very end of the chapter.
On the day Moses finished setting up the tabernacle, and anointed it and made it holy, along with all its things, the leaders of Israel bring an offering. These were the princes, the heads of the tribes, the same men who helped Moses count the people.
First they bring six covered wagons and twelve oxen, one wagon for every two leaders, and an ox from each. And they bring them before the tabernacle.
The LORD tells Moses, take these, and give them to the Levites, to each according to their service.
So Moses gives two wagons and four oxen to the family of Gershon, who carry the curtains and coverings. And four wagons and eight oxen to the family of Merari, who carry the heavy boards and pillars.
But to the family of Kohath he gives none.
The heaviest loads got the most wagons. That was fair and sensible. But the holiest things were not to go on a cart at all. They were to be carried by hand, on shoulders, with reverence. Remember, when David tried to bring the ark to Jerusalem on a new cart, it ended in tragedy. God had already said, long before, carry it on your shoulders.
Then the leaders bring offerings for the dedication of the altar. And the LORD gives Moses an instruction.
So, day after day, for twelve days, the leaders come one at a time.
On the first day comes Nahshon, the son of Amminadab, from the tribe of Judah. His offering is a silver dish, weighing a hundred and thirty shekels, and a silver bowl of seventy shekels, both full of fine flour mixed with oil. A golden spoon of ten shekels, full of incense. A young bull, a ram and a lamb for a burnt offering. A young goat for a sin offering. And for peace offerings, two oxen, five rams, five goats and five lambs.
On the second day comes Nethaneel of Issachar, with exactly the same gift. On the third day, Eliab of Zebulun, with the same gift. Then Elizur of Reuben. Shelumiel of Simeon. Eliasaph of Gad. Elishama of Ephraim. Gamaliel of Manasseh. Abidan of Benjamin. Ahiezer of Dan. Pagiel of Asher. And on the twelfth day, Ahira of Naphtali.
Every time, the writer describes the whole gift in full, the dish, the bowl, the spoon, the animals, as if it were the first time.
Why? Because each gift mattered to God. No tribe’s offering was just one more of the same. The largest tribe and the smallest tribe gave the same gift, and received the same honour. God noticed each one, and recorded each one, by name.
Then the writer adds it all up. Twelve silver dishes, twelve silver bowls, twelve golden spoons. Many animals for the offerings. This was the dedication of the altar.
And then comes the last verse of the chapter. After all the gifts, after all the counting, Moses goes into the tabernacle to speak with God.
The gifts were given. The altar was dedicated. And now God speaks. That is what all the giving was for: so that God could meet with his people.
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 9. 'But unto the sons of Kohath he gave none: because the service of the sanctuary belonging unto them was that they should bear upon their shoulders.' Some things are too holy to put on a cart. The ark and the holy things were carried on the shoulders of God’s servants, close to them, with care. Some parts of serving God cannot be made easy or mechanical. They must be carried personally, with reverence.
Verse 11. 'And the LORD said unto Moses, They shall offer their offering, each prince on his day, for the dedicating of the altar.' Each prince on his day. God did not want all the gifts rushed together in one great pile. Each leader had his own day, his own moment before God. God sees each giver, one by one. When you give to God, it is not lost in the crowd. He notices.
Verse 89. 'And when Moses was gone into the tabernacle of the congregation to speak with him, then he heard the voice of one speaking unto him from off the mercy seat that was upon the ark of testimony, from between the two cherubims: and he spake unto him.' The mercy seat was the golden lid of the ark, with the cherubim above it. And from there, God spoke. Not from thunder on a mountain, but from the mercy seat, in the middle of the camp. God speaks to his people from the place of mercy.
Jesus watched people giving at the temple, and said of a poor widow, 'this poor widow hath cast more in, than all they which have cast into the treasury'. And the letter to the Hebrews says, 'Let us therefore come boldly unto the throne of grace'.
Give something to God today, of your time, your money or your care, and know that he sees it, and values it, just as he valued each leader’s gift. Then spend a quiet moment listening for him.
Here is today’s question. Every leader brought the same gift, and every gift was written down in full. What does this tell you about how God sees what you give him?
Let us pray. Lord God, thank you that you see every gift, and every giver, and that nothing given to you is lost or forgotten. Help me to give to you gladly, and to carry holy things with care. And thank you that you speak to your people from the mercy seat. Speak to me, Lord. Amen.
