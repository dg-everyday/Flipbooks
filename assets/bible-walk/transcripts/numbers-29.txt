Imagine a great camp of tents, spread out across the hills. But these are not soldiers’ tents, or refugees’ tents. They are tents for a festival. For a whole week, families leave their houses and live in shelters made of branches, eating and singing and remembering. It is the happiest week of the year. And God himself commanded it.
In chapter twenty eight, God set out the offerings Israel was to bring at their appointed times. Two lambs every day, a continual burnt offering, day after day. And offerings on Israel’s sabbaths, new moons, the Passover, and the day of firstfruits, always beside the daily offering.
Now, in chapter twenty nine, God sets out the offerings for one special month in Israel’s year, the seventh month. Listen for the three great occasions in that month. And listen for how they all point beyond themselves.
The seventh month was the high point of Israel’s year. It came at the end of the harvest, when the crops had been gathered in. And it held three great occasions.
The first came at the beginning of the month.
A day of blowing the trumpets. Remember the silver trumpets in chapter ten, used to gather the people and to call on God. On this day, the trumpets sounded across the land, calling the people to gather before the LORD, to rest from their work, and to bring their offerings. It was like a great announcement: something important is coming.
The second came on the tenth day of the month.
Afflict your souls means to humble yourselves, to fast, and to be sorry for sin. This was the day of atonement, the most solemn day of Israel’s year. The book of Leviticus describes it in detail. On this one day, the high priest went into the most holy place, behind the veil, with the blood of a sacrifice, to make atonement for the sins of the whole nation. It was a day of deep sorrow over sin, and of God’s forgiveness.
And then, after the sorrow, came the joy.
On the fifteenth day of the month began a great feast, lasting seven days. The book of Leviticus calls it the feast of tabernacles, or booths. The people lived in shelters made of branches, to remember how they had lived in tents in the wilderness, and how God had cared for them. It was a feast of thanksgiving, at the end of the harvest.
Then God lists the offerings for each day of the feast. And there is something remarkable about them. On the first day, thirteen young bulls. On the second day, twelve. On the third, eleven. And so on, one less each day, until on the seventh day, seven bulls. Altogether, seventy bulls in one week, besides rams, and lambs, and goats for sin offerings, and the daily offering.
It was the most generous week of offerings in the whole year. Israel poured out their thanks to God.
Then on the eighth day, there was a solemn assembly, a final gathering before the LORD, with one bull, one ram and seven lambs.
And God adds that all these offerings were beside the people’s own vows and freewill offerings. The set feasts did not replace personal gifts to God. They added to them.
Moses tells the children of Israel everything the LORD commanded.
Notice the order of the seventh month. First, the trumpets, calling the people to God. Then the day of atonement, sorrow for sin and forgiveness. Then the feast, overflowing joy and thanksgiving. Repentance comes before rejoicing. Forgiveness opens the way to joy.
These feasts were part of God’s covenant with Israel. Christians read them in the light of Jesus. The letter to the Hebrews explains that Jesus, our great high priest, entered not into a tent made with hands, but into heaven itself, once for all, with his own blood, to make atonement. And in the Gospel of John, at the feast of tabernacles, Jesus stood up and cried, if any man thirst, let him come unto me, and drink.
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 1. 'And in the seventh month, on the first day of the month, ye shall have an holy convocation; ye shall do no servile work: it is a day of blowing the trumpets unto you.' A holy convocation means a sacred gathering. Servile work means ordinary daily labour. God called his people to stop, gather, and listen. Trumpets in the Bible often announce God’s coming. Paul wrote that the Lord himself will one day descend from heaven with the trump of God.
Verse 7. 'And ye shall have on the tenth day of this seventh month an holy convocation; and ye shall afflict your souls: ye shall not do any work therein:' On this day, the people humbled themselves before God, sorry for their sin. True joy before God begins with honesty about sin. We cannot truly celebrate God’s goodness until we have come to him for forgiveness.
Verse 39. 'These things ye shall do unto the LORD in your set feasts, beside your vows, and your freewill offerings, for your burnt offerings, and for your meat offerings, and for your drink offerings, and for your peace offerings.' Set feasts and freewill offerings. Some worship was regular and planned. Some came freely from the heart. God wanted both. Faithful habits of worship, and spontaneous gifts of love.
The letter to the Hebrews says, 'For Christ is not entered into the holy places made with hands, which are the figures of the true; but into heaven itself, now to appear in the presence of God for us'. And at the feast, 'Jesus stood and cried, saying, If any man thirst, let him come unto me, and drink.'
Follow the pattern of the seventh month today. First, stop and turn your attention to God. Then, be honest with him about your sin, and receive his forgiveness. And then, give thanks with joy for all he has done.
Here is today’s question. In Israel’s seventh month, the day of sorrow for sin came before the feast of joy. Why do you think repentance and forgiveness open the way to real joy?
Let us pray. Lord God, I come to you, sorry for my sins. Thank you for Jesus, our great high priest, who made atonement once for all. Fill me with joy and thankfulness for your goodness. And thank you that whoever thirsts can come to Jesus, and drink. Amen.
