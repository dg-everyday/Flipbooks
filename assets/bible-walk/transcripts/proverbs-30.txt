A small spider spins her web high up in the corner of a king’s palace. She has no army. She has no crown. She is tiny, and anyone could squash her with one hand. And yet, there she is, living in the most magnificent building in the land, right next to the king. A wise man named Agur noticed her, and learned something about wisdom.
In chapter twenty nine, we heard that he that being often reproved hardeneth his neck shall suddenly be destroyed. Where there is no vision, the people perish. And the fear of man bringeth a snare, but whoso trusts in the LORD shall be safe. That was the end of Solomon’s proverbs.
Now, in chapter thirty, we hear from a different wise man, named Agur. Listen for how humble he is. And listen for his lists of things, in threes and fours.
The chapter begins, 'The words of Agur the son of Jakeh'. We know nothing else about Agur, except what he tells us in this chapter. And the first thing he tells us is surprising.
'Surely I am more brutish than any man, and have not the understanding of a man. I neither learned wisdom, nor have the knowledge of the holy.' Brutish means like an animal, without understanding. Agur says, I am not clever. I do not have great wisdom. I do not really know God as I should. What an honest way to start! The wisest people are often the most humble, because they know how much they do not know.
And then Agur asks some great questions.
Then Agur turns from his questions to God’s Word.
'Add thou not unto his words, lest he reprove thee, and thou be found a liar.' Do not add your own ideas to God’s Word, and pretend they came from him. God’s Word is perfect as it is.
Then Agur prays a beautiful prayer. 'Two things have I required of thee; deny me them not before I die'. He asks God for two things.
And then he explains his prayer. 'Lest I be full, and deny thee, and say, Who is the LORD? or lest I be poor, and steal, and take the name of my God in vain.' If I am too rich, I might forget about God, and think I do not need him. If I am too poor, I might be tempted to steal, and bring shame on God’s name. Just enough is best.
Then Agur describes four kinds of wicked people: those who curse their parents, those who think they are pure but are not, the proud, and those who devour the poor.
Then Agur starts making lists. He often says, three things, yea four. It is a way of catching our attention, and saying, here is a list, and the last one is the most important.
He speaks of things that are never satisfied, always wanting more, like a leech that keeps sucking blood. Greed is like that. It never says, enough.
Then three things too wonderful for him to understand. 'The way of an eagle in the air; the way of a serpent upon a rock; the way of a ship in the midst of the sea'. How does an eagle stay up in the air? How does a snake move across a rock without legs? How does a ship find its way across the sea? Agur was amazed by the wonders of God’s world.
Then a list of four little creatures. 'There be four things which are little upon the earth, but they are exceeding wise'. 'The ants are a people not strong, yet they prepare their meat in the summer'. The ants are weak, but they plan ahead. 'The conies are but a feeble folk, yet make they their houses in the rocks'. Conies are small animals, a bit like large guinea pigs. They are weak, but they make their homes in the rocks, where they are safe. 'The locusts have no king, yet go they forth all of them by bands'. Locusts have no leader, but they work together in great groups. 'The spider taketh hold with her hands, and is in kings’ palaces.' Tiny and easily crushed, and yet she lives in the palace of a king!
Small things can be wise. Plan ahead like the ant. Find your safety in the Rock, like the conies. Work together like the locusts. And hold on, like the spider.
And Agur ends with some advice. 'If thou hast done foolishly in lifting up thyself, or if thou hast thought evil, lay thine hand upon thy mouth.' If you have been proud or foolish, put your hand over your mouth. Stop talking, and stop making it worse.
'Surely the churning of milk bringeth forth butter, and the wringing of the nose bringeth forth blood: so the forcing of wrath bringeth forth strife.' Churning milk makes butter. Twisting a nose makes it bleed. And stirring up anger makes a fight. Every action has a result.
Now, three verses to hold on to.
Verse 4. 'Who hath ascended up into heaven, or descended? who hath gathered the wind in his fists? who hath bound the waters in a garment? who hath established all the ends of the earth? what is his name, and what is his son’s name, if thou canst tell?' Agur asks, who can go up to heaven and come down? Who holds the wind in his fists, and wraps up the oceans like a cloak? Only God. And then, what is his son’s name? Many Christians have seen in that question a hint of Jesus, the Son of God, who came down from heaven, and went back up. Agur could not answer. But one day, the answer would come.
Verse 5. 'Every word of God is pure: he is a shield unto them that put their trust in him.' Pure means tested, true, with nothing false mixed in. Like silver tested in fire, every word of God has been tested, and found completely true. You can trust it. And God himself is a shield, protecting all who trust in him. His Word and his protection go together.
Verse 8. 'Remove far from me vanity and lies: give me neither poverty nor riches; feed me with food convenient for me'. Vanity means emptiness, worthless things. Food convenient for me means the food I need, my daily bread. Agur asked God to keep him honest, and to give him just enough. Not so much that he forgets God, and not so little that he is tempted to do wrong. It is a prayer of contentment, and a good one for us to pray too.
The psalmist wrote, 'The words of the LORD are pure words: as silver tried in a furnace of earth'. And Jesus taught us to pray, 'Give us this day our daily bread.'
Pray Agur’s prayer today: Lord, keep me from lies, and give me just what I need. And look for one small creature today, an ant, a bird, a spider, and thank God for what it can teach you.
Here is today’s question. Agur prayed for neither poverty nor riches, but just enough. Why do you think too much and too little can both be dangerous for our faith?
Let us pray. Lord God, every word of yours is pure, and you are a shield to those who trust you. Keep me humble, like Agur. Remove far from me empty things and lies. Give me neither poverty nor riches, but feed me with the food I need. And thank you for the wonders of your world, and the little creatures that teach me wisdom. Amen.
