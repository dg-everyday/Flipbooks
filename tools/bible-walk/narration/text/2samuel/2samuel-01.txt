The Living Voice. 2 Samuel, chapter 1. How Are the Mighty Fallen.

A man comes running into the camp, his clothes torn and dust on his head. He falls on the ground before David, and pulls out two things: a royal crown, and a gold armband. He expects a reward. Saul, David’s enemy, the man who hunted him for years, is dead. Surely David will be glad. But David tears his own clothes, and weeps.

In the introduction, we heard that the second book of Samuel tells the story of David’s reign, the glory and the shame. We heard about God’s promise of a throne for ever, about David’s great sin and true repentance, and about God’s great mercy.

Now, in chapter one, the news of Saul’s death reaches David. Listen for how David responds to the death of his enemy. And listen for the song he sings.

After the death of Saul, David has come back from defeating the Amalekites, and has been in Ziklag for two days. On the third day, a man arrives from Saul’s camp, with his clothes torn and dust on his head, the signs of grief. He falls to the ground before David.

David asks, where have you come from? He says, I have escaped from the camp of Israel.

David says, what happened? Tell me. And the man says, the people have fled from the battle. Many have fallen and died. And Saul and his son Jonathan are dead too.

David asks, how do you know that Saul and Jonathan are dead?

And the young man tells a story. He says he happened to be on mount Gilboa, and saw Saul leaning on his spear, with the chariots closing in. Saul called to him, and asked him to end his life. The young man says he did so, because he was sure Saul could not survive. And he took the crown from Saul’s head, and the armband from his arm, and brought them to David.

But we know from the last chapter of first Samuel that Saul had already taken his own life. It seems the young man was not telling the truth. He found Saul’s body, took the crown, and made up a story, hoping David would reward him.

David tears his clothes. So do all the men with him. And they mourn and weep and fast all that day, for Saul, and for Jonathan his son, and for the people of the LORD, and for the house of Israel, because they had fallen in battle.

David does not celebrate. He mourns. He mourns for Saul, the man who tried again and again to take his life.

Then David asks the young man, where are you from? He says, I am the son of a foreigner, an Amalekite.

And David says, how were you not afraid to lift your hand to destroy the LORD’s anointed? Twice, David had refused to harm Saul, even when he had the chance. And now this young man claims to have done it, for a reward. David has him put to death, saying, your own mouth has testified against you.

Then David sings a lament over Saul and Jonathan. He tells the people to teach it to the children of Judah. It is called the song of the bow, and it is written in an ancient book called the book of Jasher.

David sings that the beauty of Israel has fallen on the high places.

'How are the mighty fallen!' That line will come back three times in the song.

He says, do not tell it in Gath, do not announce it in the streets of Ashkelon, or the daughters of the Philistines will rejoice.

He calls out to the mountains of Gilboa, where Saul fell. 'Ye mountains of Gilboa, let there be no dew, neither let there be rain, upon you'. Let nothing grow there, because there the shield of the mighty was thrown down, the shield of Saul.

He remembers the bow of Jonathan, which never turned back, and the sword of Saul, which never came back empty.

And then he sings of both of them.

He calls on the daughters of Israel to weep over Saul, who clothed them in scarlet and gold.

Then David sings of Jonathan, his friend.

David says nothing bad about Saul in this song. He does not mention the spears, the hunting, the years of running. He remembers only the good. He honours the king, and grieves for his friend.

Now, three verses to hold on to.

Verse 23. 'Saul and Jonathan were lovely and pleasant in their lives, and in their death they were not divided: they were swifter than eagles, they were stronger than lions.' Lovely and pleasant. David chooses to remember the best in Saul. Saul had treated David terribly, and had even turned on Jonathan. But in death, father and son were together. David’s generosity is remarkable. He does not gloat. He honours. That is the heart of someone who has truly forgiven.

Verse 26. 'I am distressed for thee, my brother Jonathan: very pleasant hast thou been unto me: thy love to me was wonderful, passing the love of women.' My brother Jonathan. Their friendship was deeper than family. Jonathan had loved David as his own soul, given him his robe and sword, protected him from his father, and strengthened his hand in God. Now he is gone. David’s grief shows how much a true friend is worth. It is right to grieve deeply for those we love.

Verse 27. 'How are the mighty fallen, and the weapons of war perished!' The song ends with the same words, how are the mighty fallen. Saul was the tallest man in Israel, the first king. Jonathan was a brave and faithful prince. And both are gone. No one is so mighty that they will not one day fall. It is a reminder that our lives are short, and that our strength is not our own.

Jesus wept at the grave of his friend Lazarus, and the people said, 'Behold how he loved him!' And Paul wrote, 'Rejoice with them that do rejoice, and weep with them that weep.'

Is there someone who hurt you, whom you could choose to remember with kindness? Ask God to help you see the good in them. And if you are grieving someone, bring your grief honestly to God. He weeps with you.

Here is today’s question. David mourned for Saul instead of celebrating his death. What does it take to grieve for someone who has hurt us?

Let us pray. Lord God, thank you for the gift of friends, and for the love that is stronger than death. Comfort all who grieve today. Give me a generous heart like David’s, that remembers the good in others, even those who hurt me. And remind me that my life and strength are in your hands. Amen.
