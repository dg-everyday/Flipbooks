The Living Voice. Isaiah, chapter 53. Wounded for Our Transgressions.

A man from Africa is riding in his chariot along a desert road, reading aloud from a scroll of the prophet Isaiah. He reads about someone led like a sheep to the slaughter, who did not open his mouth. And he stops, puzzled. Who is the prophet talking about? Himself, or someone else? A stranger running alongside his chariot asks, understandest thou what thou readest? That question, and its answer, are the subject of this chapter.

In First Kings chapter eighteen, God sent fire on Mount Carmel, and the people cried, the LORD, he is the God. But the turning did not last. The northern kingdom of Israel kept on worshipping idols, and in the end God allowed the Assyrian empire to conquer it and carry its people away.

In the southern kingdom of Judah, God sent the prophet Isaiah. Isaiah saw the LORD high and lifted up in the temple, and heard the angels cry holy, holy, holy. He warned Judah that judgment was coming if they did not turn back. But he also spoke more beautifully than any other prophet about the hope beyond the judgment. He promised a child: 'For unto us a child is born, unto us a son is given'. And he wrote songs about a mysterious servant of the LORD.

Chapter fifty-three is the greatest of these songs. In The Big Picture, the prophets are pointing forward to how God will finally deal with sin.

This chapter is a poem, and it tells the story of the servant from beginning to end.

It begins with a question: 'Who hath believed our report?' What the prophet is about to say is so surprising that few will believe it.

The servant grows up like a tender young plant, like a root coming out of dry ground. There is nothing impressive about him to look at, nothing that would make people want him. 'He is despised and rejected of men; a man of sorrows, and acquainted with grief'. People turn their faces away from him, and think nothing of him.

But then the poem turns. 'Surely he hath borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows'. The sorrows he carried were not his own. They were ours. And we thought he was being punished by God for something he had done.

The truth is the opposite. He was wounded for what we did wrong. We are the ones who wandered away, every one of us. And the LORD laid all of it on him.

He was treated cruelly, and he suffered, but he did not complain or defend himself. Like a lamb led to the slaughter, and like a sheep silent before its shearers, 'so he openeth not his mouth.' He was taken away by an unjust judgment. He was cut off out of the land of the living. 'for the transgression of my people was he stricken.' He was given a grave with the wicked, and with the rich in his death, although he had done no violence, and there was no deceit in his mouth.

'Yet it pleased the LORD to bruise him'. It was God’s own plan. His soul was made an offering for sin.

And then, impossibly, the poem goes on after his death. 'he shall see his seed, he shall prolong his days'. He will see the results of his suffering and be satisfied. He will make many righteous, because he bears their sins. God will give him a place among the great, because he poured out his soul to death, and was counted among the sinners, and 'bare the sin of many, and made intercession for the transgressors.' He even prays for the people who wounded him.

Now, three verses to hold on to.

Verse 5. 'But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed.' Listen to all the small words: our, our, our, we. Transgressions and iniquities are our sins. Chastisement is the punishment that brings us peace with God. Stripes are the wounds of a whip. He took the wounds; we receive the healing. He took the punishment; we receive the peace. This is the heart of the good news.

Verse 6. 'All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned every one to his own way; and the LORD hath laid on him the iniquity of us all.' This verse begins and ends with the word all. All of us have wandered, like sheep leaving the shepherd. No one is left out. And the LORD laid on him the sin of us all. No one is left out of that either. The same all that names our guilt names the reach of his rescue.

Verse 11. 'He shall see of the travail of his soul, and shall be satisfied: by his knowledge shall my righteous servant justify many; for he shall bear their iniquities.' Travail means hard, painful labour. To justify means to declare someone righteous, to put them right with God. The servant’s suffering was not wasted. It had a purpose: to make many right with God. And he will look on what his suffering won, and he will be satisfied. Every person saved through him is part of that joy.

The man in the chariot was an official of the queen of Ethiopia. The stranger running beside him was Philip, a follower of Jesus. The official asked whom the prophet was speaking of. 'Then Philip opened his mouth, and began at the same scripture, and preached unto him Jesus.' The servant Isaiah saw, wounded for our transgressions and bearing the sin of many, is Jesus, who died on the cross and rose again.

Read verse five again slowly, and put your own name in it. He was wounded for my transgressions. He was bruised for my iniquities. With his stripes I am healed. Then thank him.

Here is today’s question. Isaiah says the LORD laid on him the iniquity of us all. What will you say to Jesus today, knowing that he carried your sins?

Let us pray. Lord Jesus, you were despised and rejected, a man of sorrows, and you carried my griefs. You were wounded for my transgressions and bruised for my iniquities. Thank you that by your stripes I am healed, and that you made intercession even for those who wounded you. Amen.
