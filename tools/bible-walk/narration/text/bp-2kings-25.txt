The Living Voice. 2 Kings, chapter 25. The City Was Broken Up.

For months the city has been surrounded. No food comes in. Children are hungry, and then starving. And one day, the great walls of Jerusalem, the city of David, the city of God, are broken through. Enemy soldiers pour in. And within weeks, the temple that Solomon built, where God put his name, is on fire.

In Isaiah chapter fifty-three, the prophet saw a servant who would carry the sins of many. But Isaiah also warned Judah that if they did not turn back, judgment would come. Some kings listened. King Hezekiah trusted God, and Jerusalem was saved from the Assyrian army. Young King Josiah found the book of the law in the temple, tore his clothes, and led the people back to God.

But after Josiah, the kings and the people turned away again, worse than before. God kept sending prophets. The book of Chronicles says, 'But they mocked the messengers of God, and despised his words, and misused his prophets, until the wrath of the LORD arose against his people, till there was no remedy.'

A new empire had risen in the east: Babylon, under King Nebuchadnezzar. He had already carried away some of Jerusalem’s young men, among them a youth named Daniel. Now Judah’s last king, Zedekiah, rebels against Babylon. In The Big Picture, this chapter is the darkest moment in the Old Testament.

Nebuchadnezzar and his whole army come against Jerusalem and build siege walls all around it. The siege goes on, and on. At last 'the famine prevailed in the city, and there was no bread for the people of the land.'

Then the city wall is broken through. King Zedekiah and his soldiers flee in the dark, through a gate between the walls, toward the plains of Jericho. But the army of Babylon chases them down, and his soldiers scatter. Zedekiah is captured and brought to the king of Babylon. There his sons are killed before his eyes. Then they put out his eyes, bind him in chains of brass, and carry him away to Babylon.

Then Nebuzaradan, the captain of Babylon’s guard, comes to Jerusalem. He burns the house of the LORD, the king’s palace, and every great house in the city. His army breaks down the walls of Jerusalem all around. He carries away the people who are left, and leaves only the poorest of the land to work the vineyards and the fields.

The great bronze pillars of the temple, the bronze sea, and all the vessels used in the worship of God are broken up and carried away to Babylon. The leaders of the people are taken to the king of Babylon and put to death. And then come the saddest words of the chapter.

Over those who are left, the king of Babylon appoints a governor named Gedaliah. But Gedaliah is murdered, and the people, afraid of Babylon, flee to Egypt. The family God once brought out of Egypt, with such a mighty hand, is running back to it.

And yet the chapter, and the whole book of Kings, does not end there. Many years later, in Babylon, a new king comes to the throne. He brings Jehoiachin, one of the last kings of David’s line, out of prison. He speaks kindly to him, sets his seat above the other captive kings, and gives him a place at the king’s table for the rest of his life.

Now, three verses to hold on to.

Verse 9. 'And he burnt the house of the LORD, and the king’s house, and all the houses of Jerusalem, and every great man’s house burnt he with fire.' The temple was the place where God had chosen to put his name, the centre of Israel’s worship. Its destruction seemed to say that God had abandoned his people. But the prophets explained it differently. God had not broken his promises. His people had broken theirs, again and again, and God was doing exactly what he had warned he would do. Sin has real consequences.

Verse 21. 'So Judah was carried away out of their land.' Seven short words. All the way back in Genesis, God promised Abraham the land. In Joshua, God gave it to them. Now they are carried away from it. It feels like the story of the promise has come to a dead end. The people would ask in Babylon, has God forgotten us? Is the promise over?

Verse 29. 'And changed his prison garments: and he did eat bread continually before him all the days of his life.' The book ends with a king of David’s family, out of prison, in new clothes, eating at a king’s table. It is a small thing. But God had promised David a throne for ever, and David’s line had not been wiped out. A tiny light was still burning. God had not forgotten his promise.

Among the ruins of Jerusalem, the prophet Jeremiah wept. And out of his grief came these words: 'It is of the LORD’S mercies that we are not consumed, because his compassions fail not. They are new every morning: great is thy faithfulness.' Even in judgment, God was faithful. And the story of the promise was not over.

Sometimes life brings us to a place of ruins, through our own choices or through the choices of others. If that is where you are right now, please do not give up. Turn back to God honestly, and look for the small signs that he has not forgotten you. His mercies are new every day.

Here is today’s question. Even after Jerusalem fell, God kept a light burning for David’s family. Where do you need to hold on to God’s faithfulness today?

Let us pray. LORD, I have seen what sin does. Forgive me for the times I have ignored your warnings. Thank you that even in judgment you do not forget your promises, and that your mercies are new every day. Great is your faithfulness. Amen.
