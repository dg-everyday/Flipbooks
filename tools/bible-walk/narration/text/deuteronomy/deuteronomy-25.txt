The Living Voice. Deuteronomy, chapter 25. Thou Shalt Not Muzzle the Ox When He Treadeth Out the Corn.

An ox is walking round and round on a threshing floor, its hooves crushing the stalks of grain, separating the wheat from the straw. All day it works, surrounded by food. And its owner could tie a muzzle over its mouth, so it cannot eat a single bite. God says: do not do that. Let the ox eat while it works. It is a tiny law about an animal. But Paul saw something much bigger in it.

In chapter twenty four, Moses gave laws that protected the vulnerable: return a poor man’s cloak before he sleeps, pay a worker’s wages the same day, and leave the forgotten sheaf in the field, for the stranger, for the fatherless, and for the widow. And Ruth gleaned in the fields of Boaz, under that very law.

Now, in chapter twenty five, Moses gives more laws about fairness. Listen for how God limits punishment. And listen for the law that shows up again, in the story of Ruth and Boaz.

If there is a dispute between people, Moses says, and they come to court, the judges shall decide the case, declaring the innocent innocent, and the guilty guilty.

And if the guilty person deserves to be beaten, the judge shall see it done in front of him, with no more than forty strokes. Not more. Moses gives the reason: if he is beaten more than that, your brother would be degraded in your sight.

Even a guilty man was still a brother. God set a limit on punishment, to protect his dignity. Punishment was never to become cruelty.

Long after, the apostle Paul wrote that he had been beaten five times by his own people, with forty strokes less one. They stopped one short, to be sure they never broke this limit.

Then comes the short law about the ox.

The ox worked hard, and deserved to eat. God cared about animals. But Paul saw something more in this law. In the New Testament, he wrote that God was also teaching his people that those who work deserve to be paid. Those who serve God and his people should be supported, just as the ox should eat from the grain it treads.

Then Moses gives a law about family. If brothers live together, and one dies without a son, his widow should not marry a stranger outside the family. Her husband’s brother should marry her. And their first son would carry on the name of the brother who died, so that his name would not be blotted out of Israel.

This law protected the widow, who might otherwise be left alone and poor, and kept the dead man’s family line alive.

But what if the brother refused? Then the widow could go to the elders at the town gate, and say, my husband’s brother refuses to keep his brother’s name alive in Israel. The elders would speak to him. And if he still refused, she would take off his sandal in front of the elders, as a sign of shame, and his family would be known as the house of him whose sandal was taken off.

Do you remember the book of Ruth? Naomi’s close relative would not redeem Ruth, and he took off his sandal before the elders at the gate. And then Boaz stepped forward, and married Ruth, to keep the name of her husband alive. The law in this chapter is the background to that beautiful story.

Then Moses speaks about honest business.

Do not have two kinds of weights in your bag, Moses says, a heavy one and a light one. Or two kinds of measures in your house, a large one and a small one.

A dishonest trader might use a heavy weight when buying, to get more, and a light weight when selling, to give less. God hates that kind of cheating. Everyone who does such things, Moses says, is detestable to the LORD.

And the chapter ends with a memory.

Remember what Amalek did to you on the way, when you came out of Egypt.

When God gives you rest from your enemies, Moses says, you shall blot out the memory of Amalek. Do not forget.

Now, three verses to hold on to.

Verse 4. 'Thou shalt not muzzle the ox when he treadeth out the corn.' God cared for the ox. And Paul used this verse to teach that those who work for God’s people should be supported by them. Paul wrote, the labourer is worthy of his reward. God wants workers, whether ox or person, to share in the good they help to produce.

Verse 15. 'But thou shalt have a perfect and just weight, a perfect and just measure shalt thou have: that thy days may be lengthened in the land which the LORD thy God giveth thee.' A perfect and just weight. Honesty in business matters to God. Cheating, even in small ways, even when no one notices, is wrong in his eyes. A proverb says, a false balance is abomination to the LORD: but a just weight is his delight.

Verse 18. 'How he met thee by the way, and smote the hindmost of thee, even all that were feeble behind thee, when thou wast faint and weary; and he feared not God.' Amalek attacked the weakest, those lagging behind, the tired and the feeble. And he feared not God. God takes special notice of cruelty to the weak. His people are to protect those at the back, not take advantage of them.

Paul wrote, 'For the scripture saith, Thou shalt not muzzle the ox that treadeth out the corn. And, The labourer is worthy of his reward.' And a proverb says, 'A false balance is abomination to the LORD: but a just weight is his delight.'

Is there any area where you are tempted to cut corners, to give a little less, or take a little more, than is fair? Choose honesty today. And look out for anyone at the back, tired and struggling, who needs your help.

Here is today’s question. Amalek attacked the feeble at the back, who were faint and weary. How can you look out for those who are struggling and falling behind?

Let us pray. Lord God, you delight in honesty and fairness. Make me honest in all my dealings, big and small. Thank you that you care for the weak, and even for the ox at work. Help me to protect those who are tired and falling behind, and to support those who work for your people. Amen.
