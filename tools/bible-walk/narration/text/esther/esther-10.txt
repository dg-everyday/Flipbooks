The Living Voice. Esther, chapter 10. Seeking the Wealth of His People.

Some stories end with a grand finale. The book of Esther ends with a quiet note, just three short verses. A word about the king’s taxes. A mention of the official records. And a final picture of a good man, using his power to care for his people. But sometimes the quietest endings say the most.

In chapter nine, the day appointed for the destruction of the Jews was turned to the contrary. They stood for their lives, and took no plunder. Mordecai grew greater and greater. And the days were turned from sorrow to joy, with feasting, sharing and gifts to the poor. They called them Purim, after the lot that Haman cast.

Now we come to the last chapter of Esther. It is the shortest chapter of the book, so before we hear it, we will look back over the whole story. Listen for all the ways it turned around. And listen for the hand of God, never named, yet never absent.

The book of Esther has only three verses left. Before we hear them, let us look back at the road we have travelled.

The story began with a great king, showing off his glory at a feast that lasted six months. His queen refused to come when he called, and her place was given to another. It seemed like palace gossip. But it opened a door.

A young Jewish orphan named Esther, raised by her cousin Mordecai, was taken to the palace and made queen. She kept her people a secret. It seemed like chance. But she was being placed exactly where she would be needed.

Mordecai overheard a plot against the king, and saved his life. He was never thanked. It seemed forgotten. But it was written in the book.

Then Haman rose to power. When Mordecai would not bow, Haman plotted to destroy all the Jews. He cast the lot, and it fell on a day almost a year away. It seemed like luck. But it gave God’s people time.

The law was signed, and it could not be changed. God’s people mourned in sackcloth. And Mordecai asked Esther, who knoweth whether thou art come to the kingdom for such a time as this? Esther answered, if I perish, I perish.

She went to the king uninvited, and he held out his golden sceptre. She invited him to a banquet, and then to another. It seemed like a delay. But during that one day of waiting, the king could not sleep. The records were read. Mordecai was honoured, by the very man who wanted him dead.

At the second banquet, Esther named her enemy. Haman was hanged on the gallows he built for Mordecai. A new law was written. And the day meant for destruction became a day of deliverance, and of joy.

Again and again, the story turned upside down. The proud fell, and the humble were lifted up. And not once was God named. But who else could have done it?

Now we hear the last three verses of the book.

First, a word about the king.

Then the writer tells us where to find out more.

And the very last word goes to Mordecai.

That is how the book ends. Not with the king’s glory, which is where it began, but with a faithful man using his power to do good. Mordecai once sat in sackcloth at the gate. Now he stands next to the king, seeking the good of his people, and speaking peace.

Why does God never appear by name in this book? We are not told. But perhaps it is because the book is meant to teach us how God usually works. Most of us will never see a burning bush or hear a voice from heaven. Our lives are made of ordinary days, ordinary choices, things that seem like chance. And the book of Esther tells us that God is in all of it. Even when we cannot see him. Even when we cannot hear him. He is working, quietly, faithfully, to keep his promises and to care for his people.

Now, three verses to hold on to.

Verse 1. 'And the king Ahasuerus laid a tribute upon the land, and upon the isles of the sea.' A tribute is a tax. It seems a strange way to begin the last chapter. But it reminds us that life goes on. The king still rules his empire, collecting taxes from the land and from the islands of the sea. The great rescue is over, and ordinary life continues. God’s people still live in a world ruled by earthly kings. But now they know that a greater King watches over them.

Verse 2. 'And all the acts of his power and of his might, and the declaration of the greatness of Mordecai, whereunto the king advanced him, are they not written in the book of the chronicles of the kings of Media and Persia?' The writer points us to the official records of Persia, as if to say, this really happened. You can look it up. Mordecai’s greatness was recorded in the chronicles of the kings. Remember, it was a page in those very chronicles that the king heard read aloud, at just the right moment. What is written in the book is never forgotten by God.

Verse 3. 'For Mordecai the Jew was next unto king Ahasuerus, and great among the Jews, and accepted of the multitude of his brethren, seeking the wealth of his people, and speaking peace to all his seed.' Wealth here means wellbeing, the good of his people. Mordecai used his power, not for himself, but to seek the good of others, and to speak peace. That is a picture of what true greatness looks like. Not like Haman, who wanted everyone to bow to him. But like Mordecai, who used his position to serve. And it points us to Jesus, who said that whoever would be great among you, let him be your servant.

Mary sang that God 'hath put down the mighty from their seats, and exalted them of low degree.' And Peter wrote, 'Humble yourselves therefore under the mighty hand of God, that he may exalt you in due time'.

Like Mordecai, you have some kind of influence, at home, at school, at work, among friends. How can you use it today to seek the good of others, and to speak peace? Look for one way to serve someone with what God has given you.

Here is today’s question. We have walked through the whole book of Esther. God was never named, yet his hand was in every turn. Where can you look back and see God’s hidden hand in your own story?

Let us pray. Lord God, thank you for the book of Esther, and for showing me that you are at work even when I cannot see you. Thank you that you bring down the proud and lift up the humble. Help me to trust you in ordinary days, and to use whatever you have given me to seek the good of others, and to speak peace. Amen.
