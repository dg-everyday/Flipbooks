The Living Voice. Exodus, chapter 26. The Vail Shall Divide.

From the outside, it does not look like much. A long, low tent, covered in dull, weather beaten animal skins. You might walk right past it. But step inside, and you gasp. All around you, the walls shine with gold. Above you, a ceiling of beautiful woven cloth in blue, purple and scarlet, with golden angels embroidered all over it. And ahead of you hangs a heavy curtain, hiding the most holy place of all.

In chapter twenty five, God told Moses, let them make me a sanctuary; that I may dwell among them. The people were to bring an offering willingly, from the heart. God described the ark of the covenant, covered with gold, with the mercy seat on top, between two golden cherubim. There I will meet with thee, God said. And there would be a golden table for the bread, and a golden lampstand like an almond tree.

Now, in chapter twenty six, God describes the tent itself. Listen for how the curtains are joined together. And listen for the veil, and what it divides.

Chapter twenty six is full of measurements, curtains, loops and boards. But as we listen, we can picture the tabernacle taking shape, and see what God was teaching.

The tabernacle had four layers of coverings, like four blankets laid one on top of the other.

The first, innermost layer was the most beautiful. 'Moreover thou shalt make the tabernacle with ten curtains of fine twined linen, and blue, and purple, and scarlet: with cherubims of cunning work shalt thou make them.' Cunning work means skilful work, done by expert craftsmen. Fine white linen, woven with blue, purple and scarlet threads, and embroidered with cherubim, the angels who guard God’s presence. When the priests stood inside, they would look up and see angels all around them, as if they were standing in heaven.

These ten curtains were joined together in two groups of five. Each group had fifty loops of blue thread along its edge. And fifty golden clasps, called taches, hooked the loops together.

The second layer was made of goats’ hair, eleven curtains of it, a strong, dark cloth, good for keeping out the weather. These were joined with clasps of bronze.

The third layer was made of rams’ skins dyed red. And the fourth, outside layer was made of badgers’ skins, a tough leather. It was a plain, hard wearing covering against sun, wind and rain.

So from the outside, the tabernacle looked plain and ordinary. But inside, it was glorious. Isaiah later wrote about the coming Messiah, he hath no form nor comeliness. Jesus did not look special on the outside. But inside, he was full of the glory of God.

Then God describes the walls. They were made of boards of acacia wood, standing upright, each one ten cubits tall, about as tall as two grown men. Twenty boards on the south side, twenty on the north, and six on the west, with special corner boards. Each board stood in two sockets, or bases, made of solid silver. So the whole tent stood on a foundation of silver.

Long wooden bars ran through rings on the boards, to hold them together. 'And the middle bar in the midst of the boards shall reach from end to end.' And the boards and bars were all covered with gold.

And here is something clever. Every piece of the tabernacle could be taken apart, carried, and put together again. Whenever God led his people on, the priests would take the whole tabernacle down, pack it up, and carry it to the next place. God was not tied to one place. He travelled with his people.

Then comes the most important part of the chapter.

The veil was hung on four pillars of acacia wood, covered with gold, standing on silver sockets.

So the tabernacle had two rooms. The first, larger room was called the holy place. The golden table with the bread stood on one side, and the golden lampstand on the other. The priests served there every day.

The second, smaller room, behind the veil, was called the most holy place, or the holy of holies. The ark of the covenant stood there, with the mercy seat on top. This was where God’s presence dwelt. No one could go in there. Only the high priest, once a year, with the blood of a sacrifice.

And at the front of the tent, a curtain hung over the doorway, beautifully embroidered in blue, purple and scarlet.

The veil was a constant reminder. God is holy, and we are sinful. Our sin separates us from him. We cannot just walk into his presence.

But many centuries later, something amazing happened. When Jesus died on the cross, the Gospels tell us that the veil of the temple was torn in two, from the top to the bottom. From the top! Not torn by any man, but by God. The way into God’s presence was open. Through Jesus’ death, everyone who trusts in him can come close to God.

Now, three verses to hold on to.

Verse 6. 'And thou shalt make fifty taches of gold, and couple the curtains together with the taches: and it shall be one tabernacle.' Couple means join. Ten separate curtains, joined by golden clasps, became one tabernacle. Many pieces, one dwelling place for God. The New Testament says that God’s people are now his dwelling place, and that we are built together, like one building. Many different people, joined together by God’s love, into one home for him.

Verse 31. 'And thou shalt make a vail of blue, and purple, and scarlet, and fine twined linen of cunning work: with cherubims shall it be made'. The veil was beautiful, but it was also a barrier. The cherubim on it reminded people of the cherubim who guarded the way to the tree of life in Eden, after Adam and Eve sinned. Ever since then, sinful people had been shut out from God’s presence. The veil said, you cannot come in. Not yet.

Verse 33. 'and the vail shall divide unto you between the holy place and the most holy.' Divide means separate. The veil separated the place where the priests served from the place where God dwelt. But the letter to the Hebrews tells us that Jesus has opened for us a new and living way, through the veil, that is to say, his flesh. When his body was broken on the cross, the veil was torn, and the way was opened.

Matthew tells us, 'And, behold, the veil of the temple was rent in twain from the top to the bottom'. And the letter to the Hebrews says, 'By a new and living way, which he hath consecrated for us, through the veil, that is to say, his flesh'.

Thank God today that, because of Jesus, the veil is torn, and you can come close to him. You do not need a priest or a sacrifice. You can come to God in prayer, any time, through Jesus.

Here is today’s question. From the outside, the tabernacle looked plain, but inside it was glorious. What do you think that teaches us about how God sees people, and about Jesus?

Let us pray. Lord God, you are holy, and my sin separates me from you. But thank you that when Jesus died, the veil was torn from the top to the bottom. Thank you that, through him, I can come into your presence. Join your people together, like the curtains of the tabernacle, into one home for you. Amen.
