The Living Voice. Joshua, chapter 4. What Mean These Stones?.

A child is walking with her father, and they come to a strange pile of large river stones, standing on their own in a field. The child stops, and asks, what are these stones for? And her father smiles, because he has been waiting for that question. He kneels down beside her, and begins to tell her the story of the day the river stood still.

In chapter three, the priests carried the ark of the covenant to the Jordan, in full flood at harvest time. As soon as their feet touched the water, the river stood up in a heap far upstream, and the people crossed on dry ground. The priests stood firm in the middle of the riverbed until every person was across.

Now, in chapter four, God wants Israel to remember what he has done. Listen for the twelve stones, and for the question that children will one day ask.

When all the people have crossed over the Jordan, the LORD speaks to Joshua.

Choose twelve men, one from each tribe, God says. Tell them to take twelve stones from the middle of the Jordan, from the very place where the priests’ feet are standing firm. Carry them over with you, and set them down in the place where you will camp.

So Joshua calls the twelve men he has chosen. Go into the middle of the Jordan, he tells them, in front of the ark. Each of you lift up a stone onto your shoulder, one for each tribe of Israel.

These are not small pebbles. Each man carries a stone on his shoulder. It is heavy work, walking up out of the riverbed with a great stone. But Joshua tells them why.

Joshua gives them the answer.

So the twelve men do as Joshua commands. They take up twelve stones from the riverbed, and carry them to the camp, and lay them down there.

Joshua also sets up twelve stones in the middle of the Jordan, in the place where the priests stood. And the writer adds, 'they are there unto this day.'

All this time, the priests carrying the ark stand in the middle of the river, until everything God commanded is done. And the people hurry across.

The fighting men of Reuben, Gad and half the tribe of Manasseh cross over armed, ahead of the others, just as they promised Joshua in chapter one. About forty thousand of them go over, ready for battle, onto the plains of Jericho. They keep their word.

The writer tells us what this day meant for Joshua. 'On that day the LORD magnified Joshua in the sight of all Israel; and they feared him, as they feared Moses, all the days of his life.' God had promised to do this, and he did.

Then the LORD tells Joshua to bring the priests up out of the Jordan. Joshua says to them, come up out of the river. And as soon as the soles of the priests’ feet touch the dry land on the far bank, the waters of the Jordan return to their place, and overflow all their banks, just as before.

Imagine standing on the bank, watching. The moment the ark is out, the river comes rushing back. It is clear to everyone that it was God who held it back, and God alone.

The people come up out of the Jordan in the first month, and camp at a place called Gilgal, on the eastern edge of Jericho. And there Joshua sets up the twelve stones they carried from the river.

And he speaks to the people about the future, about their children and grandchildren who were not born yet.

Then Joshua explains why God did this.

Why stones? Stones last. Long after everyone who crossed the river was gone, the stones would still be standing. And every time a child asked about them, the story would be told again. God knows how easily we forget. So he gives us ways to remember.

Now, three verses to hold on to.

Verse 7. 'Then ye shall answer them, That the waters of Jordan were cut off before the ark of the covenant of the LORD; when it passed over Jordan, the waters of Jordan were cut off: and these stones shall be for a memorial unto the children of Israel for ever.' A memorial is something that helps us remember. God expected the children to ask questions. And the answer to their questions was not about the strength of Israel. It was about the LORD, and his ark going before them. Every family can have its own memorials, ways of remembering and telling what God has done.

Verse 22. 'Then ye shall let your children know, saying, Israel came over this Jordan on dry land.' Notice who is to teach the children. Not just the priests. Not just Joshua. Ordinary fathers and mothers. Faith is passed on at home, one question and one story at a time. And notice how simple the answer is. Israel came over this Jordan on dry land. A child can understand it, and remember it, and one day tell it to their own children.

Verse 24. 'That all the people of the earth might know the hand of the LORD, that it is mighty: that ye might fear the LORD your God for ever.' God opened the river for two reasons. First, so that all the people of the earth would know how mighty he is. God’s work in Israel was meant to be seen by the whole world. And second, so that his own people would fear him, love and honour him, for ever. What God does for us is meant to draw us closer to him, and to point others to him.

A psalm says, 'We will not hide them from their children, shewing to the generation to come the praises of the LORD, and his strength, and his wonderful works that he hath done.' And another psalm says, 'One generation shall praise thy works to another, and shall declare thy mighty acts.'

What has God done in your life that you never want to forget? Find a way to remember it today. Write it down, or keep a small object as a reminder. And tell the story to someone else, especially someone younger.

Here is today’s question. Israel set up stones so their children would ask, what mean these stones? What is one story of God’s faithfulness that you would want the next generation to hear?

Let us pray. Lord God, thank you for all the wonderful things you have done. Forgive me for how quickly I forget. Help me to remember your faithfulness, and to tell it to others, especially to children, so that they too will know that your hand is mighty. Amen.
