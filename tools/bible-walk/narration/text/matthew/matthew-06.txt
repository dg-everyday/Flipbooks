The Living Voice. Matthew, chapter 6. Our Father Which Art in Heaven.

Imagine giving a big gift to someone in need, and then hiring a trumpet player to march down the street ahead of you, so everyone knows how generous you are. It sounds silly. But Jesus said some people in his day did something very like it. They gave, and prayed, and fasted, so that others would notice. And Jesus said, they have their reward. That is all they will get.

In chapter five, Jesus began the Sermon on the Mount. He said, blessed are the poor in spirit, the merciful and the pure in heart. He called his followers the salt of the earth and the light of the world. And he took God’s law deep into the heart, even to loving our enemies.

Now, in chapter six, Jesus talks about our life with God that others cannot see: giving, praying and fasting. He teaches the prayer that Christians all over the world still pray. And he talks about money and worry. Listen for who is watching when no one else is. And listen for what Jesus says to put first.

Jesus begins with a warning. 'Take heed that ye do not your alms before men, to be seen of them'. Alms are gifts to the poor. If we give so that people will praise us, then their praise is all the reward we get.

Jesus says, when you give, do not sound a trumpet, as the hypocrites do. A hypocrite was an actor, someone wearing a mask. Instead, 'let not thy left hand know what thy right hand doeth'. Give so quietly that it is almost a secret even from yourself. And your Father, who sees in secret, will reward you.

It is the same with prayer. Do not pray on street corners to be seen. Go into your room, shut the door, and pray to your Father, who is in secret. And do not pile up empty words, as if God only listens to long prayers, 'for your Father knoweth what things ye have need of, before ye ask him.'

Then Jesus teaches them how to pray.

'Hallowed be thy name.' Hallowed means treated as holy. 'Thy kingdom come. Thy will be done in earth, as it is in heaven. Give us this day our daily bread. And forgive us our debts, as we forgive our debtors. And lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil'. Many Bibles add at the end, 'For thine is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory, for ever. Amen.'

Jesus adds a word about forgiving. If you forgive others, your Father will forgive you. If you will not forgive others, you are shutting the door on his forgiveness too.

When you fast, going without food to seek God, do not put on a sad face so everyone knows. Wash your face, and let it be between you and God.

Then Jesus turns to money and treasure. Do not store up treasure on earth, where moths eat clothes, rust spoils metal, and thieves break in and steal. Store up treasure in heaven, where nothing can spoil it or steal it.

And Jesus explains why it matters so much.

'No man can serve two masters'. You will end up loving one and despising the other. 'Ye cannot serve God and mammon.' Mammon means money and possessions, when they become a master.

So Jesus says, do not worry about your life, what you will eat or drink or wear. 'Behold the fowls of the air: for they sow not, neither do they reap, nor gather into barns; yet your heavenly Father feedeth them. Are ye not much better than they?' Can any of you add a single day to your life by worrying?

'Consider the lilies of the field, how they grow; they toil not, neither do they spin'. Even Solomon in all his glory was not dressed like one of these. If God clothes the wild flowers, which bloom for a day, will he not much more clothe you, 'O ye of little faith?'

People who do not know God chase after these things. But your heavenly Father knows you need them.

And then Jesus gives a promise.

He ends with, 'Take therefore no thought for the morrow: for the morrow shall take thought for the things of itself.' Live one day at a time, trusting your Father.

Now, three verses to hold on to.

Verse 9. 'After this manner therefore pray ye: Our Father which art in heaven, Hallowed be thy name.' After this manner means, like this. Jesus gave us a pattern, not a magic formula. And it begins with two words that change everything. Our Father. Jesus invites us to speak to the almighty God the way a child speaks to a loving father. And the prayer puts God first, his name, his kingdom, his will, before we ask for our own needs.

Verse 21. 'For where your treasure is, there will your heart be also.' What we treasure pulls our hearts towards it. If our greatest treasure is money, or things, or what people think of us, our hearts will follow those. If our treasure is God and his kingdom, our hearts will follow him. A good question to ask is this: what do I think about most, and spend most on? That shows where my treasure is.

Verse 33. 'But seek ye first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness; and all these things shall be added unto you.' This is the answer to worry. Jesus does not say food and clothes do not matter. He says, put God first, and trust him with the rest. Remember Haggai’s people, who put their own houses first and never had enough. Jesus shows us the better way. Seek God first, and he will take care of what we need.

The psalmist wrote, 'Cast thy burden upon the LORD, and he shall sustain thee'. And David said, 'I have been young, and now am old; yet have I not seen the righteous forsaken, nor his seed begging bread.'

What are you worried about right now? Name it to your Father in prayer, just as Jesus taught. Then try praying the Lord’s Prayer slowly, thinking about each line. And look for one way to give or help someone today, quietly, where only God will see.

Here is today’s question. Jesus said, where your treasure is, there will your heart be also. What do you treasure most, and does it pull your heart towards God or away from him?

Let us pray. Our Father which art in heaven, hallowed be thy name. Thy kingdom come, thy will be done, in my life as it is in heaven. Forgive me, as I forgive others. Help me to seek you first, and to trust you with the days ahead. Amen.

Next on the walk, Matthew chapter seven. Jesus finishes his great sermon with a golden rule, a narrow gate, and a story about two builders, one wise and one foolish.
