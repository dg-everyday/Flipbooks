The Living Voice. Nehemiah, chapter 1. Let Thine Ear Now Be Attentive.

A visitor comes into the palace, tired from a long journey. A young official greets him warmly. It is his brother, back from the land of their fathers. Tell me, he says. How are our people? How is Jerusalem? And his brother looks down at the floor. Not good, he says. Not good at all.

In the introduction, we heard that the book of Nehemiah tells how the walls of Jerusalem were rebuilt, by a cupbearer from the palace of Persia. We heard that it is mostly told in Nehemiah’s own words. And we heard three things to watch for: prayer, perseverance, and the words, remember me, O my God.

Now, in chapter one, Nehemiah hears the news from Jerusalem. Listen for how he responds. And listen to his prayer.

The book begins, 'The words of Nehemiah the son of Hachaliah.' And then Nehemiah tells us where he was. In Shushan the palace, the winter palace of the kings of Persia. This was the same city where Queen Esther had lived, not long before.

One of Nehemiah’s brothers, Hanani, arrives with some men from Judah. And Nehemiah asks them about the Jews who had escaped the exile, and about Jerusalem.

And they tell him. 'The remnant that are left of the captivity there in the province are in great affliction and reproach: the wall of Jerusalem also is broken down, and the gates thereof are burned with fire.'

Great affliction means great trouble. Reproach means shame. The people who had gone home were struggling, and they were mocked by their neighbours. And the walls, broken down by Nebuchadnezzar all those years ago, were still in ruins. The gates were still burned. The city was open and helpless.

Nehemiah could have said, how sad, and gone back to his comfortable life. Instead, something happened in his heart.

For days, Nehemiah prays. And the writer gives us his prayer. It is worth listening to carefully, because it is a model of how to pray.

He begins with who God is. 'I beseech thee, O LORD God of heaven, the great and terrible God, that keepeth covenant and mercy for them that love him and observe his commandments'. Terrible here means awesome, causing wonder and fear. God is great. And God keeps his promises.

Then he asks God to listen. 'Let thine ear now be attentive, and thine eyes open, that thou mayest hear the prayer of thy servant, which I pray before thee now, day and night'.

Then he confesses sin. Not just the sins of the people, but his own. 'both I and my father’s house have sinned. We have dealt very corruptly against thee'. Like Ezra, Nehemiah includes himself.

Then he reminds God of his promise. Remember the word you gave to Moses. If you are unfaithful, I will scatter you among the nations.

Nehemiah was not telling God anything God did not know. He was holding on to God’s promise, and praying it back to him.

'Now these are thy servants and thy people, whom thou hast redeemed by thy great power, and by thy strong hand.' They are still yours, Nehemiah says. You rescued them. Do not forget them.

And then, at the very end, Nehemiah makes a specific request.

And then a final line, that explains everything. 'For I was the king’s cupbearer.'

A cupbearer had a very important job. He served the king’s wine, and tasted it first, to make sure no one had poisoned it. He had to be completely trustworthy. And because he was close to the king every day, he could speak to him. Nehemiah was in exactly the right place, to ask exactly the right person, for exactly the right help. God had put him there.

But before Nehemiah spoke to the king, he spoke to the King of kings.

Now, three verses to hold on to.

Verse 4. 'And it came to pass, when I heard these words, that I sat down and wept, and mourned certain days, and fasted, and prayed before the God of heaven'. Nehemiah did not just feel sorry. He wept, and mourned, and fasted, and prayed. For days. He let the needs of God’s people break his heart. Before God uses someone to fix a problem, he often first lets them feel it. Caring deeply is where God’s work often begins.

Verse 9. 'But if ye turn unto me, and keep my commandments, and do them; though there were of you cast out unto the uttermost part of the heaven, yet will I gather them from thence, and will bring them unto the place that I have chosen to set my name there.' This is God’s promise, through Moses. Even if you are scattered to the ends of the earth, if you turn back to me, I will gather you. Nehemiah built his whole prayer on this promise. The best prayers stand on what God has already said.

Verse 11. 'O Lord, I beseech thee, let now thine ear be attentive to the prayer of thy servant, and to the prayer of thy servants, who desire to fear thy name: and prosper, I pray thee, thy servant this day, and grant him mercy in the sight of this man. For I was the king’s cupbearer.' This man was the king of Persia, the most powerful man in the world. To Nehemiah, he was just this man. Nehemiah knew who was really in charge. He asked God to give him favour with the king. And notice, he did not only pray. He was ready to act, and to be part of the answer to his own prayer.

James wrote, 'The effectual fervent prayer of a righteous man availeth much.' And the psalmist sang, 'If I forget thee, O Jerusalem, let my right hand forget her cunning.'

Is there a need that God has laid on your heart? Bring it to him, using Nehemiah’s pattern. Start with who God is. Confess your sin. Hold on to his promises. And ask him for something specific. Then be ready to be part of the answer.

Here is today’s question. Nehemiah began his prayer with who God is, then confessed sin, then held on to God’s promise, and only then made his request. How could this pattern help your own prayers?

Let us pray. O LORD God of heaven, you are great and awesome, and you keep your promises to those who love you. I confess my sins to you. Let your ear be attentive to my prayer. Give me a heart that breaks for what breaks yours. And use me, Lord, to be part of the answer. Amen.
