The Living Voice. Nehemiah, chapter 2. The Good Hand of My God upon Me.

In the dark, a few men slip quietly out of a sleeping city. One rides a donkey. The others walk beside him. They do not carry torches. They do not talk. They pick their way along the bottom of a broken wall, past burned gates and heaps of rubble, until the rubble is so thick that even the donkey cannot get through. The man on the donkey is looking at everything, and saying nothing. He is making a plan.

In chapter one, Nehemiah, the king’s cupbearer in the palace of Persia, heard that the walls of Jerusalem were still broken down, and the gates burned. He sat down and wept, and fasted and prayed for days. He confessed the sins of his people, held on to God’s promises, and asked God to give him favour with the king.

Now, in chapter two, Nehemiah’s prayer is answered. Listen for a very short prayer. And listen for what Nehemiah does when he reaches Jerusalem.

Months pass. Then one day, Nehemiah is serving wine to king Artaxerxes, as usual. But he cannot hide his sadness any more. He had never looked sad in the king’s presence before.

And the king notices. Why is your face sad, since you are not sick? This must be sorrow of heart.

'Then I was very sore afraid'. Why was he afraid? Because in those days, servants were expected always to look happy in front of the king. A sad face could be taken as a sign of disloyalty, or a plot. It could cost him his job, or even his life.

But Nehemiah answers honestly. Let the king live for ever. Why should my face not be sad, when the city where my fathers are buried lies in ruins, and its gates have been burned with fire?

And the king says, 'For what dost thou make request?' What do you want?

And then come four little words, which say so much. 'So I prayed to the God of heaven.'

Right there, in front of the king, in the middle of the conversation, in the time it takes to breathe, Nehemiah sends up a quick, silent prayer. He had prayed for months. Now he prays again, in an instant. Then he answers the king. If it pleases the king, send me to Judah, to the city of my fathers, so that I can rebuild it.

The king, with the queen sitting beside him, asks, how long will your journey take, and when will you come back? Nehemiah gives him a time. He had planned this carefully.

Then Nehemiah asks for more. Letters to the governors of the region, so they will let him pass safely. And a letter to the keeper of the king’s forest, to give him timber for the gates, for the wall, and for his own house.

And the king grants it all.

The king even sends army officers and horsemen with him. And Nehemiah sets off for Jerusalem.

But not everyone is pleased. When Sanballat, a governor from the region of Samaria, and Tobiah, an official from Ammon, hear about it, they are very upset that someone has come to help the people of Israel.

Nehemiah arrives in Jerusalem, and rests for three days. He tells no one why he has come. And then he goes out, secretly, in the dark.

He rides out through the valley gate, along the walls, past the gates that had been burned. Then he comes to a place where there is so much rubble that his donkey cannot get through. So he goes along the valley instead, looking at the wall, and then turns back. The city officials have no idea where he has been, or what he is doing.

Then Nehemiah calls the people together.

And he tells them how the good hand of his God had been upon him, and what the king had said. And they reply, let us rise up and build! So they strengthen their hands for this good work.

But Sanballat and Tobiah, and Geshem the Arab, hear about it, and they laugh at them, and mock them. What are you doing? Are you rebelling against the king?

And Nehemiah answers boldly. 'The God of heaven, he will prosper us; therefore we his servants will arise and build'. But you have no share, no right, and no claim in Jerusalem.

Now, three verses to hold on to.

Verse 8. 'And a letter unto Asaph the keeper of the king’s forest, that he may give me timber to make beams for the gates of the palace which appertained to the house, and for the wall of the city, and for the house that I shall enter into. And the king granted me, according to the good hand of my God upon me.' Nehemiah asked the king for big things. And he got them. But he did not say, the king was generous, or, I was clever. He said, according to the good hand of my God upon me. When good things come, Nehemiah, like Ezra, gives God the credit.

Verse 12. 'And I arose in the night, I and some few men with me; neither told I any man what my God had put in my heart to do at Jerusalem: neither was there any beast with me, save the beast that I rode upon.' What my God had put in my heart. Nehemiah’s plan came from God. And notice how wisely he acted. He did not rush in and make speeches. He went quietly, looked carefully, and learned the size of the problem first. Faith and careful planning go together.

Verse 17. 'Then said I unto them, Ye see the distress that we are in, how Jerusalem lieth waste, and the gates thereof are burned with fire: come, and let us build up the wall of Jerusalem, that we be no more a reproach.' Notice the words, we, and let us. Nehemiah did not say, you need to fix this. He said, we are in distress, let us build. He joined them. A good leader stands with his people, and works beside them.

Solomon wrote, 'A man’s heart deviseth his way: but the LORD directeth his steps.' And Paul wrote, 'Pray without ceasing.'

Try praying a quick, silent prayer today, in the middle of a conversation or a task, like Nehemiah. And if God has put something in your heart to do, think and plan carefully, and then step out in faith.

Here is today’s question. Nehemiah prayed for months, and then sent up a quick prayer in a moment of need. How could quick prayers fit into your everyday life?

Let us pray. Lord God, thank you that you hear both my long prayers and my short ones. When I am afraid, help me to send a quick prayer to you. Put in my heart what you want me to do, and give me wisdom to plan it well. Let your good hand be upon me, and help me to work alongside others for your glory. Amen.
