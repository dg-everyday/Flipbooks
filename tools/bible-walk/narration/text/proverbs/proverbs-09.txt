The Living Voice. Proverbs, chapter 9. Wisdom Hath Builded Her House.

Two houses stand on the same hill, at either end of the same street. Outside each one, someone is calling to the people passing by, with exactly the same words. Come in here! Come in! One house has a great feast laid out on the table, with bread and wine and plenty for all. The other house promises secret pleasures. From the outside, it is hard to tell them apart. But one leads to life, and the other does not.

In chapter eight, Wisdom stood at the city gates and called out to everyone. My instruction is better than silver, she said, and wisdom is better than rubies. She told how she was with God when he made the world, rejoicing before him. And she said, whoso findeth me findeth life.

Now, in chapter nine, the first part of Proverbs comes to an end. Listen for two invitations, from two very different hosts. And listen for how wise people and foolish people react when they are corrected.

The chapter begins with a picture of a grand house.

'Wisdom hath builded her house, she hath hewn out her seven pillars'. Seven is often the number of completeness in the Bible. Wisdom’s house is complete, strong and beautiful.

She has prepared a great feast. She has prepared her meat, mixed her wine, and laid her table. Everything is ready. Then she sends out her young women to call people in, from the highest places in the city.

And who does she invite? 'Whoso is simple, let him turn in hither'. And to those who lack understanding, she says, 'Come, eat of my bread, and drink of the wine which I have mingled.' Wisdom does not invite only the clever or the important. She invites the simple, the ones who know they need help.

And her invitation has a purpose.

Then, in the middle of the chapter, come some sayings about correction. Who will listen when you try to help them see their mistakes?

'He that reproveth a scorner getteth to himself shame'. A scorner is someone who mocks anything good or serious. If you try to correct a scorner, he will just insult you. 'Reprove not a scorner, lest he hate thee: rebuke a wise man, and he will love thee.'

What a difference. A scorner hates being corrected. A wise person loves it, and is grateful.

Then comes a verse we have heard before, in chapter one, but slightly different.

'For by me thy days shall be multiplied, and the years of thy life shall be increased.' Wisdom adds life.

'If thou be wise, thou shalt be wise for thyself: but if thou scornest, thou alone shalt bear it.' Each of us is responsible for our own choice. If we choose wisdom, we are the ones who benefit. If we mock it, we are the ones who suffer.

And then, at the end of the chapter, a second host appears. 'A foolish woman is clamorous'. Clamorous means loud, noisy. She is simple, and knows nothing. She too sits at the door of her house, on a seat in the high places of the city. She too calls to the people passing by, the ones who are going straight on their way.

And listen to what she says. 'Whoso is simple, let him turn in hither'. Exactly the same words as Wisdom! Folly copies Wisdom’s invitation. Temptation often sounds like something good.

But what Folly offers is different. 'Stolen waters are sweet, and bread eaten in secret is pleasant.' She offers the thrill of doing something forbidden. Things taken that do not belong to us. Pleasures enjoyed in secret, because we know they are wrong. That sounds exciting.

But the people who go into her house do not know where it leads. Her house leads down to death.

So the first nine chapters of Proverbs end with two houses, two hosts, two invitations, using the same words. One offers a feast of life. The other offers stolen water, sweet for a moment, and then death. Every one of us will hear both invitations. And every one of us must choose which door to walk through.

From chapter ten, the book changes. Instead of long speeches, there are hundreds of short sayings. But the choice is always the same. Wisdom or folly. Life or death.

Now, three verses to hold on to.

Verse 6. 'Forsake the foolish, and live; and go in the way of understanding.' Forsake means leave behind. Wisdom’s invitation is not only, come to my feast. It is also, leave foolishness behind. Sometimes that means leaving foolish habits, or foolish friends, or foolish ways of thinking. And then, walk in the way of understanding. It is a choice that leads to life.

Verse 9. 'Give instruction to a wise man, and he will be yet wiser: teach a just man, and he will increase in learning.' Wise people keep growing. When you give them advice, they take it, and become even wiser. The truly wise are always willing to learn. The moment we think we know it all, we stop growing. A teachable heart is one of the surest signs of wisdom.

Verse 10. 'The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom: and the knowledge of the holy is understanding.' The beginning of wisdom is the fear of the LORD, respect and awe for God. And the knowledge of the holy, knowing God himself, is understanding. Wisdom is not just knowing facts, or having good sense. At its heart, it is knowing God. The first nine chapters began with the fear of the LORD, and they end with it too.

Isaiah heard God say, 'Ho, every one that thirsteth, come ye to the waters'. And Jesus said, 'I am the bread of life: he that cometh to me shall never hunger'.

Which invitation are you hearing loudest today? Wisdom’s, or Folly’s? Remember that Folly often uses the same words as Wisdom. And when someone corrects you today, try to respond like the wise, with thanks, not anger.

Here is today’s question. Wisdom and Folly both call out, whoso is simple, let him turn in hither. How can you tell the difference between them, when they sound so alike?

Let us pray. Lord God, thank you for the feast of wisdom you have prepared, and that you invite even the simple to come in. Help me to forsake foolishness, and walk in the way of understanding. Give me a teachable heart, that loves correction. Keep me from the stolen waters of Folly. And let me know you, the Holy One, for that is understanding. Amen.
