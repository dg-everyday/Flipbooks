The Living Voice. Meet the Book of Psalms.

A shepherd boy sits on a hillside, watching his sheep. In his hands is a small harp. As the sun goes down behind the hills, he begins to play, and to sing. He sings about the stars he can see, and about the God who made them. He sings about the lions and bears that come for his sheep, and the God who protects him. Years later, that boy would become a king. And many of his songs are still sung today, all over the world.

Welcome to the Psalms, on The Living Voice. The book of Psalms is the longest book in the Bible, with one hundred and fifty psalms. On this walk, we will not walk through all of them. Instead, we have chosen thirty favourites, some of the best known and best loved psalms, and we will walk through them one at a time.

A psalm is a song, or a poem, or a prayer. The book of Psalms was Israel’s song book and prayer book. People sang them in the temple, prayed them at home, and sang them as they walked to Jerusalem for the great feasts. Jesus sang them too.

Many of the psalms were written by David, the shepherd boy who became king. We have walked through his story in the books of Samuel. Others were written by Asaph, one of David’s musicians, and by a family of singers called the sons of Korah. One was written by Moses. Some by Solomon. And many by people whose names we do not know. Many psalms begin with a short title, telling us who wrote them, or what was happening when they were written.

The psalms are poetry. And Hebrew poetry has a special pattern. Instead of rhyming sounds, it often rhymes ideas. A psalm says something, and then says it again in different words. Listen. The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork. The second half echoes the first. Once you notice it, you will hear it everywhere.

You will also hear a strange word, Selah. No one is completely sure what it means. It may have been a musical sign, perhaps a pause. When you hear it, it is a good moment to stop and think.

What makes the psalms so special is how honest they are. They bring every feeling to God. Joy and thankfulness, yes. But also fear, loneliness, sorrow, guilt, anger, and even feeling that God has forgotten us. The psalms show us that we can bring anything to God, just as we are. And they teach us words to pray, when we do not know what to say.

Jesus prayed the psalms. When he hung on the cross, he cried out words from Psalm twenty two. My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? Many psalms point forward to him, the true King, the Son of David.

Our thirty psalms are arranged by theme, like a journey. We begin with the two ways of life, and God’s Word. Then God the Creator. Then trusting God, our shepherd, our rock and our refuge. Then the God who knows us completely. Then psalms for sorrow and hard times. Then psalms of repentance and forgiveness. Then praise, and thanksgiving, and worship. And we end, as the book of Psalms ends, with everything that has breath praising the LORD.

Here are four things to watch for as we walk.

Watch for honesty. The psalmists do not pretend. Listen to how openly they speak to God.

Watch for pictures of God. A shepherd. A rock. A fortress. A shield. A refuge. A light.

Watch for the turn. Many sad psalms begin in darkness, and end in trust or praise. Something changes as they pray.

And watch for Jesus. Many psalms point to him, the King, the suffering servant, the good shepherd.

Here is a question to carry with you. The psalms bring every feeling to God, even the hard ones. Is there a feeling you find hard to bring to God, and what might the psalms teach you about that?

Let us pray. Lord God, as I walk through these psalms, teach me to pray. Help me to bring every feeling to you, honestly, the joy and the sorrow. Show me who you are: my shepherd, my rock, my refuge and my light. And put a new song in my heart. Amen.
