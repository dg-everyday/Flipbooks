# 1 Kings 20: Ben-hadad besieges Samaria with thirty two kings, his demands, the elders say do not consent, let not him that girdeth on his harness boast, a prophet promises victory by the young men, Ben-hadad drinking (not "drunk"), the Syrians flee; gods of the hills, Aphek, two little flocks of kids, God of the valleys, victory (no numbers of dead), Ben-hadad in sackcloth, he is my brother, the covenant, the wounded prophet's parable (20:35-36 lion not told), heavy and displeased. Keys 20:11, 20:28, 20:43.
TITLE: The LORD Is God of the Valleys

HOOK: A proud king sits in his tent with thirty two other kings, feasting and drinking. Outside, his huge army surrounds a small city. He has already sent a message: everything you have is mine. He is so sure he has won. And then a scout runs in. Some men are coming out of the city. Only a few hundred of them. Take them alive, the king laughs. It will be the last time he laughs that day.

WHERE: In chapter nineteen, Elijah fled from Jezebel into the desert, and asked God to let him die. An angel fed him, and said, arise and eat, because the journey is too great for thee. On Mount Horeb, God spoke to him in a still small voice, and told him there were still seven thousand in Israel who had not bowed to Baal. And Elijah called Elisha to follow him.

Now, in chapter twenty, we return to king Ahab. Listen for how God helps even this wicked king. And listen for a choice Ahab makes that he will regret.

STORY: Ben-hadad, king of Syria, gathers his whole army, with thirty two other kings and their horses and chariots, and surrounds Samaria. And he sends a message to Ahab. Your silver and gold are mine. Your wives and children are mine.

Ahab gives in at once. But Ben-hadad wants more. His servants will search the palace, he says, and take whatever they like. Now even Ahab sees this is too much, and the elders tell him, do not agree. So Ahab refuses.

Ben-hadad boasts that he will flatten Samaria to dust. And Ahab answers with a famous saying.

Then, unexpectedly, a prophet comes to Ahab. 'Thus saith the LORD, Hast thou seen all this great multitude? behold, I will deliver it into thine hand this day; and thou shalt know that I am the LORD.' God was going to help Ahab, not because Ahab deserved it, but so that he would know who the LORD is.

How? Ahab asks. By the young men who serve the district governors, the prophet says. Who will start the battle? You will.

So Ahab gathers the young men, only a few hundred of them, and behind them the small army of Israel. They march out in the middle of the day. And Ben-hadad and his kings are sitting in their tents, drinking. Take them alive, Ben-hadad says, whether they come for peace or for war. But the young men of Israel attack, and the Syrian army panics and runs. Ben-hadad escapes on a horse. Israel wins a great victory.

Then the prophet comes back to Ahab. Get ready, he says. The king of Syria will come back next spring.

Meanwhile, Ben-hadad’s officials have an explanation for their defeat. Their gods are gods of the hills, they say. That is why they were stronger than us. Let us fight them on the flat land, in the plain, and we will surely win. So Ben-hadad rebuilds his army, horse for horse, and chariot for chariot.

The next spring, the Syrians come to a place called Aphek, in the plain. And the army of Israel goes out to meet them. 'and the children of Israel pitched before them like two little flocks of kids; but the Syrians filled the country.' Two little flocks of baby goats, facing an army that filled the whole land.

And then a man of God comes to Ahab.

On the seventh day, the battle begins, and Israel wins an even greater victory. The Syrians who escape run into the city of Aphek, and the city wall falls on many of them.

Ben-hadad runs into the city, and hides in an inner room. His servants say, we have heard that the kings of Israel are merciful. Let us put on sackcloth and ropes, and go to the king of Israel and beg for your life. So they do. Your servant Ben-hadad says, please let me live.

And Ahab says, is he still alive? He is my brother. Bring him here. And when Ben-hadad comes out, Ahab invites him up into his chariot. They make a treaty. Ben-hadad promises to give back the cities his father took from Israel, and Ahab lets him go.

It sounds generous. It was not God’s will. God had given Ben-hadad, Israel’s great enemy, into Ahab’s hand. And Ahab let him go, for a trade deal.

So God sends a prophet, wounded and disguised with ashes on his face. He tells the king a story. I was given a prisoner to guard, on pain of my life. But while I was busy here and there, he was gone. And Ahab says, you have decided your own judgment.

Then the prophet wipes off the ashes, and says, thus saith the LORD, because you let go the man I had appointed for judgment, your life will go for his life, and your people for his people.

KEY 20:11: 'And the king of Israel answered and said, Tell him, Let not him that girdeth on his harness boast himself as he that putteth it off.' Harness here means armour. Do not boast when you are putting your armour on before the battle, as if you had already taken it off after winning. Ben-hadad was celebrating before the fight had even started. Pride often comes just before a fall. Wait until the battle is over before you boast.

KEY 20:28: 'And there came a man of God, and spake unto the king of Israel, and said, Thus saith the LORD, Because the Syrians have said, The LORD is God of the hills, but he is not God of the valleys, therefore will I deliver all this great multitude into thine hand, and ye shall know that I am the LORD.' The Syrians thought every god ruled over its own little area. But the LORD is not a small, local god. He is God of the hills and the valleys, the mountain tops and the low places. Wherever you are today, high or low, he is God there too.

KEY 20:43: 'And the king of Israel went to his house heavy and displeased, and came to Samaria.' Heavy and displeased. Ahab did not repent. He sulked. God had given him two great victories, and sent prophets to speak to him. But when God corrected him, he went home in a bad mood. We will see that mood again in the very next chapter. Sulking when God corrects us is the opposite of turning back to him.

THREAD: The psalmist wrote, 'I will lift up mine eyes unto the hills, from whence cometh my help. My help cometh from the LORD, which made heaven and earth.' And David wrote, 'Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil: for thou art with me'.

TODAY: Are you in a valley right now, a low and hard place? Remember that the LORD is God of the valleys too. He is with you there. And if God has corrected you about something, do not sulk. Turn back to him.

QUESTION: The Syrians thought God was only God of the hills. Where in your life do you find it hardest to believe that God is with you?

PRAYER: Lord God, you are God of the hills and God of the valleys. There is nowhere I can go where you are not. Thank you that you help even when we do not deserve it. Keep me from boasting before the battle is won. And when you correct me, give me a humble heart that turns back to you, instead of going away heavy and displeased. Amen.
