# 2 Samuel 24: the census (Chronicles' angle noted gently), Joab objects, David's heart smote him, I have done very foolishly, Gad and the three choices, let us fall into the hand of the LORD for his mercies are great, the plague (no count), it is enough, these sheep what have they done, Araunah's threshingfloor, not of that which doth cost me nothing, the plague stayed; looking back over 1 and 2 Samuel and ahead. Keys 24:10, 24:14, 24:24.
TITLE: Let Us Fall Now into the Hand of the LORD

HOOK: A king stands on a hill outside his city, looking up. Above the city, he sees an angel, with a sword stretched out over Jerusalem. Below, his people are suffering, because of his sin. And the king cries out, I am the one who sinned. These people are like sheep. What have they done? Let your hand be against me instead.

WHERE: In chapter twenty three, we heard the last words of David, the sweet psalmist of Israel. He that ruleth over men must be just. God hath made with me an everlasting covenant, ordered in all things, and sure. Three mighty men brought David water from the well of Bethlehem, and he poured it out to the LORD. And the last name among his mighty men was Uriah the Hittite.

Now we come to the last chapter of the second book of Samuel. Listen for how David sins one more time, and how he repents. And listen for where this book ends, and why it matters.

STORY: The anger of the LORD is kindled against Israel again. And David is moved to say, go and count the people of Israel and Judah.

Another book of the Bible, the first book of Chronicles, tells the same story from a different angle. What is clear is that David’s decision to count the people was wrong. Perhaps he wanted to boast about the size of his kingdom, or to trust in the number of his soldiers, instead of in God.

David says to Joab, go through all the tribes of Israel, from Dan to Beer-sheba, and count the people, so that I may know how many there are.

Even Joab, the tough commander, objects. May the LORD your God multiply the people a hundred times over, and may my lord the king see it. But why does my lord the king want to do this?

But the king’s word wins. So Joab and the commanders go through the whole land, and count the people.

And then David’s conscience wakes up.

Once again, David does not make excuses. He confesses at once.

The word of the LORD comes to the prophet Gad, and Gad goes to David. God offers you three choices, he says. Choose one. Shall there be years of famine in your land? Or shall you flee for months before your enemies while they chase you? Or shall there be three days of plague in your land? Think it over, and decide what answer I should give.

And David answers.

David chose to put himself in God’s hands, rather than in the hands of men. He knew that God, even in judgment, is merciful.

So the LORD sends a plague on Israel, and many people die, from Dan to Beer-sheba.

And then, as the angel stretches out his hand over Jerusalem to destroy it, the LORD feels sorrow over the disaster, and says to the angel, it is enough. Stay your hand. The angel of the LORD is standing by the threshing floor of a man named Araunah, a Jebusite.

When David sees the angel striking the people, he cries out to the LORD. I am the one who has sinned. I have done wrong. But these sheep, what have they done? Let your hand be against me, and against my father’s family.

David the shepherd. At the end of his life, he still thinks of his people as sheep. And like a good shepherd, he offers himself in their place.

That day, Gad comes to David and says, go up and build an altar to the LORD on the threshing floor of Araunah. So David goes up, as the LORD commanded.

Araunah sees the king and his servants coming, and bows down with his face to the ground. Why has my lord the king come to his servant?

David says, to buy your threshing floor, so that I can build an altar to the LORD, and the plague may stop.

Araunah offers to give the king everything for nothing, the place, the oxen and the wood.

But David answers.

So David buys the threshing floor and the oxen. He builds an altar to the LORD there, and offers burnt offerings and peace offerings. And the LORD hears the prayer for the land, and the plague stops.

And that is where the books of Samuel end. Not with a coronation or a battle, but with an altar, on a threshing floor, in Jerusalem. The Bible tells us later that on that very spot, David’s son Solomon would build the temple of the LORD.

From Hannah’s prayer to David’s altar, we have seen that God lifts up the lowly, looks on the heart, and that his mercies are great.

KEY 24:10: 'And David’s heart smote him after that he had numbered the people. And David said unto the LORD, I have sinned greatly in that I have done: and now, I beseech thee, O LORD, take away the iniquity of thy servant; for I have done very foolishly.' I have done very foolishly. Even at the end of his life, David could still sin. And even at the end, he still knew how to repent. That is the mark of a man after God’s own heart. Not that he never fell, but that when he fell, he turned straight back to God.

KEY 24:14: 'And David said unto Gad, I am in a great strait: let us fall now into the hand of the LORD; for his mercies are great: and let me not fall into the hand of man.' A great strait is a terrible dilemma. Every choice was hard. But David knew one thing for certain. God’s mercies are great. It is always better to fall into God’s hands than anyone else’s. Even when God disciplines us, he is merciful.

KEY 24:24: 'And the king said unto Araunah, Nay; but I will surely buy it of thee at a price: neither will I offer burnt offerings unto the LORD my God of that which doth cost me nothing. So David bought the threshingfloor and the oxen for fifty shekels of silver.' That which doth cost me nothing. David would not give God something that cost him nothing. True worship costs us something. Our time, our money, our pride, our comfort. And it points us to the greatest gift of all, which cost God everything: his own Son.

THREAD: Jesus, the good shepherd, said, 'I am the good shepherd: the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep.' And Jeremiah wrote, 'It is of the LORD’S mercies that we are not consumed, because his compassions fail not.'

TODAY: Is there a way you could give to God something that truly costs you, today? And whatever you are facing, put yourself in God’s hands, as David did. His mercies are great.

QUESTION: David said he would not offer to God that which cost him nothing. What does it look like for your worship and service to God to cost you something?

PRAYER: Lord God, thank you for walking with me through the books of Samuel. Thank you that your mercies are great, and that I can fall into your hands. When I sin, give me a heart that turns back to you at once. Help me to give you what truly costs me. And thank you for Jesus, the good shepherd, the Son of David, whose kingdom will never end. Amen.

NEXT: That is the end of our walk through the second book of Samuel. Well walked. You have finished another whole book of the Bible, and the story of King David. The story of David’s kingdom goes on in the books of Kings, with his son Solomon.
