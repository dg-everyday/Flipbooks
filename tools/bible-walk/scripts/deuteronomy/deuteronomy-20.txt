# Deuteronomy 20: be not afraid of horses and chariots, the priest's word: let not your hearts faint, the LORD goeth with you to fight for you, those sent home (new house, new vineyard, betrothed, the fearful), proclaim peace first, the hard commands about cities told briefly ("Christians have understood these chapters in different ways"; avoids 20:13-17 detail), spare the fruit trees in a siege. Keys 20:1, 20:4, 20:10.
TITLE: For the LORD Your God Is He That Goeth with You

HOOK: An army is lined up for battle. Across the field, the enemy has horses and chariots, more soldiers than anyone can count. Hearts are pounding. And then, before the battle, an officer walks along the line and calls out: is anyone here afraid? Go home. Is anyone here newly married? Go home. You might think that would weaken the army. But God says it is how his people go to war.

WHERE: In chapter nineteen, Moses set apart cities of refuge, so that innocent blood would not be shed. He warned, thou shalt not remove thy neighbour’s landmark. And he said that no one could be condemned except at the mouth of two or three witnesses. Jesus later taught his followers not to seek revenge at all.

Now, in chapter twenty, Moses speaks about war. This chapter includes some hard commands, which we will tell briefly. Listen for why Israel should not be afraid. And listen for what they must offer first, before any battle.

STORY: When you go out to battle against your enemies, Moses says, and see horses and chariots, and an army bigger than yours.

Horses and chariots were the most feared weapons of the time. Israel had few of either. But they had something better.

Then Moses tells what will happen before every battle. The priest will come forward, and speak to the army.

He will say, hear, O Israel. Today you are going into battle against your enemies. Do not let your hearts faint. Do not be afraid, or tremble, or be terrified because of them.

And then the priest gives the reason.

Then the officers speak to the army, and send some men home.

Has anyone built a new house, and not yet lived in it? Let him go home, in case he dies in battle and another man lives in it.

Has anyone planted a vineyard, and not yet enjoyed its fruit? Let him go home.

Is anyone engaged to be married, and not yet married? Let him go home, in case he dies in battle and another man marries her.

And then the officers ask one more question. Is anyone afraid, or fainthearted? Let him go home, so that his fear does not spread to his brothers.

What a strange way to raise an army. God sent home the people who had new beginnings in life, a new house, a new vineyard, a new marriage, so they could enjoy them. And he sent home the fearful. Why? Because the battle did not depend on the size of the army. It depended on God. Remember Gideon, whose army God made smaller and smaller, until only three hundred were left?

Then Moses gives an important command.

Before attacking any city, Israel was to offer peace first. If the city made peace, and opened its gates, its people would serve Israel, but they would live.

Then the Bible records hard commands about cities that refused peace, and about the nations of Canaan, which were to come under God’s judgment, so that they would not teach Israel to follow their detestable practices. These are among the hardest parts of the Old Testament, and Christians have understood these chapters in different ways. They belonged to a particular time, to Israel’s entry into the land God was judging. They are not a pattern for God’s people today.

And then the chapter ends with a surprising, gentle law.

When you besiege a city for a long time, Moses says, do not destroy its trees by cutting them down with an axe. You may eat their fruit, but do not cut them down. For is the tree of the field a man, that it should be besieged by you? Only trees that do not bear fruit may you cut down, to build siege works.

Even in war, God cared about his creation. The fruit trees would feed people long after the battle was over. Israel was not to destroy the land for the sake of winning a fight.

KEY 20:1: 'When thou goest out to battle against thine enemies, and seest horses, and chariots, and a people more than thou, be not afraid of them: for the LORD thy God is with thee, which brought thee up out of the land of Egypt.' The LORD thy God is with thee, which brought thee up out of Egypt. When we face something frightening, it helps to remember what God has already done. The God who brought Israel through the Red Sea would not abandon them now. And the God who has helped us before will help us again.

KEY 20:4: 'For the LORD your God is he that goeth with you, to fight for you against your enemies, to save you.' He goeth with you. He fights for you. He saves you. Israel did not face their battles alone. Neither do we. Whatever we face, God goes with us.

KEY 20:10: 'When thou comest nigh unto a city to fight against it, then proclaim peace unto it.' Peace first. Before any battle, Israel was to offer peace. God’s people are always to seek peace, and to offer it, before anything else. Jesus said, blessed are the peacemakers: for they shall be called the children of God.

THREAD: Paul wrote, 'If it be possible, as much as lieth in you, live peaceably with all men.' And he wrote, 'For the weapons of our warfare are not carnal, but mighty through God'.

TODAY: Is there a battle you are facing, a fear, a conflict, a problem that feels bigger than you? Remember today: the LORD your God goes with you, to fight for you. And if there is someone you are in conflict with, take the first step and offer peace.

QUESTION: Before every battle, the priest said, let not your hearts faint, for the LORD goes with you. What would change if you faced your own battles believing that God goes with you?

PRAYER: Lord God, you go with me, to fight for me, and to save me. When I face things bigger than I am, help me not to be afraid. Make me a peacemaker, who offers peace first. And help me to care for the good things you have made, even in hard times. Amen.
