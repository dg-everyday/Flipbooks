# Deuteronomy 24: divorce regulated briefly (Matthew 19:8, "Christians have understood… in different ways"; 24:1-4 not quoted), a new husband free at home one year, no millstone as pledge, kidnapping forbidden, Miriam remembered, pledges: stand outside, return the poor man's cloak at sundown (in quotes), wages the same day, each for his own sin, justice for stranger, fatherless and widow, the forgotten sheaf and gleanings (Ruth). Keys 24:5, 24:15, 24:19.
TITLE: It Shall Be for the Stranger, for the Fatherless, and for the Widow

HOOK: A farmer has finished harvesting his field. As he walks home, he notices one sheaf of grain lying forgotten at the edge. He could go back and get it. It is his. But he leaves it there. And not long after, a poor widow, a foreigner, comes along, and finds it, and gathers it, and her family eats that day. That farmer did exactly what God asked.

WHERE: In chapter twenty three, Moses said that the LORD turned Balaam’s curse into a blessing, because he loved his people. He said, the LORD thy God walketh in the midst of thy camp. And he told them to give refuge to a runaway servant, and let him live wherever he chose.

Now, in chapter twenty four, Moses gives laws that protect the vulnerable. Listen for the people God keeps mentioning. And listen for what the farmer is to leave behind in the field.

STORY: The chapter begins with a law about divorce. In Israel, as in the nations around, men sometimes sent their wives away. This law put limits on it, and protected the woman, by requiring a written certificate, so that she was free to remarry. Long after, when people asked Jesus about this law, he said that Moses allowed it because of the hardness of their hearts, but that from the beginning, God’s design was for husband and wife to be joined for life. Christians have understood how these teachings apply today in different ways.

Then Moses gives a lovely law.

A whole year at home, free from war and from heavy duties, to make his new wife happy, and build their life together. God valued marriage, and wanted it to have a good beginning.

Then Moses gives laws about lending. Do not take a man’s millstone as a pledge, as security for a loan. The millstone ground the family’s flour every day. Taking it would be taking their very life.

Anyone found kidnapping a fellow Israelite, and selling him as a slave, was to be put to death. Stealing a person was one of the gravest crimes.

Moses reminds them to follow the priests’ instructions about skin diseases, and to remember what happened to Miriam on the journey.

Then Moses gives some of the most caring laws in the whole book.

When you lend your neighbour something, Moses says, do not go into his house to take his pledge. Stand outside, and let him bring it out to you.

Even a poor borrower had a right to dignity in his own home.

And if the man is poor, and gives you his cloak as a pledge, do not keep it overnight. 'In any case thou shalt deliver him the pledge again when the sun goeth down, that he may sleep in his own raiment'. And he will bless you, and it will be counted as righteousness before the LORD your God.

A poor man’s cloak might be his only blanket. God would not let him go cold, even for a loan.

Then Moses speaks about workers.

Do not oppress a hired worker who is poor and needy, whether he is an Israelite or a foreigner living in your town.

Then Moses gives a principle of justice. Fathers are not to be put to death for their children, nor children for their fathers. Each person is responsible for his own sin.

And again, Moses speaks for the most vulnerable. Do not twist justice for the foreigner or the fatherless. Do not take a widow’s cloak as a pledge. Remember that you were a slave in Egypt, and the LORD your God rescued you.

And then comes the law of the harvest.

And the same with olives, Moses says. When you beat your olive trees to knock down the fruit, do not go over the branches a second time. Leave the rest for the stranger, the fatherless and the widow. And when you harvest your grapes, do not go back over the vineyard. Leave the rest for them.

Remember why, Moses says. You were slaves in Egypt. Therefore I command you to do this.

Remember Ruth? She was a widow and a foreigner. And she went out to glean in the fields of Boaz, gathering what the harvesters left behind. Boaz even told his workers to leave extra on purpose for her. Boaz was keeping this very law. And through it, God provided for Ruth, and led her into his great story.

KEY 24:5: 'When a man hath taken a new wife, he shall not go out to war, neither shall he be charged with any business: but he shall be free at home one year, and shall cheer up his wife which he hath taken.' Cheer up his wife. God cared about the happiness of a new marriage. He wanted husbands and wives to have time to build their love. Making time for those we love is something God values.

KEY 24:15: 'At his day thou shalt give him his hire, neither shall the sun go down upon it; for he is poor, and setteth his heart upon it: lest he cry against thee unto the LORD, and it be sin unto thee.' Hire means wages. A poor worker needed his pay that same day, to feed his family. Holding back wages was a sin that God heard. James later warned, the hire of the labourers… crieth: and the cries of them… are entered into the ears of the Lord.

KEY 24:19: 'When thou cuttest down thine harvest in thy field, and hast forgot a sheaf in the field, thou shalt not go again to fetch it: it shall be for the stranger, for the fatherless, and for the widow: that the LORD thy God may bless thee in all the work of thine hands.' Leave something for others. God built generosity into the harvest. Not everything had to be gathered for yourself. The edges, the forgotten sheaf, the last grapes, were for the poor. And God promised to bless those who shared.

THREAD: James wrote, 'Behold, the hire of the labourers who have reaped down your fields, which is of you kept back by fraud, crieth'. And the book of Ruth says that Ruth 'went, and came, and gleaned in the field after the reapers'.

TODAY: Is there something you could leave for others today, time, money, or something you could share? And if you owe anyone money or pay, settle it promptly. Remember the poor, as God does.

QUESTION: God told farmers to leave the forgotten sheaf and the last fruit for the stranger, the fatherless and the widow. What could leaving something for others look like in your life?

PRAYER: Lord God, you care for the poor, the stranger, the fatherless and the widow. Make me generous, leaving something for others, not gathering everything for myself. Help me to treat workers fairly, and to protect the dignity of those in need. And remind me always that you rescued me. Amen.
