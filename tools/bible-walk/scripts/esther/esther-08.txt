# Esther 8: Haman's house given to Esther, the ring given to Mordecai, Esther pleads with tears, the law that cannot be reversed, a new decree to stand for their lives, Mordecai in royal apparel, light and gladness. Keys 8:6, 8:16, 8:17.
TITLE: The Jews Had Light, and Gladness

HOOK: The villain is gone. The gallows has been used. It sounds like the end of the story. But it is not. Somewhere in every province of the empire, a law is still waiting, sealed with the king’s own ring. And on the day it names, anyone who wishes may attack the Jewish people. Not even the king can undo it. So what can be done?

WHERE: In chapter seven, at the second banquet, Esther finally made her request: let my life be given me, and my people. She named the enemy: this wicked Haman. The king was furious, and Haman was hanged on the gallows he had built for Mordecai.

Now, in chapter eight, the danger still remains, because Haman’s law cannot be changed. Esther goes to the king again, and a way is found. Listen for how mourning is turned into joy, and for how Mordecai’s sackcloth is changed for royal robes.

STORY: On that same day, King Ahasuerus gives the house of Haman, the Jews’ enemy, to Esther the queen. And Mordecai comes before the king, because Esther has told him how Mordecai is related to her.

The king takes off his signet ring, the one he had taken back from Haman, and gives it to Mordecai. And Esther puts Mordecai in charge of Haman’s house.

The ring that once sealed the law to destroy the Jews is now on the hand of a Jew.

But Esther’s work is not done. She speaks again before the king. She falls down at his feet, and begs him with tears to put an end to the evil plan of Haman the Agagite, which he had devised against the Jews.

Esther is now safe herself. Haman is gone, and the king favours her. She could have stopped there. But she does not rest while her people are still in danger.

The king holds out the golden sceptre toward Esther once again. So Esther rises, and stands before the king.

She says, if it please the king, and if I have found favour in his sight, and the thing seems right to the king, and I am pleasing in his eyes, let it be written to reverse the letters Haman wrote to destroy the Jews in all the king’s provinces.

And she tells him why.

But there is a problem. The king says to Esther and to Mordecai, look, I have given Esther the house of Haman, and he has been hanged, because he laid his hand upon the Jews. Now write whatever you wish concerning the Jews, in the king’s name, and seal it with the king’s ring. But remember: 'for the writing which is written in the king’s name, and sealed with the king’s ring, may no man reverse.'

Haman’s law cannot be cancelled. But a second law can be written, to answer it.

So the king’s scribes are called, and a new decree is written, just as Mordecai commands. It goes out to the Jews, and to the governors and rulers of all the hundred and twenty seven provinces, from India to Ethiopia, to every province in its own writing, and to every people in its own language, and to the Jews in their own writing and language.

It is written in the king’s name, and sealed with the king’s ring. And it is sent out by messengers on swift horses, the fastest in the royal service.

The new law gives the Jews in every city the right to gather together and stand for their lives. On the very day that Haman’s law appointed for their destruction, they may defend themselves against any people or province that attacks them.

Haman’s law said that the Jews could be attacked. The new law says that they may fight back. They are no longer helpless. And everyone in the empire now knows that the king, and his new chief minister, are on the side of the Jews.

The messengers ride out in haste, pressed on by the king’s command. And the decree is given in Shushan the palace.

Then Mordecai goes out from the presence of the king. And what a sight he is. He wears royal robes of blue and white, with a great crown of gold, and a garment of fine linen and purple. A few chapters ago, Mordecai sat at the king’s gate in sackcloth and ashes, crying a loud and bitter cry. Now he walks out in royal robes. 'and the city of Shushan rejoiced and was glad.' Remember, when Haman’s decree went out, the city was perplexed. Now the city is glad.

And among the Jews, the darkness lifts.

KEY 8:6: 'For how can I endure to see the evil that shall come unto my people? or how can I endure to see the destruction of my kindred?' Esther could have been satisfied with her own safety. But she could not bear to see her people suffer. She went back to the king, with tears. True love does not stop at saving ourselves. It cares for others, and weeps for them, and keeps pleading for them. Who do you care about so much that you cannot bear to see them suffer? Bring them to the King.

KEY 8:16: 'The Jews had light, and gladness, and joy, and honour.' Four beautiful words. Light, after the darkness of the death sentence. Gladness and joy, after mourning and fasting. And honour, after being despised. Not long before, in every province there had been weeping and wailing, and many lay in sackcloth and ashes. Now there is light. This is what God does for his people. He turns darkness into light, and mourning into joy.

KEY 8:17: 'And in every province, and in every city, whithersoever the king’s commandment and his decree came, the Jews had joy and gladness, a feast and a good day. And many of the people of the land became Jews; for the fear of the Jews fell upon them.' Many of the people of the land became Jews. They saw what had happened to this people, and they wanted to belong to them, and to their God. When God rescues his people, others take notice. Our joy in God can draw others to him.

THREAD: A psalm says, 'weeping may endure for a night, but joy cometh in the morning.' And Isaiah wrote that the LORD would 'give unto them beauty for ashes, the oil of joy for mourning, the garment of praise for the spirit of heaviness'.

TODAY: Esther did not stop pleading until her people were safe. Who are you praying for, who still needs God’s rescue? Do not give up. Keep bringing them to God, with tears if need be. And if you are in a season of mourning, hold on to God’s promise: joy cometh.

QUESTION: Mordecai’s sackcloth was changed for royal robes, and the Jews had light, and gladness, and joy. How has God turned sorrow into joy in your life, or how are you hoping he will?

PRAYER: Lord God, thank you that you turn darkness into light, and mourning into joy. Thank you that you give beauty for ashes. Give me a heart like Esther’s, that cannot bear to see others suffer, and that keeps pleading for them. And let my joy in you draw others to you. Amen.
