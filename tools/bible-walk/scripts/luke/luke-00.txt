# Luke intro: the doctor who wrote an orderly account. From tools/books/content/gospels_acts.py.
TITLE: Meet the Book of Luke

HOOK: A doctor sits down to write. He did not walk with Jesus himself. But he has spent a long time listening to people who did: fishermen, women who followed Jesus, perhaps even Jesus’ own mother. He has checked their stories carefully. And now he writes to a friend named Theophilus, so that he 'mightest know the certainty of those things' he has been taught.

BODY: Welcome to the book of Luke, on The Living Voice. Luke is the longest of the four Gospels, the books that tell the life of Jesus. It has twenty-four chapters, so on this walk it is twenty-four steps, one chapter at a time.

Who wrote it? The book does not give its writer’s name, but the early Christians said it was Luke, a doctor and a travelling companion of the apostle Paul. Paul calls him 'Luke, the beloved physician'. Luke also wrote a second book, the Acts of the Apostles, which tells what happened after Jesus rose and returned to heaven. Luke and Acts belong together, like two volumes of one story.

How did he write it? Luke tells us himself, in his very first sentences. Many people, he says, had already written about Jesus, using what eyewitnesses had passed on. So Luke traced everything carefully from the beginning, and set it down in order. He names the rulers of the day, so that his readers would know these things really happened, in real places, under real kings and governors.

Who was it written for? Luke wrote to Theophilus, whose name means friend of God, and to readers like him who were not Jews. So Luke loves to show that the good news is for everyone: for the poor and the rich, for women and men, for outsiders and sinners and foreigners. About half of what is in Luke is found in no other Gospel. Without Luke we would not have the shepherds and the angels, the good Samaritan, the prodigal son, Zacchaeus up his tree, or the two friends on the road to Emmaus.

Luke’s book has five parts. First, the births of John the Baptist and of Jesus. Second, Jesus is baptized and tempted. Third, Jesus teaches and heals in Galilee. Fourth, the longest part, a journey: Jesus sets his face toward Jerusalem, and along the road he tells many of his best known parables. And fifth, in Jerusalem, his death, his rising again, and his return to heaven.

Here are four things to watch for as we walk.

Watch for the lost being found. Luke shows Jesus again and again with the people others pushed away. If you remember one verse from Luke, make it this one: 'For the Son of man is come to seek and to save that which was lost.'

Watch for prayer. Luke shows Jesus praying more than any other Gospel does: at his baptism, before choosing the twelve, on the mountain, in the garden, and on the cross.

Watch for the Holy Ghost, the Spirit of God, filling people and leading Jesus from the very first chapter.

And watch for joy. Luke’s Gospel begins with angels bringing good tidings of great joy, and it ends with the disciples returning to Jerusalem with great joy, praising God.

QUESTION: Luke wrote so that his friend would know the certainty of what he had been taught. What would you like to be more certain of about Jesus by the end of this book?

PRAYER: Lord God, thank you for Luke, who wrote carefully so that we could know the truth. As I walk through his Gospel, show me Jesus, who came to seek and to save the lost. Fill me with your Spirit, teach me to pray, and give me his joy. Amen.
