# Philemon 1: greeting from Paul a prisoner and Timothy to Philemon, Apphia, Archippus and the church in thy house, thanks for his love and faith, the saints refreshed by him (1:7 told without "bowels"), Paul could command but for love's sake beseeches, Paul the aged, my son Onesimus begotten in my bonds, unprofitable now profitable (Onesimus means useful), sent back, mine own heart (1:12, 1:20 told without "bowels"), I would have kept him but did nothing without thy mind, departed for a season that thou shouldest receive him for ever, not now as a servant but a brother beloved, receive him as myself, put that on mine account (a picture many Christians see of Christ), I will repay it, thou wilt do more than I say, prepare me a lodging, greetings from Epaphras, Mark, Aristarchus, Demas, Luke; looking back; on to the Psalms. Keys 1:11, 1:16, 1:18.
TITLE: Put That on Mine Account

HOOK: A boy accidentally breaks a window at the neighbour’s house. He has no money to pay for it. He stands at the door, ashamed and afraid. And then his older brother steps up beside him, and says to the neighbour, I know he did wrong. But he is my brother. Whatever it costs to fix, send the bill to me. I will pay it.

WHERE: In the introduction, we heard that Philemon was a believer in Colosse, with a church meeting in his house. His servant Onesimus had run away, met Paul in prison, and come to believe in Jesus. Now Paul was sending him home, with this letter. We heard three things to watch for: how Paul asks, how the good news changes relationships, and what Paul offers to do.

Now we walk through the whole letter. Listen for how gently Paul makes his request. And listen for a clever play on Onesimus’s name.

STORY: The letter begins. 'Paul, a prisoner of Jesus Christ, and Timothy our brother, unto Philemon our dearly beloved, and fellowlabourer'. Paul also greets Apphia, who may have been Philemon’s wife, and Archippus, who may have been his son, and the church that met in Philemon’s house. Do you remember Archippus? In the letter to the Colossians, Paul told him, fulfil the ministry you have received.

Then Paul thanks God for Philemon. 'I thank my God, making mention of thee always in my prayers, Hearing of thy love and faith, which thou hast toward the Lord Jesus, and toward all saints'. Philemon was known for his love. Paul says that his love has brought great joy and comfort, because Philemon has refreshed the hearts of God’s people. He was the kind of person who made others feel better.

'Wherefore, though I might be much bold in Christ to enjoin thee that which is convenient, Yet for love’s sake I rather beseech thee'. Enjoin means command. Beseech means beg, appeal. Paul says, I could order you to do what is right. But instead, I am asking you, because of love. And he describes himself: 'being such an one as Paul the aged, and now also a prisoner of Jesus Christ.' An old man, in chains.

And then he comes to the point. 'I beseech thee for my son Onesimus, whom I have begotten in my bonds'. Begotten in my bonds means that Onesimus became a believer while Paul was in prison. Paul led him to Christ, so he thinks of him as his own son.

And then Paul makes a little joke, a play on words.

I am sending him back to you, Paul says, and sending him is like sending my very own heart. I would have liked to keep him here with me, to help me while I am in chains for the good news. 'But without thy mind would I do nothing; that thy benefit should not be as it were of necessity, but willingly.' Paul would not keep Onesimus without Philemon’s permission. He wanted Philemon’s kindness to be freely given, not forced.

Then Paul suggests a new way of seeing what happened. 'For perhaps he therefore departed for a season, that thou shouldest receive him for ever'. Perhaps, Paul says, the reason Onesimus ran away for a little while was so that you could have him back for ever. Not just as a servant, for a lifetime, but as a brother, for eternity. God can bring good even out of a runaway servant’s wrong choice.

And then the heart of the letter.

'If thou count me therefore a partner, receive him as myself.'

And then Paul makes an offer.

'I Paul have written it with mine own hand, I will repay it'. Paul takes the pen, and signs it himself, like signing a promise to pay. And then he adds, gently, that he will not mention that Philemon owes Paul his own self, his very life, because Paul had brought him to Christ.

'Yea, brother, let me have joy of thee in the Lord'. Refresh my heart, Paul says. You refresh everyone else’s. Now refresh mine.

'Having confidence in thy obedience I wrote unto thee, knowing that thou wilt also do more than I say.' Paul is sure Philemon will do even more than he asks. Perhaps he hints that Philemon might set Onesimus free.

And then a cheerful request. 'But withal prepare me also a lodging'. Get a room ready for me! Paul hoped to be released, and to visit.

And the letter ends with greetings from the same friends we heard about in Colossians. Epaphras, Paul’s fellow prisoner. Mark, Aristarchus, Demas, and Luke. 'The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit. Amen.'

We are not told what Philemon did. But the fact that this letter was kept, copied, and treasured by the churches suggests that Philemon did what Paul asked. A runaway servant came home, and was welcomed as a brother.

KEY 1:11: 'Which in time past was to thee unprofitable, but now profitable to thee and to me'. Here is Paul’s play on words. The name Onesimus means useful. But when he ran away, he had been useless, unprofitable, to Philemon. Now, because he has met Jesus, he really is useful, to Philemon and to Paul. Jesus can take someone who has failed, and make them truly useful. He can live up to his name at last.

KEY 1:16: 'Not now as a servant, but above a servant, a brother beloved, specially to me, but how much more unto thee, both in the flesh, and in the Lord?' This is the heart of the letter. In the world’s eyes, Onesimus was a servant, and Philemon his master. But in Christ, they were brothers. Family. Paul planted a seed here that would grow. When people see each other as brothers and sisters in Christ, it changes everything about how they treat one another.

KEY 1:18: 'If he hath wronged thee, or oweth thee ought, put that on mine account'. Ought means anything. Put it on my account means charge it to me. Paul offered to take Onesimus’s debt, and pay it himself. Many Christians have seen in this a beautiful picture of what Jesus does for us. We have wronged God, and owe a debt we cannot pay. And Jesus says to the Father, put that on my account. And he paid it, on the cross.

THREAD: Paul wrote to the Galatians, 'There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither bond nor free, there is neither male nor female: for ye are all one in Christ Jesus.' And Jesus said, 'For the Son of man is come to seek and to save that which was lost.'

TODAY: Is there someone who has wronged you, whom God may be asking you to welcome back? And is there someone you have wronged, whom you need to go back to, like Onesimus? Take one step towards putting things right today.

QUESTION: Paul said, put that on mine account. How does it make you feel to know that Jesus has taken your debt, and paid it for you?

PRAYER: Lord Jesus, thank you that you took my debt, and said, put that on my account, and paid it on the cross. Thank you that you can make useless lives useful, and turn runaways into brothers and sisters. Give me the courage of Onesimus, to go back and put things right. Give me the heart of Philemon, to welcome others. And help me, like Paul, to appeal in love. Amen.
