# Proverbs 26: sayings about fools (snow in summer, the causeless curse, answer not a fool / answer a fool explained, a message by a fool, a stone bound in a sling), 26:9 and 26:11 not quoted, a man wise in his own conceit, the slothful (a lion in the way, the door upon his hinges, his hand in the dish, wiser in his own conceit), meddling with strife (a dog by the ears), am not I in sport, where no wood is the fire goeth out, a contentious man as coals, the talebearer's words as wounds, burning lips and a potsherd covered with silver dross, he speaketh fair believe him not, whoso diggeth a pit shall fall therein. Keys 26:12, 26:20, 26:27.
TITLE: Where No Wood Is, There the Fire Goeth Out

HOOK: A campfire crackles and blazes. As long as someone keeps adding sticks, it keeps burning, bigger and hotter. But when everyone stops adding wood, the flames get smaller, and smaller, until all that is left is a few glowing embers, and then nothing. Proverbs says arguments are like that fire. And gossip is the wood.

WHERE: In chapter twenty five, we heard that the men of Hezekiah copied out more proverbs of Solomon. A word fitly spoken is like apples of gold in pictures of silver. If thine enemy be hungry, give him bread. And he that hath no rule over his own spirit is like a city broken down, without walls.

Now, in chapter twenty six, the sayings come in groups. Listen for a group about fools, a group about lazy people, and a group about troublemakers and gossips.

STORY: The first group of sayings is about fools. 'As snow in summer, and as rain in harvest, so honour is not seemly for a fool.' Snow in summer, or rain at harvest time, is out of place, and can ruin the crops. Giving honour to a fool is just as out of place.

'As the bird by wandering, as the swallow by flying, so the curse causeless shall not come.' If someone curses you for no reason, it is like a bird flying around overhead. It will not land on you. You do not need to be afraid of curses you do not deserve.

Then two sayings that seem to contradict each other. 'Answer not a fool according to his folly, lest thou also be like unto him.' And straight after, 'Answer a fool according to his folly, lest he be wise in his own conceit.' Which is right? Both! Wisdom means knowing which one fits the moment. Sometimes, if you argue with a foolish person, you become foolish too, so it is better to stay quiet. Other times, you need to answer, so they do not think they are right. Wisdom is not just following rules. It is knowing when.

'He that sendeth a message by the hand of a fool cutteth off the feet, and drinketh damage.' Sending a fool with a message is like cutting off your own feet. It will not arrive.

'As he that bindeth a stone in a sling, so is he that giveth honour to a fool.' A sling is meant to throw a stone. If you tie the stone in, it cannot go anywhere, and the sling is useless. Honouring a fool is just as silly.

And the group ends with a warning.

The next group is about lazy people, and some of these are very funny. 'The slothful man saith, There is a lion in the way; a lion is in the streets.' He makes up wild excuses not to go to work.

'As the door turneth upon his hinges, so doth the slothful upon his bed.' A door swings back and forth on its hinges, but never goes anywhere. The lazy person just turns over in bed, back and forth, and never gets up.

'The slothful hideth his hand in his bosom; it grieveth him to bring it again to his mouth.' Too lazy even to lift his food to his mouth.

'The sluggard is wiser in his own conceit than seven men that can render a reason.' The lazy person thinks he is cleverer than seven wise people. He always has an excuse, and thinks it is a good one.

Then a group about troublemakers. 'He that passeth by, and meddleth with strife belonging not to him, is like one that taketh a dog by the ears.' Grab a strange dog by the ears, and you will get bitten. Jump into someone else’s quarrel, and you will get hurt too.

'As a mad man who casteth firebrands, arrows, and death, So is the man that deceiveth his neighbour, and saith, Am not I in sport?' Firebrands are burning sticks. Someone who tricks or hurts people, and then says, I was only joking, is like a madman throwing burning arrows. Calling something a joke does not make it harmless.

Then come sayings about gossip and fire.

'As coals are to burning coals, and wood to fire; so is a contentious man to kindle strife.' A quarrelsome person keeps fights going, like adding coal to a fire. 'The words of a talebearer are as wounds, and they go down into the innermost parts of the belly.' We heard this in chapter eighteen. Gossip goes down easily, and wounds deeply.

Then sayings about people who pretend. 'Burning lips and a wicked heart are like a potsherd covered with silver dross.' A potsherd is a piece of broken pottery. Silver dross is cheap shiny stuff. Covering broken pottery with shiny paint does not make it valuable. Warm words covering a wicked heart are just as fake. 'When he speaketh fair, believe him not'. Do not be fooled by sweet words from someone who hates you. In the end, their wickedness will be seen by everyone.

And then a saying about traps.

'A lying tongue hateth those that are afflicted by it; and a flattering mouth worketh ruin.'

KEY 26:12: 'Seest thou a man wise in his own conceit? there is more hope of a fool than of him.' Wise in his own conceit means someone who thinks he is wise. After all these sayings about fools, Proverbs says there is something even worse. A person who thinks they already know everything. A fool might still learn. But someone who thinks they are wise will never listen to anyone. Pride shuts the door to learning.

KEY 26:20: 'Where no wood is, there the fire goeth out: so where there is no talebearer, the strife ceaseth.' A talebearer is a gossip. Quarrels are like fires, and gossip is the wood. As long as people keep passing on stories, the fire keeps burning. But stop the gossip, and the quarrel dies down. We can help put out fires simply by refusing to pass on gossip.

KEY 26:27: 'Whoso diggeth a pit shall fall therein: and he that rolleth a stone, it will return upon him.' People used to dig pits to trap animals, or enemies. Proverbs says the person who digs a trap for someone else often ends up falling into it themselves. And whoever starts a stone rolling downhill to hurt someone may find it rolling back on them. Plans to harm others usually end up harming ourselves. Do you remember Haman, in the book of Esther, who was hanged on the gallows he built for Mordecai?

THREAD: James wrote, 'Behold, how great a matter a little fire kindleth!' And the psalmist wrote, 'He made a pit, and digged it, and is fallen into the ditch which he made.'

TODAY: When someone tells you a piece of gossip today, do not pass it on. Let the fire go out. And ask yourself, is there any area where I think I am wise, and am not willing to listen?

QUESTION: Proverbs says, where no wood is, there the fire goeth out. How could you help put out a quarrel or a rumour by refusing to add wood to the fire?

PRAYER: Lord God, keep me from thinking I am wise in my own eyes. Give me wisdom to know when to answer and when to stay silent. Keep me from gossip, from meddling, and from hurting others and calling it a joke. Help me to be someone who puts out fires, not someone who feeds them. Amen.
