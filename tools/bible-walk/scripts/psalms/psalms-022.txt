# Psalm 22: David; my God my God why hast thou forsaken me (Jesus on the cross, Matthew 27:46); I cry but thou hearest not (time words only inside quotes), thou art holy, our fathers trusted, I am a worm and no man, they shake the head (He trusted on the LORD, Matthew 27:43), bulls and lions and dogs, poured out like water, they pierced my hands and my feet, they part my garments and cast lots (John 19:24), the turn: thou hast heard me, I will declare thy name unto my brethren (Hebrews 2:12), he hath not despised the affliction of the afflicted, all the ends of the world shall turn, unto a people that shall be born, that he hath done this (It is finished). 22:9-10, 22:14b not quoted. Told gently. Keys 22:1, 22:18, 22:27.
TITLE: My God, My God, Why Hast Thou Forsaken Me?

HOOK: Outside the walls of Jerusalem, on a hill, three crosses stand against a darkened sky. On the middle cross hangs Jesus. He has been there for hours. And then, with a loud voice, he cries out. My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? The people standing near the cross may have recognised those words. They were the first words of a psalm. A psalm David had written long, long before.

WHERE: Before this, in Psalm forty two, the writer longed for God, as the hart panteth after the water brooks. He told his own soul, why art thou cast down? hope thou in God.

Now we go back to Psalm twenty two, the sixteenth of our thirty favourite psalms, which are grouped by theme, not in number order. It is the last of our psalms for hard times, a psalm of deep suffering, and of wonderful victory. Listen for the details that came true when Jesus died on the cross. And listen for the great turn, from crying out, to praise.

STORY: Psalm twenty two is a psalm of David. David knew suffering. He was hunted, betrayed, mocked and surrounded by enemies. But as you listen to this psalm, you will notice that some of what it describes goes far beyond anything that ever happened to David. It points forward, to Jesus. That is why Jesus prayed it on the cross.

The psalm begins with a cry of abandonment.

'O my God, I cry in the daytime, but thou hearest not; and in the night season, and am not silent.' The writer has been praying and praying. But heaven seems silent.

And yet, even in this darkness, he remembers who God is. 'But thou art holy, O thou that inhabitest the praises of Israel. Our fathers trusted in thee: they trusted, and thou didst deliver them.' God rescued his people in the past. When they cried, he answered.

'But I am a worm, and no man; a reproach of men, and despised of the people.' He feels like a worm, something people step on without a second thought. 'All they that see me laugh me to scorn: they shoot out the lip, they shake the head saying, He trusted on the LORD that he would deliver him: let him deliver him, seeing he delighted in him.' People make faces, shake their heads, and mock him. If God loves you so much, let God rescue you!

At the cross, the chief priests and scribes mocked Jesus with almost exactly the same words. He trusted in God; let him deliver him now, if he will have him.

The psalm goes on. Enemies surround him like strong bulls, like a roaring lion, and like a pack of dogs. 'I am poured out like water, and all my bones are out of joint'. He is completely weak. 'My strength is dried up like a potsherd; and my tongue cleaveth to my jaws'. A potsherd is a piece of broken pottery, baked dry. His tongue sticks to his mouth with thirst. On the cross, Jesus said, I thirst.

And then, words that sound like a crucifixion. 'For dogs have compassed me: the assembly of the wicked have inclosed me: they pierced my hands and my feet.' David wrote this long before anyone in Israel had heard of crucifixion. And yet it describes exactly what happened to Jesus, when the soldiers nailed his hands and his feet to the cross.

'I may tell all my bones: they look and stare upon me.' He can count his bones. People stand and stare.

And then.

'But be not thou far from me, O LORD: O my strength, haste thee to help me.' And then, suddenly, the psalm turns. In the middle of verse twenty one, the writer says, 'for thou hast heard me'.

Everything changes. From here on, the psalm is full of praise. 'I will declare thy name unto my brethren: in the midst of the congregation will I praise thee.' The letter to the Hebrews says these are Jesus’ words, as he calls us his brothers and sisters.

'For he hath not despised nor abhorred the affliction of the afflicted; neither hath he hid his face from him; but when he cried unto him, he heard.' God did not turn away. He heard. For Jesus, God’s answer was the resurrection. Three days after the cross, Jesus rose from the dead.

And then the praise spreads, wider and wider. To the poor, who will eat and be satisfied. To the whole world.

'For the kingdom is the LORD’S: and he is the governor among the nations.'

And it reaches into the future, to people not yet born. 'They shall come, and shall declare his righteousness unto a people that shall be born, that he hath done this.' He hath done this. In other words, it is finished. Do you remember Jesus’ last words on the cross? It is finished.

The psalm that began with, my God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me, ends with, he hath done this. From the darkest cry to the greatest victory.

KEY 22:1: 'My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? why art thou so far from helping me, and from the words of my roaring?' Forsaken means abandoned, left alone. Roaring here means groaning, crying out in pain. When Jesus cried these words, he was carrying the sin of the whole world. In that moment, he went through the separation from God that our sin deserves, so that we would never have to be forsaken. And notice, even then, he still says, my God.

KEY 22:18: 'They part my garments among them, and cast lots upon my vesture.' Vesture means clothing. To cast lots is like throwing dice, or drawing straws. At the foot of the cross, the soldiers divided Jesus’ clothes between them. But his coat was woven in one piece, so they gambled for it instead of tearing it. John tells us this happened, that the scripture might be fulfilled. Every detail was in God’s plan.

KEY 22:27: 'All the ends of the world shall remember and turn unto the LORD: and all the kindreds of the nations shall worship before thee.' Kindreds means families, peoples. Out of one man’s suffering comes worship from every nation on earth. That is exactly what has happened. Through Jesus’ death and resurrection, people from every country, every language, every family, have turned to God. If you trust Jesus, you are part of the fulfilment of this verse.

THREAD: Matthew tells us, 'Jesus cried with a loud voice, saying, Eli, Eli, lama sabachthani? that is to say, My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?' And John wrote of the soldiers, 'These things therefore the soldiers did.'

TODAY: Take a quiet moment today to thank Jesus for what he went through on the cross for you. And if you ever feel forsaken, remember that Jesus has been there too. He understands, and he will never forsake you.

QUESTION: Psalm twenty two was written long before Jesus was born, yet it describes his death in detail. What does that tell you about God’s plan, and about the Bible?

PRAYER: Lord Jesus, thank you that you were willing to be forsaken, so that I would never be forsaken. Thank you for suffering on the cross for me, and for rising again. Thank you that God did not hide his face, but heard you. I join with all the ends of the world, to turn to you and worship. You have done this. Amen.
