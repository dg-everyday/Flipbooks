# Psalm 42: Maschil for the sons of Korah (a psalm of instruction); the hart panteth after the water brooks, thirsty for God, tears, where is thy God (taunts), remembering going to the house of God with the multitude, why art thou cast down O my soul (refrain twice: talking to your own soul), far away in the north (Jordan, Hermon, Mizar), deep calleth unto deep (waves going over him), yet the LORD will command his lovingkindness (time words inside quotes only), God my rock, hope thou in God. Psalm 43 continues it. Keys 42:1, 42:8, 42:11.
TITLE: As the Hart Panteth After the Water Brooks

HOOK: In a dry, dusty land, a deer has been running for a long time. Her mouth is dry. Her tongue is hanging out. She is desperately thirsty. She sniffs the air, searching for the smell of water. She goes to the stream where she always drinks, but it has dried up. Only cracked mud is left. She pants, and pants, longing for water. Psalm forty two begins with that thirsty deer.

WHERE: In Psalm thirteen, David cried, how long wilt thou forget me, O LORD? how long wilt thou hide thy face from me? He was honest about his sorrow. But then came the turn. But I have trusted in thy mercy, he said; my heart shall rejoice in thy salvation. And the psalm that began with how long ended with, I will sing unto the LORD.

Now, in Psalm forty two, we hear someone who is far from home, and longing for God. Listen for the question this person asks their own soul. And listen for the line that is repeated, like a chorus.

STORY: Psalm forty two is called a Maschil for the sons of Korah. A Maschil probably means a psalm that teaches, a psalm of instruction. The sons of Korah were a family of singers and musicians who served at the temple.

The writer is far away from Jerusalem, far from the temple where he used to worship. And he feels far from God. The psalm begins with the thirsty deer.

Then he says, 'My soul thirsteth for God, for the living God: when shall I come and appear before God?' Not just any god, but the living God. He longs to go back to the temple, and worship God again.

'My tears have been my meat day and night, while they continually say unto me, Where is thy God?' Meat here means food. He has been crying so much, it is as if tears are all he eats. And people around him make it worse. They mock him. Where is your God now? If your God is real, why does he not help you?

Then he remembers happier times. 'When I remember these things, I pour out my soul in me: for I had gone with the multitude, I went with them to the house of God, with the voice of joy and praise, with a multitude that kept holyday.' He remembers walking to the temple with great crowds of people, singing and celebrating at the great feasts. Those were happy times. Now he is alone, and far away.

And then, he speaks to his own soul.

This is one of the most helpful things in the psalms. The writer is not just listening to his feelings. He is talking back to them. Why are you so sad, soul? Why are you so troubled? Hope in God! Instead of letting his feelings boss him around, he tells his feelings what is true.

'O my God, my soul is cast down within me: therefore will I remember thee from the land of Jordan, and of the Hermonites, from the hill Mizar.' Jordan, Hermon and Mizar are places in the far north of Israel, near the great mountain called Hermon, where the river Jordan begins. That is where the writer is, a long way from Jerusalem. But even there, he will remember God.

And then a dramatic picture. 'Deep calleth unto deep at the noise of thy waterspouts: all thy waves and thy billows are gone over me.' Near Mount Hermon, the snow melts and roars down in great waterfalls. The writer feels like someone caught in that rushing water, wave after wave crashing over his head. Billows are huge waves. His troubles feel like they are drowning him.

And yet, even then, he finds hope.

Then he prays honestly again. 'I will say unto God my rock, Why hast thou forgotten me? why go I mourning because of the oppression of the enemy?' Notice that he calls God my rock, even as he asks, why hast thou forgotten me? He is holding on to God, while asking hard questions.

'As with a sword in my bones, mine enemies reproach me; while they say daily unto me, Where is thy God?' The mocking hurts like a sword.

And then the chorus comes back, and the psalm ends with it.

This psalm does not end with everything fixed. The writer is still far away. He is still sad. But he is still hoping in God. And in many Bibles, the next psalm, Psalm forty three, continues the same prayer, with the same chorus, and ends with him going back to the altar of God, to his exceeding joy.

KEY 42:1: 'As the hart panteth after the water brooks, so panteth my soul after thee, O God.' A hart is a deer. Water brooks are streams. Just as a deer cannot live without water, the writer cannot live without God. Do you ever feel that kind of thirst for God? Sometimes, when we are far from God, we feel empty, restless, dry inside. That emptiness is a kind of thirst, and only God can satisfy it. Jesus said, if any man thirst, let him come unto me, and drink.

KEY 42:8: 'Yet the LORD will command his lovingkindness in the daytime, and in the night his song shall be with me, and my prayer unto the God of my life.' Lovingkindness is God’s faithful, never failing love. And God commands it, sends it out like a king giving an order. All through the day, God sends his love. And in the dark hours, God gives him a song. Even when the waves crash over us, God’s love does not stop, and he gives us songs in the darkness.

KEY 42:11: 'Why art thou cast down, O my soul? and why art thou disquieted within me? hope thou in God: for I shall yet praise him, who is the health of my countenance, and my God.' Cast down means sad, low, discouraged. Disquieted means restless, upset. The health of my countenance means the one who brings joy back to my face. Notice the little word yet. I shall yet praise him. I am not praising now, perhaps, but I will again. When you feel low, speak to your soul. Hope thou in God.

THREAD: Jesus said, 'If any man thirst, let him come unto me, and drink.' And Isaiah wrote, 'Ho, every one that thirsteth, come ye to the waters'.

TODAY: The next time you feel sad or discouraged, try talking to your soul, as this writer did. Ask yourself, why art thou cast down? Then tell yourself something true about God. Hope thou in God, for I shall yet praise him.

QUESTION: The writer talks back to his feelings, saying, why art thou cast down, O my soul? hope thou in God. Why do you think it helps to tell ourselves what is true, instead of only listening to how we feel?

PRAYER: O God, as the deer pants for the water brooks, so my soul longs for you. When I feel far away, and the waves of trouble go over me, help me to remember you. Send your lovingkindness through the day, and give me your song in the darkness. When my soul is cast down, help me to hope in you, for I shall yet praise you, my God. Amen.
