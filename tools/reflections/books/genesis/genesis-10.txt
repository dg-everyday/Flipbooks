# Genesis 10: The table of nations

== 10:1
T: God writes the story of nations
R: 'Now these are the generations of the sons of Noah, Shem, Ham, and Japheth: and unto them were sons born after the flood.' Genesis 10 is often called the Table of Nations. It traces how the peoples of the world descended from Noah's three sons. To many readers, it is a list of hard-to-pronounce names. But it carries a profound message: every nation on earth has its origin in one family, and every nation has a place in God's story. God did not forget the world after the flood. He watched over the growth of every people. Before God called Abraham in chapter 12, He showed that He cared about all nations. And when He did call Abraham, it was so that in him all families of the earth would be blessed. The Bible begins with the nations and ends with them too, gathered before the throne of the Lamb. Today, remember that God's heart is for the whole world. Every nation matters to Him, including the people next door.

== 10:2
T: Distant peoples, known by God
R: 'The sons of Japheth; Gomer, and Magog, and Madai, and Javan, and Tubal, and Meshech, and Tiras.' These names became peoples who spread north and west. Madai is linked with the Medes, and Javan with the Greeks. Centuries later, God would use the Medes to bring down Babylon and set His people free. And the Greek language of Javan's descendants would become the language in which the New Testament was written, carrying the gospel across the Roman world. God was already at work in these distant peoples long before they knew Him. He weaves the histories of nations into His purposes. Daniel says, he removeth kings, and setteth up kings. The nations that seem far from God today may one day play a part in His plan. On the day of Pentecost, Medes were among those who heard the gospel in their own language. Today, pray for a people group far from you. God knows their names, and He has a purpose for them.
S: The sons of Japheth became peoples such as the Medes and the Greeks. God wove these distant nations into His purposes long before they knew Him. Pray today for a people group far from you, trusting God's plan for them.
P: God knows every nation by name. Genesis 10:2 lists 'Madai, and Javan,' ancestors of the Medes and Greeks. God used both in His plan. Pray for the nations today.

== 10:3
T: Every family recorded
R: 'And the sons of Gomer; Ashkenaz, and Riphath, and Togarmah.' Another list of names, another family branch recorded. Why would God include these details in Scripture? Because God is interested in families. He records names because people matter to Him. Every name in this chapter represents a real person, a father or son who lived and loved and had descendants. Many of us may not know much about our family history beyond a few generations. But God knows every branch of your family tree. He knows the names of your ancestors, the places they lived, and the paths that led to you. Your life is not an accident of history. Acts 17 says that God determined the times and places where people would live, so that they should seek the Lord. Every branch of your family history was part of bringing you to the place where you could seek Him. Today, thank God for your family heritage, and seek Him with the life He has given you.
S: Gomer's sons are recorded by name in Scripture. God is interested in families and knows every branch of every family tree. Thank Him for your heritage and seek Him with the life He has given you.

== 10:4
T: Tarshish and the runaway prophet
R: 'And the sons of Javan; Elishah, and Tarshish, Kittim, and Dodanim.' Tarshish became the name of a distant place, a port at the far edge of the known world. Centuries later, the prophet Jonah tried to flee from God by sailing to Tarshish. God had told him to go east to Nineveh, but Jonah went west, as far away as he could. Yet even on the way to Tarshish, God found him. The storm, the great fish, and the second chance all show that no one can outrun God. Psalm 139 asks, Whither shall I flee from thy presence? If I take the wings of the morning, and dwell in the uttermost parts of the sea; even there shall thy hand lead me. Is there a Tarshish in your life, a place you are running to in order to avoid what God has asked? You cannot outrun Him. But you also do not need to. His call comes with grace. Turn around today and follow where He leads.
S: Tarshish became a distant port where Jonah later tried to flee from God. No one can outrun God's presence or His call. Stop running today and follow where He leads.
P: You can't outrun God. Genesis 10:4 names 'Tarshish,' the place Jonah tried to flee to. God found him there. Stop running and follow Him today.

== 10:5
T: The isles wait for Him
R: 'By these were the isles of the Gentiles divided in their lands; every one after his tongue, after their families, in their nations.' The descendants of Japheth spread out to the coastlands and islands, each with their own language, family, and nation. This verse introduces a beautiful biblical theme: the isles. Isaiah repeatedly speaks of the isles waiting for God's law and rejoicing in His salvation. Isaiah 42 says of the Messiah, he shall not fail nor be discouraged, till he have set judgment in the earth: and the isles shall wait for his law. The farthest coastlands, the most remote islands, were in God's view from the beginning. The gospel has since reached islands across every ocean. Notice too the phrase every one after his tongue. Languages and cultures are part of God's wise ordering of the world. In heaven, people from every tongue will worship together. Today, celebrate the diversity of cultures God has made, and pray for the remote places still waiting to hear.
S: Japheth's descendants spread to the coastlands and islands, each with their own language and nation. Isaiah later spoke of the isles waiting for God's law and salvation. Pray today for remote places still waiting to hear the gospel.

== 10:6
T: Nations God would bless
R: 'And the sons of Ham; Cush, and Mizraim, and Phut, and Canaan.' Ham's sons became great peoples. Cush is associated with the lands south of Egypt, Mizraim with Egypt itself, Phut with parts of North Africa, and Canaan with the land later promised to Abraham. These nations would play major roles in biblical history. Egypt would shelter Joseph and his family, then enslave Israel, and later become a refuge for the infant Jesus. Isaiah even prophesied a day when God would say, Blessed be Egypt my people. Ham's descendants were not outside God's care. An Ethiopian official became one of the first Gentile converts in Acts, and people of Cyrene, in North Africa, helped spread the gospel to Antioch. The story of Ham's line is full of grace. No nation is beyond God's reach, and no people group is excluded from His blessing. Today, let your view of God's love be as wide as His. He has a purpose for every nation He made.
S: Ham's sons became peoples including Egypt, Cush, Phut and Canaan. God included these nations in His purposes and blessing throughout Scripture. Let your view of God's love be as wide as His.

Centuries later, the Queen of Sheba traveled a great distance to hear the wisdom of Solomon and was overwhelmed by what she saw. She blessed the LORD God of Israel. Jesus referred to her, saying she came from the uttermost parts of the earth to hear Solomon's wisdom, and behold, a greater than Solomon is here. People from distant lands have always been drawn to God's truth. Psalm 72 speaks of the kings of Sheba and Seba offering gifts to the Messiah. The wise men from the east who came to worship Jesus fulfilled this kind of prophecy. Those who seem far away often seek God earnestly. Today, ask yourself whether you are seeking God's wisdom with the same diligence as the Queen of Sheba. And rejoice that a greater than Solomon is here: Jesus Christ.

== 10:8
T: The rise of power
R: 'And Cush begat Nimrod: he began to be a mighty one in the earth.' Nimrod stands out in this list. He is the first person described as a mighty one, someone who sought power and dominance. Many scholars see him as the first empire-builder. After the flood, humanity was supposed to spread out and fill the earth under God's blessing. Nimrod gathered power and built kingdoms. Ambition itself is not wrong; God gives gifts of leadership. But the question is always whether power is used for God's glory or our own. The rest of the Bible shows a long line of mighty rulers who exalted themselves, from Pharaoh to Nebuchadnezzar. Each one was eventually humbled. Mary sang in the Magnificat, He hath put down the mighty from their seats, and exalted them of low degree. Jesus showed a different kind of greatness, the greatness of a servant. Today, examine your ambitions. Are you seeking to become mighty, or to serve? God exalts the humble.
S: Nimrod became the first mighty one in the earth, a builder of power and kingdoms. Throughout Scripture, God humbles those who exalt themselves. Examine your ambitions and choose the path of humble service.
P: God humbles the mighty and exalts the humble. Genesis 10:8: Nimrod 'began to be a mighty one in the earth.' Seek to serve, not to dominate, today.

== 10:9
T: Before the LORD
R: 'He was a mighty hunter before the LORD: wherefore it is said, Even as Nimrod the mighty hunter before the LORD.' Nimrod's reputation became a proverb. People spoke of him as the standard of a mighty hunter. The phrase before the LORD can mean in God's sight, and some interpreters see it as a hint of defiance, as if Nimrod's power was displayed in God's face. Whatever the exact sense, it reminds us that everything we do is done before the LORD. Our skills, our accomplishments, and our reputations are all seen by Him. Nimrod became famous, and people quoted his name. But fame among people is not the same as approval from God. Jesus asked, How can ye believe, which receive honour one of another, and seek not the honour that cometh from God only? Today, remember that you live before the LORD. Let your skills and achievements be offered to Him. Seek His approval above the praise of people.
S: Nimrod's fame as a mighty hunter before the LORD became a proverb. Everything we do is done in God's sight, and His approval matters more than human fame. Offer your skills and achievements to Him today.

== 10:10
T: The beginning of Babel
R: 'And the beginning of his kingdom was Babel, and Erech, and Accad, and Calneh, in the land of Shinar.' Nimrod's kingdom began with Babel, the city that would become infamous in the next chapter for its proud tower. Babel later became Babylon, which throughout Scripture represents human pride, rebellion against God, and worldly power. Babylon destroyed Jerusalem and carried God's people into exile. In Revelation, Babylon the great stands as a symbol of the world system opposed to God, and its fall is celebrated. So from its very beginning, Babel was associated with human kingdoms built in defiance of God. Every human kingdom eventually falls, but God's kingdom stands forever. Daniel saw a stone cut out without hands that struck the kingdoms of the world and became a great mountain filling the earth. Today, examine where you are building your life. Is it in Babel, trusting human power, or in the kingdom of God? Seek first His kingdom, and everything else will find its place.

== 10:11
T: Nineveh and mercy
R: 'Out of that land went forth Asshur, and builded Nineveh, and the city Rehoboth, and Calah.' Nineveh would become the capital of Assyria, one of the most brutal empires of the ancient world. The Assyrians were feared for their cruelty. Yet Nineveh is also the city to which God sent Jonah with a message of warning. And when Nineveh repented, from the king down to the lowest citizen, God showed mercy and spared the city. Jonah was angry about it because he knew God was gracious, merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness. The story of Nineveh shows that God's mercy reaches even the cruelest of cities when they turn to Him. God asked Jonah, should not I spare Nineveh, that great city? God cares about the people of even the most wicked places. Today, think of a place or person you consider beyond hope. Pray for them. The God who had mercy on Nineveh can do the same again.

== 10:12
T: What makes a city great
R: 'And Resen between Nineveh and Calah: the same is a great city.' Scripture notes that Resen was a great city. Human civilization flourished quickly after the flood, with cities rising in the land of Shinar and Assyria. Cities were centers of trade, culture, and power. But what makes a city truly great? The Bible's answer is not size or wealth, but righteousness. Proverbs says, Righteousness exalteth a nation: but sin is a reproach to any people. Jeremiah told the exiles in Babylon to seek the peace of the city where God had sent them, and to pray for it. God's people are called to be a blessing to the cities where they live. Ultimately, the greatest city is the one Abraham looked for, a city which hath foundations, whose builder and maker is God. That is the new Jerusalem. Today, pray for your city. Ask God to bring righteousness, justice, and peace to it. And seek ways to be a blessing where you live.
S: Resen is described as a great city in the land of Assyria. True greatness for a city comes from righteousness, not size or wealth. Pray for your city today and seek to be a blessing where you live.

== 10:13
T: The roots of Egypt
R: 'And Mizraim begat Ludim, and Anamim, and Lehabim, and Naphtuhim.' Mizraim is the Hebrew name for Egypt, and these were peoples associated with that region. Egypt plays a huge role in Scripture. It was a land of refuge in famine for Abraham and Jacob. It was where Joseph rose to power and saved his family. But it was also where Israel suffered in slavery for four hundred years. And it was the land from which God delivered His people with mighty signs and wonders. Egypt reminds us that God is sovereign over every nation's history. He can use a place for blessing, for testing, and for deliverance. Matthew notes that the infant Jesus was taken to Egypt for safety, fulfilling the word, Out of Egypt have I called my son. God's purposes run through the history of nations in ways we often cannot see. Today, trust that God is at work in the places you find yourself, whether they feel like refuge or captivity.

== 10:14
T: Giants fall before God
R: 'And Pathrusim, and Casluhim, (out of whom came Philistim,) and Caphtorim.' Tucked in parentheses is a name that would become famous: the Philistines. They became long-standing enemies of Israel, appearing in the stories of Samson, Samuel, Saul, and David. Their most famous champion was Goliath, the giant from Gath. When young David faced Goliath, he declared, Thou comest to me with a sword, and with a spear, and with a shield: but I come to thee in the name of the LORD of hosts. With a single stone, the giant fell. The Philistines represent the persistent enemies and obstacles that God's people face. Some battles last for generations. But God gives victory to those who trust in Him. Whatever giant stands before you today, whether fear, addiction, illness, or opposition, remember David's confidence. The battle is the LORD's. You do not face it alone. Go forward in the name of the LORD of hosts, and trust Him for the victory.

== 10:15
T: Sidon and a mother's faith
R: 'And Canaan begat Sidon his firstborn, and Heth.' Sidon became a great Phoenician city on the Mediterranean coast. In the Old Testament, it was known for idolatry. Jezebel, the wicked queen who promoted Baal worship in Israel, was a daughter of the king of the Sidonians. Yet in the Gospels, something beautiful happens. Jesus traveled to the region of Tyre and Sidon, and a Canaanite woman came crying out for her daughter. She persisted even when Jesus tested her, saying that even the dogs eat of the crumbs which fall from their masters' table. Jesus answered, O woman, great is thy faith. Her daughter was healed. From the line of Canaan, through the region of Sidon, came one of the most remarkable examples of faith in the Gospels. God's grace breaks through every barrier of background and history. Today, come to Jesus with persistent faith like that Canaanite mother. He hears and answers those who cry out to Him.

== 10:16

R: 'And the Jebusite, and the Amorite, and the Girgasite.' These Canaanite peoples would later occupy the land God promised to Abraham. The Jebusites held the city of Jebus, which would become Jerusalem. For centuries, their stronghold seemed impregnable. They mocked David, saying even the blind and lame could keep him out. But David captured the stronghold of Zion, and it became the city of David, the place where God would put His name and where Jesus would one day die and rise again. What was once a pagan stronghold became the holy city. God can transform the most resistant places. Paul writes that the weapons of our warfare are mighty through God to the pulling down of strong holds. Is there a stronghold in your life, a fortress of sin, fear, or unbelief that seems unconquerable? God can take it. And He can turn it into a place where His name is honored. Today, bring your strongholds to Him and trust His power to transform them.

== 10:17
T: Mercy for those who seek it
R: 'And the Hivite, and the Arkite, and the Sinite.' The Hivites appear later in a surprising story. When Israel entered Canaan, the people of Gibeon, who were Hivites, realized that God was with Israel. They used a clever deception to make a peace treaty with Joshua. Even though the treaty was made through trickery, Israel honored it, and the Gibeonites were spared. Later they served at the house of God. Their story shows that even among the nations destined for judgment, those who sought peace with God's people found a place among them. Like Rahab the Canaanite, who hid the spies and was saved, the Gibeonites found mercy. God's judgment is real, but His mercy is always available to those who turn to Him. Jesus said, him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out. No matter your past or your background, there is a place for you among God's people. Today, seek peace with God through Christ, and receive the mercy He freely offers.

God told Abraham that the iniquity of the Amorites was not yet full, meaning He would wait centuries before bringing judgment. God's patience with the Canaanites lasted over four hundred years. That is a remarkable picture of His longsuffering. He gives nations and individuals time to turn. But His patience is not endless. Eventually, judgment came. The spreading of a people can be a blessing or a danger, depending on what they spread. Influence can carry righteousness or corruption. As Christians, we are called to spread the fragrance of Christ wherever we go. Paul writes that God maketh manifest the savour of his knowledge by us in every place. Today, consider what you are spreading. Let your influence carry the knowledge and love of Christ.

Now Scripture turns to Shem, the son through whom God's promise would continue. Notice that Shem is called the father of all the children of Eber. The name Eber is probably the source of the word Hebrew. This verse quietly points toward Abraham, who would descend from Eber. God's redemptive plan was moving forward, generation by generation, through the line of Shem. Though it seemed like just another family among the nations, this line carried the promise of the seed who would crush the serpent's head. God often works quietly through ordinary families, preparing for extraordinary purposes. The line of Shem looked like any other line, yet it was the channel of salvation for the whole world. Today, do not underestimate what God might be doing through ordinary faithfulness in your family. He works quietly and patiently toward His great purposes.

== 10:22

Elam lay east of Babylon, Asshur became Assyria, and Aram became the Arameans, or Syrians. Arphaxad's line led to Abraham. Centuries later, on the day of Pentecost, when the Holy Spirit came upon the disciples, people from many nations heard the gospel in their own languages. Among them were Elamites. Descendants of Elam, who had wandered far from the knowledge of God, heard about the wonderful works of God in their own tongue. What a beautiful reversal. The God who scattered the nations would gather them back through the gospel. Elam, Asshur, and Aram appear in Scripture as both enemies and objects of God's mercy. Today, rejoice that the gospel reaches every nation. The Spirit of God still speaks to people in their own languages, drawing them back to the Father. Pray for the descendants of these ancient nations today.

== 10:23
T: The land of Uz
R: 'And the children of Aram; Uz, and Hul, and Gether, and Mash.' The name Uz may sound familiar. The book of Job begins, There was a man in the land of Uz, whose name was Job; and that man was perfect and upright, and one that feared God, and eschewed evil. Whether or not Job's homeland is directly connected to this Uz, the name reminds us of his remarkable story. Job lost everything: his wealth, his children, his health. Yet in his suffering, he declared, For I know that my redeemer liveth, and that he shall stand at the latter day upon the earth. Job's story teaches that faith can survive the deepest suffering. It also shows that God is present even when He seems silent. In the end, Job saw God and was restored. If you are walking through a season of suffering, remember Job of Uz. Hold on to your Redeemer. == 10:24
T: Links in the chain
R: 'And Arphaxad begat Salah; and Salah begat Eber.' This verse traces the line from Shem to Eber, the ancestor of the Hebrews. Arphaxad, Salah, and Eber are all included in Luke's genealogy of Jesus. These men lived ordinary lives, but they were links in the chain that led to Christ. It is easy to think that only the famous people in Scripture mattered. But God's plan depended on every generation, including those whose stories are never told. Each link mattered. Without Salah, there would be no Eber; without Eber, no Abraham; without Abraham, no David; without David, no Jesus. Your life may feel small and unremarkable. But you are part of a chain of faithfulness that stretches far beyond you. The prayers you pray, the children you raise, the people you encourage, all link forward to what God will do in the future. Today, take your place in the chain with faithfulness. God is building something through you that you may never fully see.

== 10:25
T: Living in divided times
R: 'And unto Eber were born two sons: the name of one was Peleg; for in his days was the earth divided; and his brother's name was Joktan.' Peleg's name means division, and Scripture explains that in his days the earth was divided. Many connect this with the scattering of the nations at Babel, described in the next chapter. Peleg lived in a time of upheaval, when humanity was split apart by language and geography. Many people today feel they are living in divided times too. Families, communities, and nations are torn by disagreement and conflict. Yet God is not the author of confusion and division born of sin. Through Christ, He is building a new humanity. Paul writes that Jesus is our peace, who hath made both one, and hath broken down the middle wall of partition between us. In divided times, God's people are called to be agents of reconciliation. Today, pray for unity in your family, church, and community. Be a bridge-builder in a divided world.

== 10:26
T: Remembered even in a list
R: 'And Joktan begat Almodad, and Sheleph, and Hazar-maveth, and Jerah.' Joktan, Peleg's brother, had many sons who became tribes, many believed to have settled in Arabia. These names appear only here and in the parallel list in Chronicles. We know almost nothing about them. Yet they are written in God's word. Even people who appear only once in Scripture are remembered by God. This brings to mind the book of life, which Revelation describes as the record of all who belong to God. Jesus told His disciples, rejoice, because your names are written in heaven. That is the greatest honor anyone can have. Not to be remembered by history, not to be famous, but to have your name written in heaven. These sons of Joktan were recorded in an earthly book. But the most important question for each of us is whether our names are written in the Lamb's book of life. Today, rejoice if you belong to Christ. Your name is written in heaven.

== 10:27
T: Every person counts
R: 'And Hadoram, and Uzal, and Diklah.' Three more names in a long list. It would be easy to skim past them. But Scripture is not careless. If God included these names, they matter. Each name represents a person with a life, a family, and descendants. In a world of billions, it is easy to feel like just a number. But God does not see people as numbers. He sees individuals. Jesus told the story of a shepherd who left ninety-nine sheep to search for one that was lost. He said that there is joy in the presence of the angels of God over one sinner that repenteth. Every single person counts to God. That truth should change how we see ourselves and others. The stranger at the store, the coworker you barely know, the person you pass on the street: each one is known and valued by God. Today, slow down and see the individuals around you. Treat each one as someone God cares about personally.

== 10:28
T: Names that echo later
R: 'And Obal, and Abimael, and Sheba.' The name Sheba appears again, this time in Shem's line rather than Ham's. Some scholars believe the famous kingdom of Sheba, whose queen visited Solomon, came from this line. The repeated name reminds us that biblical genealogies are complex, and peoples intermixed and migrated over centuries. But the larger lesson is that God was preparing all these nations for future encounters with His truth. The Queen of Sheba came seeking wisdom and found the God of Israel. Psalm 72 foresees the kings of Sheba bringing gifts to the Messiah. What God plants in one generation can bear fruit many generations later. The descendants of these men would one day hear of the true God. Today, trust God with the long story. The prayers you pray for your family and community may not be answered in your lifetime, but God remembers them. He is weaving a story across centuries, and nothing you entrust to Him is lost.

== 10:29

Jesus told of a merchant who found one pearl of great price and sold everything to buy it. The kingdom of heaven is worth more than all the gold of Ophir. Today, ask yourself what you treasure most. == 10:31
T: Every tongue and land
R: 'These are the sons of Shem, after their families, after their tongues, in their lands, after their nations.' The same fourfold summary appears for Shem as for Ham and Japheth: families, tongues, lands, and nations. All three branches of humanity are presented in the same way. Shem's line would carry the special promise of redemption, but Scripture does not present his descendants as superior in their humanity. All are made in God's image; all are part of His world. The promise that came through Shem was not for Shem alone. It was for the blessing of all nations. God chose one line in order to reach every line. That is how God's election always works: He chooses some in order to bless many. Israel was chosen to be a light to the Gentiles. The church is called to make disciples of all nations. If you have received God's grace, it is not only for you. Today, ask how God wants to use you to bless others with what you have received.

== 10:32

R: 'These are the families of the sons of Noah, after their generations, in their nations: and by these were the nations divided in the earth after the flood.' The Table of Nations ends with a summary: all the nations of the earth came from the families of Noah's sons. Scripture counts about seventy nations in this chapter, a number that came to represent the whole world. Jesus later sent out seventy disciples to preach, perhaps a quiet signal that His message was for all the nations. The story of Genesis 10 is one of spreading and dividing. But the story of the gospel is one of gathering. Through Christ, God is calling people from every nation back to Himself and into one family. Ephesians says God's plan is to gather together in one all things in Christ. Today, see your faith as part of this great gathering. You belong to a family that spans every nation. Pray for the day when every nation is gathered in worship before Him.
