# Genesis 15: The covenant between the pieces

== 15:1
T: Your shield and reward
R: 'After these things the word of the LORD came unto Abram in a vision, saying, Fear not, Abram: I am thy shield, and thy exceeding great reward.' After the battle and after refusing Sodom's riches, Abram may have felt vulnerable. The defeated kings might return for revenge. And he had turned down great wealth. God spoke directly to both concerns. Fear not: I am thy shield. God Himself would protect Abram. And I am thy exceeding great reward. Abram had refused Sodom's goods, but he had not lost anything. God Himself was his reward. This is one of the most beautiful promises in Scripture. Our greatest treasure is not what God gives but God Himself. Asaph wrote, Whom have I in heaven but thee? and there is none upon earth that I desire beside thee. Today, if you are afraid, hear God say, Fear not. He is your shield. And if you have given something up for Him, remember that He is your exceeding great reward.
S: God told Abram not to fear, because He was his shield and his exceeding great reward. Our greatest protection and treasure is God Himself. Rest today in the God who shields you and is your reward.

== 15:2
T: Honest questions
R: 'And Abram said, Lord GOD, what wilt thou give me, seeing I go childless, and the steward of my house is this Eliezer of Damascus?' Abram responded honestly. God had promised a great reward, but Abram's heart ached over one thing: he still had no child. What good was a reward if he had no heir? This is the first recorded time Abram speaks to God in Scripture, and it is an honest question. Abram was not rebelling; he was wrestling. God welcomes honest prayer. The psalms are full of questions: How long, O LORD? Why standest thou afar off? Faith is not pretending everything is fine. It is bringing our real concerns to God and trusting Him with them. Abram addressed God as Lord GOD, acknowledging His authority even while expressing his confusion. Today, bring your honest questions to God. Tell Him what troubles you. He can handle your honesty, and He will respond with grace.
S: Abram honestly asked God what reward could matter while he remained childless. God welcomes honest prayers and questions from His children. Bring your real concerns to God today and trust Him with them.

== 15:3
T: Wrestling with delay
R: 'And Abram said, Behold, to me thou hast given no seed: and, lo, one born in my house is mine heir.' Abram repeated his concern. Years had passed since God first promised to make him a great nation. Still there was no child. In the culture of that time, a trusted servant could become heir if there were no son. Abram seemed to be accepting that Eliezer would inherit everything. When God's promises seem delayed, we are tempted to settle for a substitute. We assume God must mean something less than what He said. But God's promises are not vague. He means exactly what He says. Abram was about to learn that God had no intention of substituting a servant for a son. Sometimes our disappointment leads us to lower our expectations of God. But God is able to do exceeding abundantly above all that we ask or think. Today, if you have settled for less than God promised, bring it back to Him. Let Him renew your hope.
S: Abram repeated his concern that a servant would be his heir since he had no son. When promises seem delayed, we can be tempted to settle for a substitute. Bring your disappointment to God and let Him renew your hope today.
P: Don't settle for less than God's promise. Genesis 15:3: 'to me thou hast given no seed.' Abram was ready to settle. God had more in mind. Hope again today!

== 15:4
T: God's clear answer
R: 'And, behold, the word of the LORD came unto him, saying, This shall not be thine heir; but he that shall come forth out of thine own bowels shall be thine heir.' God answered Abram's honest question with a clear word. Eliezer would not be his heir. Abram's own son would be. God did not scold Abram for asking. He responded with reassurance and specificity. When we bring our honest concerns to God, He speaks into them. His word cuts through our confusion and clarifies His promises. God did not give Abram a timeline, but He gave him certainty about the outcome. Sometimes that is what we need most. We may not know when, but we can know that God will keep His word. Isaiah records God saying, my word… shall not return unto me void, but it shall accomplish that which I please. Today, open God's word and listen for His answer to your concerns. He may not tell you when, but He will remind you that He is faithful.
S: God answered Abram clearly: his heir would be his own son, not his servant. God responds to honest questions with reassurance and clarity. Open His word today and listen for His answer to your concerns.

== 15:5
T: Count the stars
R: 'And he brought him forth abroad, and said, Look now toward heaven, and tell the stars, if thou be able to number them: and he said unto him, So shall thy seed be.' God took Abram outside under the night sky. Look up, God said. Count the stars if you can. So shall thy seed be. Imagine Abram standing in the darkness, gazing at thousands of stars stretching across the sky, without the glare of city lights. It was an overwhelming sight. God used creation to illustrate His promise. Every night afterward, Abram could look up and remember. Sometimes we need to get outside of our cramped perspective and look at the bigger picture. Our problems loom large when we stare at them. But when we look up at God's greatness, our perspective changes. Today, if you can, step outside tonight and look at the stars. Let them remind you of God's power and of the countless people who have come to faith through Abraham's seed.

== 15:6
T: Righteous by faith
R: 'And he believed in the LORD; and he counted it to him for righteousness.' This is one of the most important verses in the entire Bible. Abram believed God, and God credited that faith to him as righteousness. Abram did not earn righteousness by his good works. He received it by trusting God's promise. Paul builds his whole argument for justification by faith on this verse in Romans 4 and Galatians 3. He writes, Now it was not written for his sake alone, that it was imputed to him; but for us also. The same way Abraham was made right with God is the way we are made right with God: by faith. Not by our efforts, but by trusting God's promise fulfilled in Jesus Christ. This is the heart of the gospel. Today, rest in this truth. If you have believed in Jesus, God has counted you righteous. Your standing before Him is secure, not because of what you have done, but because of whom you have trusted.
S: Abram believed the LORD, and God counted his faith as righteousness. Paul shows that we too are made right with God by faith, not works. Rest today in the righteousness God gives to all who trust in Jesus.

== 15:7
T: The God who brought you out
R: 'And he said unto him, I am the LORD that brought thee out of Ur of the Chaldees, to give thee this land to inherit it.' God reminded Abram of his history. I am the LORD that brought thee out. Abram had not wandered into Canaan by accident. God had called him and led him every step of the way. Remembering what God has done in the past strengthens our faith for the future. The same God who brought Abram out of Ur would surely bring him into the inheritance. Later, God would introduce the Ten Commandments with similar words: I am the LORD thy God, which have brought thee out of the land of Egypt. God's past faithfulness is the foundation for present trust. Today, take time to remember where God has brought you from. Recall specific ways He has led, provided, and protected. Let those memories strengthen your confidence. The God who brought you out will also bring you in.

== 15:8
T: Asking for assurance
R: 'And he said, Lord GOD, whereby shall I know that I shall inherit it?' Abram had just believed God and been counted righteous. Yet now he asked for assurance. This is not a contradiction. Faith and the desire for assurance can coexist. Abram believed, but he wanted to know more deeply. God did not rebuke him. Instead, God responded with a solemn covenant ceremony. God understands that we are human and that our faith sometimes needs strengthening. Gideon asked for signs. Mary asked, How shall this be? Thomas wanted to see the wounds. God met each of them with grace. There is a difference between asking in unbelief and asking in faith. Abram was not doubting whether God could do it; he was seeking confirmation. Today, if you need assurance, ask God for it. He delights to strengthen the faith of His children. Read His promises, remember His faithfulness, and ask Him to confirm His word in your heart.
S: Even after believing, Abram asked God how he could know he would inherit the land. God graciously answered with a covenant ceremony. Ask God for assurance today; He delights to strengthen your faith.

== 15:9
T: Preparing for covenant
R: 'And he said unto him, Take me an heifer of three years old, and a she goat of three years old, and a ram of three years old, and a turtledove, and a young pigeon.' God's answer to Abram's request was unusual. He told him to gather specific animals. This was the preparation for a covenant ceremony common in the ancient world. The animals listed are the very ones later used in Israel's sacrificial system. God was teaching Abram, and through him all of Israel, that covenant relationship with God involves sacrifice. Every sacrifice in the Old Testament pointed forward to the one great sacrifice of Jesus. Hebrews says that the blood of bulls and goats could not take away sins, but Christ, by His own blood, obtained eternal redemption for us. Abram gathered animals; God would one day provide His own Son. Today, reflect on the cost of your covenant relationship with God. It was purchased at the price of Christ's life. Let that move you to gratitude and worship.
S: God told Abram to gather specific animals for a covenant ceremony. These animals foreshadowed Israel's sacrifices and ultimately the sacrifice of Christ. Reflect today on the cost of your relationship with God.

== 15:10
T: Divided pieces
R: 'And he took unto him all these, and divided them in the midst, and laid each piece one against another: but the birds divided he not.' Abram cut the animals in half and arranged the pieces in two rows with a path between them. In the ancient world, this was how a covenant was ratified. The parties would walk between the pieces, essentially saying, May this happen to me if I break this covenant. It was a solemn, binding oath. Jeremiah refers to this practice when he speaks of those who cut the calf in twain, and passed between the parts thereof. Abram prepared everything as God commanded. He did not understand all that would happen, but he obeyed. Covenant with God is serious. It is not a casual agreement but a binding commitment. Today, think about the seriousness of your relationship with God. He has bound Himself to you with the most solemn commitment. Respond with wholehearted devotion to Him.
S: Abram divided the animals and laid the pieces opposite each other, preparing for a covenant. Walking between the pieces was a solemn oath in the ancient world. Respond to God's serious commitment with wholehearted devotion.

== 15:11
T: Driving away the birds
R: 'And when the fowls came down upon the carcases, Abram drove them away.' While Abram waited for God to act, birds of prey swooped down on the sacrifice. Abram drove them away. This small detail is full of meaning. Waiting on God is not passive. While we wait, things try to steal what we have offered to Him. Distractions, doubts, and temptations swoop in like birds of prey. Jesus told a parable about seed sown by the wayside, where the fowls came and devoured it, explaining that the wicked one comes and catches away what was sown in the heart. Abram guarded his offering. We too must guard our hearts, our devotion, and our faith. Proverbs says, Keep thy heart with all diligence; for out of it are the issues of life. Today, identify the birds of prey in your life, whatever threatens to steal your time with God or your trust in His promises. Drive them away. Guard what you have offered to Him.

== 15:12
T: Darkness before revelation
R: 'And when the sun was going down, a deep sleep fell upon Abram; and, lo, an horror of great darkness fell upon him.' As evening came, a deep sleep fell on Abram, and with it, a great and terrifying darkness. This was not ordinary sleep. It was a supernatural experience, like the deep sleep God caused to fall on Adam. In this darkness, God would reveal the future. Sometimes God speaks most clearly in our darkest moments. The horror Abram felt may reflect the weight of what God was about to reveal: years of suffering for his descendants. Revelation is not always comfortable. Yet God was present in the darkness. Psalm 139 says, the darkness hideth not from thee; but the night shineth as the day. If you are in a season of darkness, know that God is there. He may be preparing to show you something important. Today, do not run from the darkness. Wait in it with God, and listen.

== 15:13
T: Suffering foretold
R: 'And he said unto Abram, Know of a surety that thy seed shall be a stranger in a land that is not theirs, and shall serve them; and they shall afflict them four hundred years.' God revealed a hard truth. Abram's descendants would be strangers in a foreign land, enslaved and afflicted for four hundred years. This was fulfilled in Israel's bondage in Egypt. God did not hide the suffering ahead. He told Abram the truth. God's promises do not exclude hardship. The path to fulfillment often goes through suffering. Jesus told His disciples, In the world ye shall have tribulation: but be of good cheer; I have overcome the world. Knowing that suffering is part of God's plan does not make it easy, but it does give it meaning. Israel's years in Egypt were not outside of God's knowledge or control. Today, if you are walking through hardship, remember that God is not surprised by it. He saw it coming, and He has a purpose that extends beyond it.

== 15:14
T: Judgment and deliverance
R: 'And also that nation, whom they shall serve, will I judge: and afterward shall they come out with great substance.' God did not leave Abram with only bad news. The oppressor nation would be judged, and Abram's descendants would come out with great wealth. This was precisely fulfilled in the Exodus. God sent plagues on Egypt, and Israel left with silver, gold, and clothing from the Egyptians. Suffering would not be the final word. Deliverance was coming. God's justice and God's faithfulness go together. He sees oppression and will judge it. He sees His people's suffering and will deliver them. This promise gives hope to all who suffer injustice. Psalm 9 says, The LORD also will be a refuge for the oppressed, a refuge in times of trouble. Today, if you are suffering, hold on to the afterward. God's story for His people always includes an afterward of deliverance. Your trial is not the end. He will bring you out.

== 15:15
T: Going in peace
R: 'And thou shalt go to thy fathers in peace; thou shalt be buried in a good old age.' God gave Abram a personal promise. He would not see the suffering of his descendants in Egypt. He would die in peace at a good old age. The phrase go to thy fathers hints at something beyond death, a gathering with those who had gone before. Abram's life would end well. To die in peace is a great gift. Many people fear death, but for those who trust God, death is not something to dread. It is going home. Simeon, after seeing the infant Jesus, prayed, Lord, now lettest thou thy servant depart in peace, according to thy word. Paul wrote that to die is gain, because it means being with Christ. Today, if you fear death, bring that fear to God. In Christ, death is not the end but a peaceful passage into His presence. Live today in such a way that you can one day go in peace.

== 15:16
T: God's patient justice
R: 'But in the fourth generation they shall come hither again: for the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet full.' Why would Abram's descendants have to wait four hundred years? Part of the reason was the Amorites. Their iniquity was not yet full. God would not judge them before their sin had reached its full measure. This reveals both God's patience and His justice. He gave the Amorites centuries to turn from their wickedness. Only when their sin had fully ripened would He bring judgment through Israel. God does not judge hastily. He is patient even with wicked nations. Peter says that God is longsuffering to us-ward, not willing that any should perish, but that all should come to repentance. At the same time, God's patience has limits. Eventually, sin reaches its fullness, and judgment comes. Today, thank God for His patience with you. And do not presume on it. Respond to His kindness with repentance while there is time.

== 15:17
T: God walks the path alone
R: 'And it came to pass, that, when the sun went down, and it was dark, behold a smoking furnace, and a burning lamp that passed between those pieces.' In the darkness, a smoking furnace and a burning lamp passed between the divided animals. These represented the presence of God. Remarkably, only God passed between the pieces. Abram did not. In ancient covenants, both parties would walk through, binding themselves to the terms. But here, God alone walked the path. He took the full responsibility for keeping the covenant on Himself. This is grace in its purest form. God bound Himself to His promises, regardless of Abram's performance. This points to the gospel. In Christ, God took upon Himself the curse of the broken covenant. He fulfilled all its demands for us. We do not hold the covenant together; He does. Today, rest in a covenant that depends entirely on God's faithfulness. He walked the path alone so that you could be secure.

== 15:18
T: The covenant is made
R: 'In the same day the LORD made a covenant with Abram, saying, Unto thy seed have I given this land, from the river of Egypt unto the great river, the river Euphrates.' The covenant was sealed. Notice the tense: have I given. God spoke of the gift as already done, even though Abram's descendants would not possess the land for centuries. God's promises are so certain that He speaks of them as completed. Paul says God calleth those things which be not as though they were. When God makes a promise, it is as good as done. The boundaries were vast, stretching from Egypt to the Euphrates. God's promises are generous. He does not give sparingly. In Christ, God's promises to us are equally certain. Paul writes, For all the promises of God in him are yea, and in him Amen. Today, speak of God's promises as certain, because they are. Live with the confidence that what God has said, He has already settled.

== 15:19
T: Specific promises
R: The land God promised included the territory of 'The Kenites, and the Kenizzites, and the Kadmonites.' God named specific peoples whose lands would be given to Abram's descendants. His promise was not vague or general. It was concrete and detailed. God knows exactly what He intends to give. Interestingly, the Kenites later had a friendly relationship with Israel. Moses' father-in-law was a Kenite, and some Kenites lived among the Israelites. Caleb, one of Israel's great heroes of faith, was a Kenizzite. Even peoples whose land was promised to Israel found a place within God's people. God's promises of judgment and blessing are always mixed with grace for those who turn to Him. His plans are specific, but His mercy is broad. Today, trust that God's promises to you are specific too. He knows your needs in detail. And remember that His grace reaches people from every background, even those who once stood outside His covenant.

== 15:20
T: Facing the giants
R: The list continues: 'And the Hittites, and the Perizzites, and the Rephaims.' The Rephaim were the giants. When Israel's spies later explored the land, they saw these giants and felt like grasshoppers. Ten of the twelve spies said the land could not be taken. Only Joshua and Caleb trusted God's promise. Here, centuries earlier, God had already named the giants as part of what He would give. God's promise accounted for the giants. He knew they were there, and He still said, I have given this land. The obstacles that terrify us do not surprise God. He factors them into His promises. Numbers records Caleb saying, Let us go up at once, and possess it; for we are well able to overcome it. Caleb remembered that the giants were already included in the promise. Today, whatever giants stand between you and God's promise, remember that God saw them first. They are not too big for Him.

== 15:21
T: Every enemy accounted for
R: The list ends: 'And the Amorites, and the Canaanites, and the Girgashites, and the Jebusites.' Ten nations in all are named. This was a land full of powerful peoples with fortified cities. From a human perspective, it was impossible that a childless old man's descendants would conquer them all. But God's promise covered every one. Not a single enemy was left out of His plan. The last name on the list, the Jebusites, held Jerusalem. That city would become the center of God's work in the world, the place where the temple would stand and where Jesus would die and rise again. What was once an enemy stronghold became the city of God. God is able to take what stands against His purposes and turn it into a place of blessing. Today, rejoice that no enemy is outside God's control. Every opposition you face has been accounted for. He is greater than all of them.
