# March 2028: Trusting God in Every Storm

== Mar 01
R: We like to understand things. We want to know why something happened, how it will turn out, and what we should do next. Understanding gives us a sense of control. But Proverbs points us to something deeper: 'Trust in the LORD with all thine heart; and lean not unto thine own understanding.' This does not mean we stop thinking. God gave us minds, and He expects us to use them. It means we do not lean our full weight on our own reasoning. Our understanding is limited. We see only a small part of the picture. God sees the whole. Leaning on our own understanding is like leaning on a cane that cannot hold us. Sooner or later, it gives way. Trusting God with all our heart means resting on Him even when we do not understand. This month we will think about trusting God in the storms of life. Begin today by handing Him the questions you cannot answer. Lean on Him instead.

== Mar 02
R: Psalm 46 was written for a time of great upheaval. The psalmist imagines mountains crumbling into the sea, waters roaring, and nations in turmoil. Into that chaos, he declares, 'God is our refuge and strength, a very present help in trouble.' A refuge is a place to run to for safety. Strength is the power to keep going. And a very present help means God is not far away when trouble comes. He is right here, readily available. Some help arrives too late. Some help is hard to reach. But God's help is near, immediate, and reliable. The psalmist does not say trouble will never come. He assumes it will. The earth may shake. But God remains a safe place and a source of strength. Whatever is shaking in your life right now, you can run to Him. He is not distant. He is present, right now, in the middle of it. Let Him be your refuge and your strength today.
S: Psalm 46 declares that God is our refuge, our strength, and a very present help in trouble. His help is near and reliable, even when everything else is shaking. Run to Him today, whatever you are facing.

== Mar 03
R: Later in the same psalm, after describing roaring waters and raging nations, God speaks directly: 'Be still, and know that I am God.' The words be still can also mean let go, stop striving, or cease fighting. It is an invitation to stop our frantic efforts and remember who is really in control. When storms come, our instinct is to do something, anything. We worry, plan, scramble, and try to fix things. Sometimes action is needed. But often what we need first is stillness. A quiet heart that remembers God is God, and we are not. He goes on to say, 'I will be exalted among the heathen, I will be exalted in the earth.' God's purposes will prevail. He will be honoured. Nothing that troubles you is beyond His reach. Today, take a few minutes to be still. Put down your phone, quiet your thoughts, and simply rest in the truth that He is God. Let that truth calm your heart.

== Mar 04
R: God did not promise His people a life without waters and fires. He promised something better: His presence in them. 'When thou passest through the waters, I will be with thee; and through the rivers, they shall not overflow thee: when thou walkest through the fire, thou shalt not be burned.' Notice the word when, not if. Hard seasons are part of life. Floods rise and fires burn. Israel remembered walking through the Red Sea and crossing the Jordan River. Later, three young men would walk through a fiery furnace untouched. God had been with them every time. The promise is not that we will avoid difficulty, but that difficulty will not destroy us. The waters will not overwhelm us. The flames will not consume us. Why? Because He is with us. Whatever waters you are passing through today, you are not alone. The One who parted seas and walked in the fire walks with you.
S: God promised His people that He would be with them through the waters and the fire. He does not promise to remove every difficulty, but He promises they will not destroy us. Trust His presence with you in whatever you are passing through.

R: The disciples were experienced fishermen. They knew the Sea of Galilee and its sudden storms. But this one was different. Waves crashed into the boat, and it began filling with water. Terrified, they woke Jesus, who was asleep on a cushion. He rose, rebuked the wind, and the sea became completely calm. Then He turned to them: 'Why are ye so fearful? how is it that ye have no faith?' The question was not harsh but searching. They had seen Him heal the sick and cast out demons. Yet in the storm, fear took over. Jesus was in the boat with them the whole time. That is the point. Fear forgets who is present. Faith remembers. The storm was real, but so was the One sleeping in the stern. Whatever storm you are facing, Jesus is not absent. Let His question search your heart today. Then answer it with faith.

R: David wrote Psalm 56 when he was captured by the Philistines in Gath. He was alone, in enemy territory, and genuinely afraid. Out of that moment came this honest prayer: 'What time I am afraid, I will trust in thee.' David did not pretend to be fearless. He admitted that fear came. But he made a decision about what to do with it. Fear and trust are not always opposites that cannot exist together. Sometimes trust is the choice we make while we are still afraid. Courage is not the absence of fear. It is the decision to rely on God in the middle of it. This short verse is easy to memorize, and it is worth keeping close. When anxiety rises, when bad news comes, when you lie awake at night, you can repeat it. What time I am afraid, I will trust in thee. R: Psalm 23 begins in green pastures and beside still waters. But then the path turns dark: 'Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil: for thou art with me.' The Shepherd does not lead His sheep around every valley. Sometimes the path goes straight through. Valleys of grief, illness, loss, and uncertainty are part of life's journey. But notice two important words: walk through. The valley is not the destination. It is a passage. And we do not walk it alone. This is also the point in the psalm where David stops talking about the Shepherd and starts talking to Him. In the dark valley, the relationship becomes personal: thou art with me. The rod protects and the staff guides. If you are in a valley right now, take comfort. You are walking through it, not staying in it. And your Shepherd is right beside you every step.

R: Have you ever stood in the shade on a scorching day? The relief is immediate. Psalm 91 uses that picture for life with God: 'He that dwelleth in the secret place of the most High shall abide under the shadow of the Almighty.' Dwelling and abiding are words of permanence. This is not about visiting God occasionally. It is about living close to Him, making His presence your home. The secret place suggests intimacy, a hidden refuge known to those who walk with Him. And the shadow of the Almighty speaks of protection. To be in someone's shadow, you must be very close to them. Psalm 91 goes on to describe dangers of every kind: snares, plagues, arrows, and terror by night. The promise is not that dangers vanish, but that those who stay close to God are sheltered. Today, do not just visit God. Dwell with Him. Stay close enough to remain in His shadow.

God does not merely tolerate those who trust Him. He knows them personally. He notices them, cares for them, and keeps track of them. R: The prophet Habakkuk ends his book with one of the most remarkable statements of faith in Scripture. He imagines the worst possible scenario for a farming nation: the fig tree without blossoms, no fruit on the vines, failed olive crops, empty fields, and no animals in the stalls. Total loss. And then he says, 'Yet I will rejoice in the LORD, I will joy in the God of my salvation.' Yet. That small word is enormous. Habakkuk's joy did not come from his circumstances. If it had, it would have vanished along with the crops. His joy came from God Himself, who does not change when the harvest fails. This kind of joy is not pretending everything is fine. It is choosing to rejoice in the One who remains when everything else is gone. What is failing in your life right now? Bring it honestly to God. Then, by faith, add Habakkuk's word: yet. Yet I will rejoice in the Lord.

R: Romans 8:28 is a verse that is quoted again and again, and also one of the most misunderstood: 'And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose.' It does not say all things are good. Some things that happen to us are painful and wrong. It says all things work together for good. Like ingredients in a recipe, some of which taste bitter alone, God weaves even hard things into His good purpose. Notice also who this promise is for: those who love God and are called according to His purpose. And notice what good means. The next verse explains that God's purpose is to make us like His Son.
