# April 2028: Forgiveness & Reconciliation

== Apr 01
R: If you travel north, eventually you reach the North Pole and begin heading south. North and south meet. But east and west never do. You can travel east forever and never start going west. David may not have known all the geography, but he chose his picture well: 'As far as the east is from the west, so far hath he removed our transgressions from us.' God does not move our sins a short distance away, where we might stumble over them again. He removes them completely, to a distance that cannot be measured. Many people believe God forgives them, yet still carry the weight of old failures. They replay past mistakes and feel condemned. But if God has removed your sin that far, you do not need to go looking for it. This month we will think about forgiveness, both receiving it and giving it. Begin by receiving it fully. Let what God has removed stay removed.
S: David wrote that God removes our sins as far as the east is from the west, a distance that can never be measured. Forgiven sins do not need to be carried again. Receive God's forgiveness fully today and let removed sins stay removed.
P: East and west never meet, and neither will you and your forgiven sins. Psalm 103:12 says, 'As far as the east is from the west, so far hath he removed our transgressions from us.' Let go of what God has removed.

== Apr 02
R: David knew the weight of sin. Psalm 32 describes what it felt like before he confessed: his bones wasted away, he groaned all day long, and God's hand was heavy on him. Hidden sin had drained the life out of him. So when he finally experienced forgiveness, he burst into this declaration: 'Blessed is he whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered.' Blessed means deeply happy, truly well off. Real happiness is not found in never having failed. It is found in being forgiven. Covered is a beautiful word. It pictures God placing a covering over our sin so that it is no longer exposed before Him. We do not cover it ourselves. That only leads to hiding and heaviness. God covers it, and that brings freedom. If you are carrying something you have never brought to God, the weight does not have to stay. The joy of being forgiven is waiting on the other side of honesty.
S: David, who knew the weight of hidden sin, declared that the one whose sin is forgiven and covered is truly blessed. Real happiness comes not from never failing but from being forgiven. Bring anything hidden to God today and find the joy of forgiveness.
P: Real happiness is found in being forgiven. Psalm 32:1 says, 'Blessed is he whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered.' Is there anything you need to bring to God today?

== Apr 03
R: Later in Psalm 32, David tells exactly how his freedom came: 'I acknowledged my sin unto thee, and mine iniquity have I not hid. I said, I will confess my transgressions unto the LORD; and thou forgavest the iniquity of my sin.' Notice the simplicity. David stopped hiding. He acknowledged what he had done. He did not make excuses, shift blame, or minimize. He confessed. And God forgave. Confession is not about informing God of something He does not know. He already knows. It is about agreeing with Him, being honest about our sin rather than defending it. Confession clears the air between us and God. It ends the exhausting work of hiding. Many of us delay confession because we fear what will happen. But David discovered that on the other side of confession was not condemnation but forgiveness. Is there something you have been hiding from God? You do not need to keep carrying it. Say it plainly to Him today. He is ready to forgive.
S: David found freedom when he stopped hiding his sin and confessed it to the Lord. Confession is agreeing honestly with God, and it ends the exhausting work of hiding. Bring anything you have been hiding to Him today.
P: Hiding is exhausting. Confession brings freedom. Psalm 32:5 says, 'I will confess my transgressions unto the LORD; and thou forgavest the iniquity of my sin.' He is ready to forgive you today.

== Apr 04
R: God's people had wandered far from Him. Isaiah's opening chapter describes their rebellion in painful detail. Yet in the middle of the charges, God extends an invitation: 'Come now, and let us reason together, saith the LORD: though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow.' Scarlet dye was one of the most permanent colours in the ancient world. Once a cloth was dyed scarlet, it was almost impossible to make it white again. That is a picture of sin's stain. We cannot scrub it out ourselves. But God can. He promises to make what is deeply stained as white as fresh snow and clean wool. Notice that the invitation comes first: come now. God does not wait for us to clean ourselves before we approach Him. He invites us to come as we are, stains and all, and let Him do the cleansing. Whatever stain you feel today, accept His invitation. Come now.
S: Through Isaiah, God invited His wayward people to come and reason with Him, promising to make scarlet sins white as snow. We cannot remove sin's stain, but God can. Accept His invitation to come as you are today.
P: The deepest stain can become white as snow. Isaiah 1:18 says, 'though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow.' Come to Him as you are today. He will do the cleansing.

== Apr 05
R: The prophet Micah's name means Who is like the Lord? He ends his book by answering his own name with wonder: 'Who is a God like unto thee, that pardoneth iniquity, and passeth by the transgression of the remnant of his heritage?' There is no other god like this. The idols of the nations around Israel were feared for their anger and demanded endless appeasement. But the true God is different. He pardons. He passes over transgression. And the reason is stunning: 'he retaineth not his anger for ever, because he delighteth in mercy.' God does not forgive reluctantly, as though it costs Him to be kind. He delights in mercy. It brings Him joy. This changes how we approach Him. We do not have to beg a reluctant God. We come to a Father who is glad to forgive. If you have been afraid to come to God because of your failures, remember this verse. He delights in mercy. Come and let Him enjoy forgiving you.
S: Micah marvels that no god is like the true God, who pardons sin and does not hold on to anger. God forgives not reluctantly but because He delights in mercy. Come to Him today, knowing He is glad to forgive.
P: God is not a reluctant forgiver. Micah 7:18 says He 'retaineth not his anger for ever, because he delighteth in mercy.' Come to Him today. Mercy brings Him joy.

== Apr 06
R: Micah continues his song of wonder with three vivid promises: 'He will turn again, he will have compassion upon us; he will subdue our iniquities; and thou wilt cast all their sins into the depths of the sea.' First, God turns again toward us with compassion. He does not keep His face turned away. Second, He subdues our iniquities. He does not only forgive sin; He also defeats its power over us. And third, He casts our sins into the depths of the sea. In the ancient world, the deep sea was the most unreachable place imaginable. Whatever sank there was gone for good. That is where God sends our sins. Not into a drawer where He can pull them out later, but into depths that cannot be reached. Some people like to say that God then puts up a sign: no fishing allowed. Do not go diving for what God has buried. If He has cast it into the sea, leave it there.
S: Micah promises that God will turn to us with compassion, subdue our sins, and cast them into the depths of the sea. Forgiven sins are gone for good, beyond reach. Do not go looking for what God has buried; leave it there today.

== Apr 07
R: When Jesus taught His disciples to pray, He included a line that is easy to say but hard to live: 'And forgive us our debts, as we forgive our debtors.' Sin is pictured as a debt, something we owe and cannot repay. We ask God to cancel what we owe Him. But in the same breath, we commit to canceling what others owe us. The two are tied together. A heart that has truly received mercy becomes a merciful heart. It would be strange to ask God to forgive a mountain of debt while we refuse to forgive a few coins owed to us. Jesus later told a parable about exactly that. Praying this line honestly can be uncomfortable. It brings to mind the people who have hurt us. But that discomfort can lead to freedom. Today, as you ask God for forgiveness, ask Him also for the grace to forgive. Name the person in your heart. Release the debt as He has released yours.
S: In teaching His disciples to pray, Jesus tied our forgiveness from God to our forgiveness of others. A heart that receives mercy becomes merciful. Ask God today for grace to release a debt someone owes you.
P: Forgiven people forgive. In Matthew 6:12 Jesus taught us to pray, 'forgive us our debts, as we forgive our debtors.' Who do you need to release today?

== Apr 08
R: Right after teaching His disciples to pray, Jesus returned to one line and explained it further: 'For if ye forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you.' Of all the lines in the prayer, this is the one He chose to emphasize. Forgiveness matters deeply to God. This does not mean we earn God's forgiveness by forgiving others. Salvation is a gift of grace. But a heart that refuses to forgive shows that it has not truly grasped how much it has been forgiven. Unforgiveness also harms us. It keeps old wounds open and ties us to the people who hurt us. Bitterness grows in its soil. Forgiving does not mean pretending the wrong was small, or that trust is immediately restored. It means releasing the debt to God and refusing to hold it over the other person. It is usually a journey rather than one decision. Today, take one step on that path. Ask God to help you forgive.
S: Jesus emphasized that those who forgive others will be forgiven by their heavenly Father. Unforgiveness shows we have not grasped our own forgiveness, and it harms us too. Take one step toward forgiving someone today.
P: Forgiveness matters deeply to God. In Matthew 6:14 Jesus said, 'if ye forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you.' Take one step toward forgiving someone today.

== Apr 09
R: Peter thought he was being generous. Jewish teachers of the time suggested forgiving someone three times. So Peter more than doubled it: 'Lord, how oft shall my brother sin against me, and I forgive him? till seven times?' Seven was a number of completeness. Surely forgiving someone seven times for the same kind of offence was more than enough. Peter's question is very human. We want to know where the limit is. At what point can we stop forgiving and start protecting ourselves? When does patience run out? It is an honest question, especially when someone keeps hurting us. Notice that Peter is asking about a brother, someone close to him. Repeated hurts often come from the people nearest to us: family members, friends, coworkers. Those wounds can feel the deepest. If you have been counting someone's offences, you are not alone. Peter did too. Bring your count to Jesus today and listen for His answer. We will hear it tomorrow.
S: Peter asked Jesus how many times he should forgive a brother, suggesting seven times as generous. Like Peter, we often want to know where forgiveness can stop. Bring your own count of offences to Jesus today.
P: Have you been keeping count? In Matthew 18:21 Peter asked, 'Lord, how oft shall my brother sin against me, and I forgive him? till seven times?' Bring your question to Jesus today.

== Apr 10
R: Jesus answered Peter's question with a number that stopped counting altogether: 'I say not unto thee, Until seven times: but, Until seventy times seven.' Four hundred and ninety. But Jesus was not giving Peter a new limit to track. He was saying, stop keeping score. Forgiveness is not a ledger with a final line. Then Jesus told a story about a servant who was forgiven a debt he could never repay, millions in today's terms. Yet that same servant refused to forgive another servant whose debt to him was tiny. The master was outraged. The point was clear. When we grasp how much God has forgiven us, our small debts against others look different. This does not mean we allow ourselves to be endlessly abused. Forgiveness and boundaries can go together. But it does mean we do not run out of grace. Today, put away the scorecard. Let God's limitless forgiveness of you shape your forgiveness of others.
S: Jesus answered Peter by saying forgiveness should reach seventy times seven, which means we should stop counting. When we grasp how much God has forgiven us, our grievances look different. Put away your scorecard today.

== Apr 11
R: Jesus gave a series of short commands about how we treat others: 'Judge not, and ye shall not be judged: condemn not, and ye shall not be condemned: forgive, and ye shall be forgiven.' It is easy to become a harsh judge of others. We see their faults clearly while overlooking our own. We assume the worst about their motives. We decide what they deserve. Jesus is not saying we should never discern right from wrong. Elsewhere He tells us to judge rightly. He is warning against a critical, condemning spirit that sets itself up above others. That kind of spirit tends to come back on us. People who are quick to condemn often find themselves condemned. But people who are generous with mercy tend to receive it. The verse goes on to say, give, and it shall be given unto you. The measure we use for others is the measure we receive. Today, choose mercy over judgment. Look for the good in others and be quick to forgive.
S: Jesus told His followers not to judge or condemn but to forgive, because the measure we use for others comes back to us. A critical spirit tends to return on us, while mercy multiplies. Choose mercy over judgment today.

He would ask to be treated as a hired servant. But something happened before he could even begin: 'when he was yet a great way off, his father saw him, and had compassion, and ran, and fell on his neck, and kissed him.' The father saw him a great way off. He must have been watching the road. He had compassion. And he ran. In that culture, an older man running was considered undignified. But this father did not care about his dignity. He cared about his son. This is Jesus' picture of God. Not a stern judge waiting for us to grovel, but a Father watching the road, ready to run. If you have wandered, the way home is open. Take the first step, and you may find Him already running toward you.

And he explained why: 'For this my son was dead, and is alive again; he was lost, and is found.' To the father, it was as if his son had come back to life. The son had been lost, and now he was found. That was worth celebrating. Not everyone was happy. The older brother was angry and refused to join the party. He felt his faithfulness had gone unnoticed while his brother's rebellion was rewarded. His reaction warns us. It is possible to be close to the Father's house and still miss the Father's heart. God rejoices when the lost come home. Do we rejoice too, or do we resent the grace shown to others? Today, celebrate the return of anyone who has come back to God. And if you are the one who was lost, know that heaven is celebrating you.

But God raised him up to become second in command over the whole land, and through his wisdom, many lives were saved during a terrible famine. After their father died, the brothers feared Joseph would finally take revenge. Instead, he said, 'ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save much people alive.' Joseph did not deny the evil. He named it plainly. But he saw a bigger story. God had taken their evil intentions and woven them into a plan to save many lives. This perspective is what allowed Joseph to forgive. He did not need revenge because he trusted God's purpose. When people have wronged you, it can be hard to see any good. But God is still at work. He can bring purpose out of pain. Now they stood before the powerful governor of Egypt, terrified, as he told them he was the brother they had sold. They expected punishment. It seeks their healing and restoration. It reaches across the hurt with compassion. Joseph wept as he embraced them. Forgiveness like this is a gift of God's grace. We cannot manufacture it on our own. But God can grow it in us. Ask Him today for a heart like Joseph's.

Notice how they build on one another. Bitterness is a settled resentment, an unhealed wound that sours inside. It leads to wrath, an outburst of temper, and anger, a simmering hostility. These spill out as clamour, loud quarrelling, and evil speaking, words that tear others down. And underneath it all is malice, a desire for harm. These things often start small. But it starts with a decision and a prayer. The very next verse shows the alternative: kindness, tender hearts, and forgiveness. R: Paul gives a simple standard for forgiveness: 'even as Christ forgave you, so also do ye.' How did Christ forgive us? Completely. He did not forgive only the small sins and hold the big ones against us. Freely. We did not earn it. Before we asked. He made the way while we were still far from Him. At great cost. Forgiveness was not cheap for Him. That is the standard. It is high, and none of us meet it perfectly. But it also gives us the power to forgive. We cannot give what we have not received. The more deeply we understand Christ's forgiveness of us, the more we are able to extend it to others. When forgiving someone feels impossible, do not start by focusing on what they did. Start by remembering what Christ has done for you. Let His forgiveness flow into you until it begins to flow through you. Forgive as you have been forgiven.

== Apr 21
R: Paul is realistic in this verse: 'If it be possible, as much as lieth in you, live peaceably with all men.' He knows that peace is not always possible. Some people refuse it. Some situations cannot be resolved. Paul does not put the entire burden of every relationship on us. But he does put our part on us. As much as lieth in you. As far as it depends on you. == Apr 24
R: Proverbs often teaches by contrast. Here it sets two forces side by side: 'Hatred stirreth up strifes: but love covereth all sins.' Hatred stirs. Like someone stirring a pot, it keeps conflict bubbling. It brings up old offences, spreads gossip, and looks for reasons to fight. Hatred is never satisfied with peace. Love, on the other hand, covers. It does not expose every fault or broadcast every failure. It protects the reputation of others. It chooses discretion and grace. It is quick to cover with grace rather than quick to expose with criticism. Peter quoted a similar line when he wrote that love shall cover the multitude of sins. Think about how you speak of others when they are not present. Do your words stir up strife, or do they cover with love? Choose love today.

R: Paul explains the heart of reconciliation in one sentence: 'God was in Christ, reconciling the world unto himself, not imputing their trespasses unto them.'
